# Module 3 — Radioactivity

Original items. Half-lives used here are the standard therapy values: Ir-192 73.8 days, Co-60 5.26 years, Cs-137 30 years, I-125 59.4 days, Pd-103 17 days, Au-198 2.7 days, I-131 8 days, Y-90 64 hours, Sr-90 28.8 years, Ra-223 11.4 days, Cs-131 9.7 days, Tc-99m 6 hours, F-18 110 minutes, P-32 14.3 days. Arithmetic uses 0.693 and a mean-life factor of 1.44.

## Radioactivity

### Q1
Stem: Radioactivity is best described as:
A. Removal of an orbital electron by a photon
B. Spontaneous transformation of an unstable nucleus
C. Bremsstrahlung produced in a tungsten target
D. The Hounsfield number of a high-Z insert
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: Radioactivity is a nuclear change (alpha, beta, electron capture, gamma, and related modes). Knocking out an orbital electron is ionization. Bremsstrahlung is an x-ray process. The Hounsfield number is a CT attenuation scale.

### Q2
Stem: One becquerel is:
A. One disintegration per second
B. One disintegration per minute
C. 3.7 × 10^10 disintegrations per second
D. One gray per hour
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: The becquerel is 1 s^−1. The curie is 3.7 × 10^10 Bq. A gray per hour is a dose rate, not an activity.

### Q3
Stem: Two sealed sources have the same activity. Which statement must be true?
A. They deliver the same absorbed dose at 1 cm in tissue.
B. They have the same number of nuclear transformations per second.
C. They have the same half-life.
D. They are the same nuclide.
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Activity is transformations per unit time. Dose also depends on the energy emitted, the radiation type, and the geometry. Equal activity does not force equal half-life or the same nuclide.

### Q4
Stem: The number of nuclei left after time t follows N = N0 e^(−λt). The shape of that curve is:
A. Linear down to zero at one half-life
B. Exponential
C. A Bragg peak
D. Flat until one mean life, then a step
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: The decay law is exponential. It is not a straight line that hits zero at T½, not a Bragg peak, and not a step at the mean life. At one half-life, half of the nuclei remain.

### Q5
Stem: A source is described as “10 mCi.” Which quantity was stated?
A. Absorbed dose
B. Dose equivalent
C. Activity
D. Exposure
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: The curie and the becquerel are activity units. Absorbed dose is the gray, dose equivalent is the sievert, and exposure is the roentgen or coulomb per kilogram.

## Decay Constant

### Q1
Stem: A nuclide has a half-life of 10.0 days. What is λ?
A. 0.0693 day^−1
B. 6.93 day^−1
C. 14.4 days
D. 0.693 day
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: λ = 0.693 / T½ = 0.693 / 10.0 = 0.0693 day^−1. 6.93 day^−1 moves the decimal. 14.4 days is 1.44 × 10, the mean life, and it is not a decay constant. 0.693 day still carries the half-life factor but neither divides nor has the right units.

### Q2
Stem: The units of the decay constant are:
A. Time
B. 1 / time
C. Activity × time
D. Dimensionless
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Because λ = 0.693 / T½, λ is an inverse time. In the exponent λt, the product must be dimensionless, so λ cannot itself be a time or a pure number.

### Q3
Stem: Source A and source B contain the same number of radioactive atoms. Source A has the larger decay constant. Source A also has:
A. The longer half-life and the lower activity
B. The shorter half-life and the higher activity
C. The same activity, because N is the same
D. The longer mean life
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: T½ = 0.693 / λ, so a larger λ is a shorter half-life and a shorter mean life (τ = 1/λ). Activity A = λN, so the same N and a larger λ give a higher activity.

### Q4
Stem: Which relation defines activity in terms of the decay constant?
A. Activity = λ / N
B. Activity = λ × N
C. Activity = N / λ^2
D. Activity = λ × T½
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: A = λN. Dividing by N, squaring λ, or multiplying λ by the half-life does not give transformations per second. λ × T½ is 0.693, a pure number.

### Q5
Stem: Doubling the decay constant, with N fixed, does what to the activity and the half-life?
A. Activity doubles and half-life doubles
B. Activity doubles and half-life is cut in half
C. Activity is unchanged and half-life doubles
D. Activity is cut in half and half-life doubles
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: A = λN, so activity scales directly with λ. T½ = 0.693 / λ, so half-life scales inversely with λ.

## Activity

### Q1
Stem: A sample has λ = 0.01 s^−1 and N = 1.0 × 10^6 radioactive nuclei. What is the activity?
A. 1.0 × 10^4 Bq
B. 1.0 × 10^6 Bq
C. 0.01 Bq
D. 1.0 × 10^8 Bq
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: A = λN = 0.01 × 1.0 × 10^6 = 1.0 × 10^4 Bq. 1.0 × 10^6 Bq drops λ. 0.01 Bq drops N. 1.0 × 10^8 Bq divides by λ instead of multiplying (10^6 / 0.01).

### Q2
Stem: 1 Ci equals:
A. 3.7 × 10^7 Bq
B. 3.7 × 10^10 Bq
C. 3.7 × 10^4 Bq
D. 37 Bq
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: 1 Ci = 3.7 × 10^10 Bq. 3.7 × 10^7 Bq is 1 mCi. 3.7 × 10^4 Bq is 1 μCi. 37 Bq is not a curie prefix.

### Q3
Stem: Specific activity is:
A. Activity per unit mass of the sample
B. Activity multiplied by the mean life
C. Absorbed dose per decay
D. The ratio of daughter activity to parent activity
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: Specific activity is activity per unit mass, highest for a carrier-free source. Activity × mean life estimates the total number of decays. Dose per decay is an energy-deposition idea. The daughter-to-parent activity ratio is an equilibrium statement.

### Q4
Stem: A short-lived nuclide and a long-lived nuclide are both carrier-free. For the same number of atoms, the short-lived nuclide has:
A. Lower specific activity
B. Higher specific activity
C. The same specific activity
D. No activity until one half-life has passed
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Specific activity scales with λ. The shorter half-life has the larger λ, so each gram contains more transformations per second.

### Q5
Stem: From time zero to infinity, a source that is not replenished undergoes how many decays?
A. A0 × λ
B. A0 / λ
C. A0 × T½
D. λ / A0
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Total disintegrations equal the initial number of nuclei, N0 = A0 / λ. That is also A0 × mean life, because τ = 1/λ. A0 × λ and λ / A0 are not a count of nuclei. A0 × T½ uses the half-life in place of the mean life and is short by about 1.44.

## Half-Life and Mean Life

### Q1
Stem: After three half-lives, the fraction of activity remaining is:
A. 1/2
B. 1/3
C. 1/8
D. 1/16
Answer: C
Objective: I.7
Why: Each half-life multiplies activity by 1/2. Three half-lives leave (1/2)^3 = 1/8. 1/3 divides by the number of half-lives. 1/16 is four half-lives. 1/2 is one half-life.

### Q2
Stem: The mean life is longer than the half-life by which factor?
A. Mean life = 0.693 × half-life
B. Mean life = half-life
C. Mean life = 1.44 × half-life
D. Mean life = half-life / 1.44
Answer: C
Objective: I.7
Why: τ = 1/λ and λ = 0.693 / T½, so τ = T½ / 0.693 = 1.44 × T½. Option A is T½ × 0.693, which is much shorter than the half-life and is not a lifetime. Option D inverts the 1.44 factor.

### Q3
Stem: A source decays for a time equal to one mean life. The fraction of the original activity that remains is about:
A. 50%
B. 37%
C. 14%
D. 90%
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: A = A0 e^(−λt) and λτ = 1, so the fraction left is e^(−1) ≈ 0.37, or 37%. Fifty percent is what remains after one half-life, not one mean life.

### Q4
Stem: A radiopharmaceutical has a physical half-life of 12 hours and a biological half-life of 12 hours. The effective half-life is:
A. 24 hours
B. 12 hours
C. 6 hours
D. 144 hours
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: 1/Teff = 1/Tp + 1/Tb = 1/12 + 1/12 = 1/6, so Teff = 6 hours. Adding the half-lives gives 24 hours. Leaving either half-life unchanged gives 12 hours. Multiplying them gives 144 hours.

### Q5
Stem: Which half-life changes if the patient excretes the nuclide faster?
A. Physical half-life
B. Biological half-life, and therefore the effective half-life
C. The decay constant of the nucleus
D. Neither biological nor effective half-life
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Physical half-life and λ are nuclear properties and do not depend on chemistry or excretion. Faster clearance shortens the biological half-life and, through 1/Teff = 1/Tp + 1/Tb, shortens the effective half-life.

## Radioactive Equilibrium

### Q1
Stem: Secular equilibrium requires which half-life relationship?
A. The parent half-life is much longer than the daughter half-life.
B. The daughter half-life is much longer than the parent half-life.
C. The two half-lives are equal.
D. The parent decays only by alpha emission.
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Secular equilibrium is the limiting case Tp ≫ Td. The daughter activity then builds up to the parent activity and appears to decay with the parent half-life. If the daughter lives longer than the parent, equilibrium is not reached. Equal half-lives are not the secular condition, and the decay mode is not the definition.

### Q2
Stem: In transient equilibrium the parent half-life is longer than the daughter half-life, but not vastly longer. At equilibrium the daughter activity is:
A. Equal to the parent activity
B. Higher than the parent activity
C. Zero
D. Independent of the parent
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: For transient equilibrium, Ad/Ap = λd / (λd − λp), which is greater than 1. Equality of the two activities is the secular case. The daughter is fed by the parent, so it is not independent, and it is not zero while the parent is still present.

### Q3
Stem: Mo-99 (half-life about 66 hours) decays to Tc-99m (half-life 6 hours). The generator relationship is:
A. Secular equilibrium
B. Transient equilibrium
C. No equilibrium, because the daughter lives longer
D. Prompt alpha equilibrium
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: The parent is longer-lived, but 66 hours is not enormous compared with 6 hours, so the pair is the textbook transient-equilibrium generator. The daughter is not the longer-lived species. Molybdenum-99 decay to technetium is not an alpha process.

### Q4
Stem: Sr-90 (half-life 28.8 years) feeds Y-90 (half-life 64 hours). After a long buildup, the yttrium activity:
A. Approaches the strontium activity and then follows the 28.8-year half-life
B. Falls with a 64-hour half-life forever, with no parent influence
C. Becomes twice the strontium activity and stays there
D. Stops, because a beta daughter cannot stay in equilibrium
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: This is secular equilibrium: Tp ≫ Td, so Ad approaches Ap and the pair follows the parent half-life. The daughter does not ignore the parent once equilibrium is established. “Twice the parent” is not the secular limit.

### Q5
Stem: A parent half-life is 2 hours and the daughter half-life is 20 hours. What kind of equilibrium can be established?
A. Secular
B. Transient
C. Neither secular nor transient equilibrium
D. Equilibrium with the daughter activity fixed at half the parent activity
Answer: C
Objective: I.1
Why: Equilibrium of either kind needs a longer-lived parent so the daughter can be continuously fed. Here the daughter outlives the parent, so the parent disappears and the daughter then follows its own 20-hour half-life. There is no equilibrium activity ratio.

## Modes of Radioactive Decay

### Q1
Stem: An alpha decay does what to Z and A?
A. Z decreases by 2 and A decreases by 4
B. Z increases by 1 and A is unchanged
C. Z decreases by 1 and A is unchanged
D. Z and A are both unchanged
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: An alpha particle is a helium nucleus, two protons and two neutrons. Beta-minus raises Z by 1 at constant A. Beta-plus and electron capture lower Z by 1 at constant A. Gamma emission changes neither Z nor A.

### Q2
Stem: Beta-minus particles from a single nuclide are emitted with:
A. One discrete energy equal to the Q value
B. A continuous spectrum of energies up to a maximum
C. Exactly 511 keV
D. The K-shell binding energy only
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: The decay energy is shared with an antineutrino, so the beta energy is continuous up to Emax. A single discrete energy is the alpha or gamma pattern. 511 keV is annihilation radiation. The K-shell binding energy belongs to characteristic x-rays and Auger electrons.

### Q3
Stem: Positron decay is energetically forbidden unless the transition energy exceeds:
A. 511 keV
B. 1.022 MeV
C. 100 keV
D. The neutron rest energy
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: Creating the positron costs 511 keV, and a later annihilation returns two 511 keV photons, so the nuclear transition must supply at least 1.022 MeV. 511 keV is only one electron rest energy. The neutron rest energy is far larger and is not the threshold used for this branch.

### Q4
Stem: A proton-rich nucleus has only 0.6 MeV available, which is below the positron threshold. Which process can still raise the neutron number and lower Z by 1?
A. Electron capture
B. Alpha emission
C. Internal conversion
D. Beta-minus decay
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Electron capture uses an orbital electron to convert a proton to a neutron when positron emission is not allowed. It changes Z by −1 and leaves A unchanged, and it is followed by characteristic x-rays or Auger electrons. Alpha emission changes A. Internal conversion emits an orbital electron but does not change Z. Beta-minus increases Z.

### Q5
Stem: Gamma emission from a nucleus changes Z and A by:
A. ΔZ = −2, ΔA = −4
B. ΔZ = +1, ΔA = 0
C. ΔZ = 0, ΔA = 0
D. ΔZ = −1, ΔA = −1
Answer: C
Objective: I.1
Why: A gamma ray carries excitation energy out of the nucleus. The nuclide stays the same species. The other options describe alpha, beta-minus, and a nucleon-emission pattern.

### Q6
Stem: Internal conversion competes with gamma emission. The particle that leaves the atom in the conversion event itself is:
A. A photon of the full transition energy
B. An orbital electron
C. An alpha particle
D. A positron of 511 keV
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: Internal conversion transfers the nuclear transition energy to an orbital electron, which is ejected. A characteristic x-ray or Auger electron follows the vacancy. The competing branch, not the conversion branch, emits the gamma photon. Alpha particles and 511 keV positrons are different decays.

## Common Radioisotopes

### Q1
Stem: The HDR remote-afterloader source in routine use, and its half-life, are:
A. Ir-192, 73.8 days
B. Co-60, 73.8 days
C. Cs-137, 73.8 days
D. I-125, 73.8 days
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Ir-192 is the HDR nuclide, with a half-life of 73.8 days and a mean photon energy near 0.38 MeV. Co-60 (5.26 years) and Cs-137 (30 years) are teletherapy-era sources. I-125 (59.4 days) is a low-energy seed, not the HDR source.

### Q2
Stem: Co-60 emits two photons per decay, 1.17 MeV and 1.33 MeV. Its half-life is:
A. 5.26 years
B. 30 years
C. 73.8 days
D. 59.4 days
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Co-60’s half-life is 5.26 years. The average photon energy used in older teletherapy teaching is about 1.25 MeV. Thirty years is Cs-137, 73.8 days is Ir-192, and 59.4 days is I-125.

### Q3
Stem: Cs-137 is identified on a source inventory by which pair?
A. Half-life 30 years and a 662 keV gamma
B. Half-life 5.26 years and 1.25 MeV average gamma energy
C. Half-life 17 days and about 21 keV photons
D. Half-life 64 hours and a pure beta spectrum
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Cs-137 decays to Ba-137m, which emits a 662 keV gamma. The half-life is 30 years. Option B is Co-60, option C is Pd-103, and option D is Y-90.

### Q4
Stem: Permanent prostate seeds are ordered. Which listing is a real seed nuclide with its half-life?
A. Pd-103, 17 days
B. Pd-103, 59.4 days
C. Ir-192, 17 days
D. Tc-99m, 17 days
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Pd-103 is a permanent seed with a 17-day half-life and photon energies near 21 keV. I-125 is the 59.4-day seed. Ir-192 is 73.8 days and is used for temporary implants and HDR, not as a 17-day product. Tc-99m is a 6-hour imaging agent.

### Q5
Stem: Among I-125 (59.4 days), Pd-103 (17 days), and Cs-131 (9.7 days), the shortest half-life is:
A. I-125
B. Pd-103
C. Cs-131
D. They are the same
Answer: C
Objective: I.7
Why: Cs-131 is 9.7 days, Pd-103 is 17 days, and I-125 is 59.4 days. All three have been used as permanent seeds. The shortest half-life gives the highest initial dose rate for the same total decay, and the mean life, not the half-life, enters the total-dose estimate.

### Q6
Stem: Y-90 microspheres and Sr-90 eye applicators are beta sources. Which pairing is correct?
A. Y-90 half-life is 64 hours, and Sr-90 (28.8 years) is its long-lived parent.
B. Y-90 half-life is 28.8 years, and Sr-90 is the 64-hour daughter.
C. Both are gamma emitters with a 662 keV line.
D. Y-90 is an alpha emitter with an 11.4-day half-life.
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Y-90 is a pure beta emitter with a 64-hour half-life and a maximum energy of 2.28 MeV. Sr-90 (28.8 years) is the parent in a secular pair. Swapping the half-lives is the common memory error. Ra-223 is the 11.4-day alpha emitter. 662 keV is Cs-137.

### Q7
Stem: Which nuclide is an alpha emitter used for systemic bone therapy?
A. Ra-223, 11.4 days
B. I-131, 8 days
C. F-18, 110 minutes
D. P-32, 14.3 days
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Ra-223 is an alpha emitter with a half-life of 11.4 days. I-131 is a beta/gamma therapy nuclide (8 days). F-18 is a positron emitter for PET (110 minutes). P-32 is a pure beta emitter (14.3 days).

## Radioactivity Calculation Tutorial

### Q1
Stem: A source is 160 mCi. Its half-life is 8 days. What is the activity after 24 days?
A. 40 mCi
B. 20 mCi
C. 53 mCi
D. 10 mCi
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Number of half-lives = 24 / 8 = 3. Activity = 160 × (1/2)^3 = 160 / 8 = 20 mCi. 40 mCi stops at two half-lives. 53 mCi is 160/3, dividing by the count of half-lives instead of by 2^3. 10 mCi is four half-lives (160/16).

### Q2
Stem: The half-life is 20 days. Using a factor of 1.44, what is the mean life?
A. 20 days
B. 13.9 days
C. 28.8 days
D. 0.0347 day^−1
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: Mean life = 1.44 × T½ = 1.44 × 20 = 28.8 days. 20 days uses the half-life as the mean life. 13.9 days is 0.693 × 20, which multiplies by 0.693 instead of dividing. 0.0347 day^−1 is λ = 0.693/20, not a mean life.

### Q3
Stem: The half-life is 6.0 hours. What is the decay constant?
A. 0.1155 hour^−1
B. 8.66 hours
C. 4.16 hours
D. 0.693 hour^−1
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: λ = 0.693 / 6.0 = 0.1155 hour^−1. 8.66 hours is the mean life, 6.0 / 0.693. 4.16 hours is 0.693 × 6.0. 0.693 hour^−1 forgets to divide by the 6-hour half-life.

### Q4
Stem: An assay is 64 mCi. Forty hours later the same source is 4 mCi. What is the half-life?
A. 10 hours
B. 40 hours
C. 4 hours
D. 14.4 hours
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: 64 / 4 = 16 = 2^4, so four half-lives elapsed in 40 hours. T½ = 40 / 4 = 10 hours. 40 hours is the elapsed time, not the half-life. 4 hours treats the final activity as a time. 14.4 hours is 1.44 × 10, the mean life of the correct half-life.

### Q5
Stem: A permanent implant has an initial dose rate of 4.0 cGy/h and a half-life of 17 days. Using total dose = initial dose rate × 1.44 × half-life, what total dose is delivered?
A. 2350 cGy
B. 1632 cGy
C. 1131 cGy
D. 98 cGy
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: Half-life in hours = 17 × 24 = 408 h. Mean life = 1.44 × 408 = 587.52 h. Total dose = 4.0 cGy/h × 587.52 h = 2350 cGy. 1632 cGy uses the half-life instead of the mean life (4.0 × 408). 1131 cGy uses 0.693 × 408 × 4.0. 98 cGy is 4.0 × 1.44 × 17 and treats the dose rate as if it were per day.

### Q6
Stem: A 50 mCi source decays for a time equal to one mean life. Using e^(−1) = 0.368, the activity left is:
A. 25 mCi
B. 18.4 mCi
C. 31.6 mCi
D. 50 mCi
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: One mean life means λt = 1, so the remaining fraction is e^(−1) = 0.368. Activity = 50 × 0.368 = 18.4 mCi. 25 mCi is one half-life (50%). 31.6 mCi is the activity that has decayed, 50 × (1 − 0.368), not the activity left.

### Q7
Stem: A 90 mCi source is observed for exactly two half-lives. What activity remains?
A. 22.5 mCi
B. 45 mCi
C. 62.4 mCi
D. 130 mCi
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: Two half-lives leave (1/2)^2 = 1/4 of the activity. 90 / 4 = 22.5 mCi. 45 mCi is only one half-life. 62.4 mCi is 90 × 0.693, inserting the decay-constant factor as if it were a remaining fraction. 130 mCi is 90 × 1.44, a mean-life multiplication that increases the activity.

### Q8
Stem: A calculation asks for activity after an integer number of half-lives. Which substitution is the correct one?
A. A = A0 × (1/2)^(t / T½)
B. A = A0 × (1/2)^(t / 1.44 T½)
C. A = A0 × 1.44 × (t / T½)
D. A = A0 × e^(+0.693 t / T½)
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: The remaining fraction is one-half raised to the number of half-lives, t/T½. Option B counts mean lives as if they were half-lives. Option C multiplies by the mean-life factor instead of cutting the activity in half. Option D uses a positive exponent, so the activity would grow.

## Radioactivity Quiz

### Q1
Stem: A source has a 15-day half-life. What fraction of the activity remains after 45 days?
A. 1/2
B. 1/3
C. 1/8
D. 1/15
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: 45 / 15 = 3 half-lives. Fraction left = (1/2)^3 = 1/8. 1/3 divides by the number of half-lives. 1/15 divides by the half-life in days. 1/2 is a single half-life.

### Q2
Stem: Using 1.44, the mean life of Cs-137 (half-life 30 years) is:
A. 30 years
B. 20.8 years
C. 43.2 years
D. 0.023 year^−1
Answer: C
Objective: I.7
Why: Mean life = 1.44 × 30 = 43.2 years. 30 years equates mean life with half-life. 20.8 years is 0.693 × 30. 0.023 year^−1 is λ = 0.693/30, not the mean life.

### Q3
Stem: Au-198 is a temporary implant nuclide. Its half-life is about:
A. 2.7 days
B. 17 days
C. 59.4 days
D. 73.8 days
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: Au-198’s half-life is 2.7 days, with a principal gamma near 412 keV. Seventeen days is Pd-103, 59.4 days is I-125, and 73.8 days is Ir-192.

### Q4
Stem: A beta-minus decay increases Z by 1 and leaves A unchanged. Which listed source is a pure beta emitter?
A. Co-60
B. P-32
C. Cs-137
D. Tc-99m
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: P-32 (half-life 14.3 days) is a pure beta-minus emitter. Co-60 and Cs-137 are known for their gamma emissions. Tc-99m decays by isomeric transition and a 140 keV gamma.

### Q5
Stem: F-18 is used for PET because it decays by positron emission. Its half-life is about:
A. 110 minutes
B. 6 hours
C. 8 days
D. 30 years
Answer: A
Objective: I.7
Why: F-18’s half-life is 110 minutes. The positrons annihilate to two 511 keV photons. Six hours is Tc-99m, 8 days is I-131, and 30 years is Cs-137.

### Q6
Stem: In secular equilibrium, a very long-lived parent and a short-lived daughter have activities that are approximately equal. The pair then appears to decay with:
A. The daughter half-life
B. The parent half-life
C. The sum of the two half-lives
D. A half-life of zero, because equal activities mean decay has stopped
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: Once secular equilibrium is reached, both activities fall together with the parent half-life. The daughter’s own half-life governed the buildup, not the later slope. Adding the half-lives has no role, and equal activities do not mean the source is stable.

## Radioactive Decay Calculation Mini-Quiz

### Q1
Stem: A source is 240 mCi and its half-life is 6 hours. What is the activity after 18 hours?
A. 120 mCi
B. 80 mCi
C. 30 mCi
D. 15 mCi
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: 18 / 6 = 3 half-lives. 240 × (1/2)^3 = 240 / 8 = 30 mCi. 120 mCi is one half-life. 80 mCi is 240/3. 15 mCi is four half-lives (240/16).

### Q2
Stem: The half-life is 74.0 days. What is λ?
A. 0.00937 day^−1
B. 106.8 days
C. 51.3 days
D. 0.0937 day^−1
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: λ = 0.693 / 74.0 = 0.009365 day^−1, which rounds to 0.00937 day^−1. 106.8 days is the mean life, 74.0 / 0.693. 51.3 days is 0.693 × 74.0. 0.0937 day^−1 is a tenfold decimal error.

### Q3
Stem: Activity falls from 200 mCi to 25 mCi. The half-life is 5 days. How much time elapsed?
A. 5 days
B. 8 days
C. 15 days
D. 25 days
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: 200 / 25 = 8 = 2^3, so three half-lives elapsed. Time = 3 × 5 = 15 days. Five days is one half-life. Eight days treats the factor of 8 as a time. Twenty-five days multiplies the final activity by the half-life.

### Q4
Stem: The half-life is 50 hours. Using 1.44, the mean life is:
A. 72 hours
B. 50 hours
C. 34.7 hours
D. 0.0139 hour^−1
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: Mean life = 1.44 × 50 = 72 hours. Fifty hours equates the two lifetimes. 34.7 hours is 0.693 × 50. 0.0139 hour^−1 is λ = 0.693/50.

### Q5
Stem: λ = 0.05 hour^−1 and the elapsed time is 20 hours. The initial activity is 80 mCi. Using e^(−1) = 0.368, what activity remains?
A. 29.4 mCi
B. 40 mCi
C. 4.0 mCi
D. 115 mCi
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: λt = 0.05 × 20 = 1, so the remaining fraction is e^(−1) = 0.368. Activity = 80 × 0.368 = 29.4 mCi. 40 mCi assumes a half-life has passed (λt = 0.693, not 1). 4.0 mCi is 80 × 0.05, multiplying by λ and dropping the time. 115 mCi is 80 × 1.44.

### Q6
Stem: The physical half-life is 60 days and the biological half-life is 30 days. What is the effective half-life?
A. 90 days
B. 45 days
C. 20 days
D. 30 days
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: 1/Teff = 1/60 + 1/30 = 1/60 + 2/60 = 3/60 = 1/20, so Teff = 20 days. Ninety days adds the half-lives. Forty-five days averages them. Thirty days ignores the physical half-life.

### Q7
Stem: A 128 mCi source decays through 7 half-lives. What activity remains?
A. 1 mCi
B. 18.3 mCi
C. 64 mCi
D. 2 mCi
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: (1/2)^7 = 1/128. Activity = 128 / 128 = 1 mCi. 64 mCi is one half-life. 2 mCi is six half-lives (128/64). 18.3 mCi is 128/7.

### Q8
Stem: An assay is 48 mCi. Four hours later it is 12 mCi. What is the half-life?
A. 2 hours
B. 4 hours
C. 1 hour
D. 8 hours
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: 48 / 12 = 4 = 2^2, so two half-lives passed in 4 hours. T½ = 4 / 2 = 2 hours. Four hours is the clock time. One hour splits the interval into four. Eight hours doubles the clock time instead of dividing by the number of half-lives.

### Q9
Stem: λt = 0.30 and the initial activity is 100 mCi. Use e^(−0.30) = 0.741. What activity remains?
A. 74.1 mCi
B. 50 mCi
C. 30 mCi
D. 135 mCi
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: A = 100 × e^(−0.30) = 100 × 0.741 = 74.1 mCi. Fifty millicuries would require λt = 0.693, a full half-life. Thirty millicuries is 100 × 0.30, using the exponent as a remaining fraction. 135 mCi uses e^(+0.30) ≈ 1.35 and makes the source grow.
