# Module 41. Radiation Protection

Original CMD practice items. Not retired MDCB questions and not a commercial quiz.

## ALARA and maximum permissible dose equivalent based on NRCP recommendations, regulatory guidelines

### Q1
Stem: What does ALARA require beyond the numeric limit?
A. Keep doses as low as reasonably achievable
B. Stop all work once any badge reads zero
C. Ignore the numeric limits if the task is clinical
D. Double the occupational limit for experienced staff
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: ALARA means as low as reasonably achievable, economic and social factors included. It sits on top of the NCRP and regulatory limits. It does not replace them and it does not require zero dose. Correct choice: Keep doses as low as reasonably achievable. ALARA does not demand zero. It demands reasonable further reduction. Limits still bind. ALARA is additional. Experience does not raise the limit.

### Q2
Stem: NCRP 116 annual occupational effective-dose limit is which value?
A. 1 mSv
B. 150 mSv
C. 500 mSv
D. 50 mSv
Answer: D
Objective: VI.1
Why: NCRP 116 sets the occupational effective dose at 50 mSv in a year, with a cumulative guidance of 10 mSv times age in years. The public continuous limit is 1 mSv, the lens is 150 mSv, and the skin and extremities are 500 mSv. Correct choice: 50 mSv. 1 mSv is the annual public limit for continuous exposure, not the occupational limit. 150 mSv is the NCRP 116 annual lens limit. 500 mSv is the NCRP 116 annual skin, hand, and foot limit.

### Q3
Stem: A worker is 44 years old. NCRP 116 cumulative effective-dose guidance is 10 mSv times age in years. What is that cumulative guidance?
A. 44 mSv
B. 50 mSv
C. 880 mSv
D. 440 mSv
Answer: D
Objective: VI.1
Why: Cumulative guidance = 10 mSv × age = 10 × 44 = 440 mSv. The 50 mSv annual limit still applies in any single year. Correct choice: 440 mSv. That forgot the factor of 10 mSv per year of age. 50 mSv is the annual limit, not the cumulative guidance. Doubling 10 × age is not the guidance.

### Q4
Stem: Once a pregnancy is declared, which NCRP-style limits are used for the embryo or fetus?
A. 50 mSv in a month
B. 0.5 mSv in a month and 5 mSv for the gestation
C. 5 mSv in a month and 50 mSv for the gestation
D. No limit, because the worker is occupational
Answer: B
Objective: VI.1
Why: The protection recommendation used for a declared pregnancy is 0.5 mSv per month and 5 mSv over the gestation. It is not the 50 mSv occupational annual limit. Correct choice: 0.5 mSv in a month and 5 mSv for the gestation. 50 mSv is the adult occupational annual limit, not the fetal monthly limit. The monthly and total numbers are swapped and too high. A declared pregnancy has its own limit.

### Q5
Stem: Radiation-protection planning for stochastic effects uses which dose-response assumption?
A. A sharp threshold at 50 mSv with zero risk below it
B. Hormesis as the regulatory model
C. Linear no-threshold
D. Quadratic only above 1 Gy, and zero below
Answer: C
Objective: VI.1
Why: Regulatory and NCRP protection practice uses a linear no-threshold model for stochastic risk: risk is taken as proportional to dose, with no safe cutoff built into the planning. That is why ALARA still applies below the numeric limit. Correct choice: Linear no-threshold. 50 mSv is a limit, not a proof that risk is zero underneath it. Protection programs do not plan on a benefit from small doses. That is not the protection assumption used to set public and occupational limits.

### Q6
Stem: An unrestricted corridor reads 0.05 mSv in one hour from external radiation. The public hourly limit is 0.02 mSv in any one hour. By how much does this hour exceed that hourly limit?
A. 0.03 mSv
B. 0.05 mSv
C. 0.02 mSv
D. 0.07 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: 0.05 − 0.02 = 0.03 mSv. Correct choice: 0.03 mSv. The 0.05 mSv reading is the measured hour, not the excess. The 0.02 mSv figure is the limit, not the excess. Adding the reading to the limit gives 0.05 + 0.02 = 0.07 mSv.

### Q7
Stem: A waiting room reads 0.012 mSv in one hour from external sources. The public hourly limit is 0.02 mSv in any one hour. Which comparison fits that hour?
A. The hour is over 0.02 mSv by 0.008 mSv
B. The hour is under 0.02 mSv by 0.008 mSv
C. The hour exceeds the 1 mSv annual public limit
D. The hour exceeds the 50 mSv occupational TEDE limit
Answer: B
Objective: VI.1
Why: 0.020 − 0.012 = 0.008 mSv, so the hour is under the hourly limit. Correct choice: The hour is under 0.02 mSv by 0.008 mSv. The hour does not sit 0.008 mSv over 0.02 mSv. One hour at 0.012 mSv is under both 1 mSv and 50 mSv. The annual public test is the sum for the year, not this single hour.

### Q8
Stem: A member of the public receives 1.6 mSv TEDE in a year. The annual public limit is 1 mSv. How far over that annual limit is 1.6 mSv?
A. 1.6 mSv
B. 1.0 mSv
C. 0.6 mSv
D. 48.4 mSv
Answer: C
Objective: VI.1
Why: 1.6 − 1.0 = 0.6 mSv. Correct choice: 0.6 mSv. The received dose is 1.6 mSv. The limit is 1.0 mSv. The gap to the occupational TEDE limit is 50 − 1.6 = 48.4 mSv, and that occupational gap leaves a public dose that is already over 1 mSv still over the public limit.

### Q9
Stem: Which pair is the public dose limit in 10 CFR 20.1301?
A. 50 mSv in a year and 1 mSv in any one hour
B. 1 mSv in a year and 0.02 mSv in any one hour
C. 5 mSv in a year and 0.02 mSv in any one hour
D. 1 mSv in a year and 0.5 mSv in any one hour
Answer: B
Objective: VI.1
Why: 10 CFR 20.1301 limits a member of the public to 1 mSv TEDE in a year and to 0.02 mSv in any one hour from external sources in an unrestricted area. Correct choice: 1 mSv in a year and 0.02 mSv in any one hour. Fifty millisieverts is the adult occupational TEDE limit. Five millisieverts is 0.10 × 50, the minor TEDE limit and the adult TEDE monitoring threshold. The NRC gestation limit is also written as 5 mSv, in a different rule. The hourly public figure is 0.02 mSv. The 0.5 mSv value is the NCRP monthly embryo recommendation.

### Q10
Stem: This lesson uses 0.02 mSv in a week as an uncontrolled-area design goal and 0.02 mSv in any one hour as a public limit. Which statement keeps those two uses apart?
A. They are one limit, because both numbers are 0.02 mSv
B. The weekly figure is the design goal behind a barrier. The hourly figure is the 10 CFR 20.1301 cap in an unrestricted area
C. The hourly figure is the occupational TEDE limit
D. The weekly figure replaces the 1 mSv annual public limit
Answer: B
Objective: VI.1
Why: Fifty weeks at 0.02 mSv is 50 × 0.02 = 1.0 mSv, so the weekly design goal lines up with the annual public limit of 1 mSv. Correct choice: The weekly figure is the design goal behind a barrier. The hourly figure is the 10 CFR 20.1301 cap in an unrestricted area. The hourly cap is one hour in an unrestricted area. The occupational TEDE limit is 50 mSv. The weekly design goal is the weekly slice of the 1 mSv annual public limit.

### Q11
Stem: The adult occupational TEDE limit is 50 mSv in a year. A minor’s annual TEDE limit is 10 percent of that adult limit. The deep-dose monitoring trigger for the minor is a separate 1 mSv figure. Which pairing is correct?
A. Annual limit 5 mSv, and monitoring when the deep dose is likely to exceed 1 mSv
B. Annual limit 1 mSv, and monitoring when the deep dose is likely to exceed 5 mSv
C. Annual limit 50 mSv, and monitoring when the deep dose is likely to exceed 10 mSv
D. Annual limit 15 mSv, and monitoring when the deep dose is likely to exceed 0.5 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: 0.10 × 50 = 5 mSv. That product is the minor’s annual TEDE limit under 10 CFR 20.1207. 10 CFR 20.1502 monitors a minor when the deep dose is likely to exceed 1 mSv. Correct choice: Annual limit 5 mSv, and monitoring when the deep dose is likely to exceed 1 mSv. Swapping the two numbers uses 1 mSv as the limit and 5 mSv as the trigger. Fifty millisieverts is the adult limit. Ten millisieverts is neither minor figure. Fifteen millisieverts is 0.10 × 150, the minor lens limit, and 0.5 mSv is the NCRP monthly embryo recommendation.

## Radiation Monitoring for Personnel and Patients

### Q1
Stem: Where is a collar badge worn when a lead apron is used?
A. Under the apron at the waist only
B. In the control room, not on the person
C. Inside the lead glove
D. Outside the apron at the collar
Answer: D
Objective: VI.2
Why: The collar badge sits outside the apron so it sees the unshielded head and neck and is the usual lens monitor. A second badge under the apron, if used, estimates the protected body. Correct choice: Outside the apron at the collar. An under-apron badge estimates the protected torso. It misses head, neck, and lens dose. A badge left in the console does not measure the worker. The glove is an extremity shield. The collar badge is for the neck and lens.

### Q2
Stem: A contamination event gives an absorbed dose with a stated wR. Absorbed dose is 2.5 mGy from alpha particles. The problem states wR = 20. Dose equivalent H = wR × absorbed dose. Do not apply a tissue weighting factor; that would make effective dose, which is not asked. What is H?
A. 50.0 mSv
B. 2.5 mSv
C. 0.1 mSv
D. 22.5 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: Substitute H = 20 × 2.5 mGy = 50 mSv, reported as 50.0 mSv. With wR stated, you do not replace it by 1 unless the radiation actually has wR = 1 (photons and electrons). Reporting the absorbed dose unchanged gives 2.5 mSv and drops wR. Dividing by wR gives 0.1 mSv. Adding wR to the absorbed dose gives 22.5 mSv.

### Q3
Stem: A family member not trained as a radiation worker receives 3 mSv in a year from a released patient. Which limit is exceeded?
A. The 50 mSv occupational limit
B. The 150 mSv lens limit
C. The 1 mSv public limit
D. No limit, because 3 mSv is under 5 mSv
Answer: C
Objective: VI.2
Why: Members of the public are limited to 1 mSv in a year for continuous exposure under NCRP 116. A 3 mSv annual dose exceeds that public limit even though it is under the 50 mSv occupational limit and under the 5 mSv patient-release criterion. Correct choice: The 1 mSv public limit. 3 mSv is under 50 mSv, but the person is not an occupational worker. 3 mSv is not a lens overage. 5 mSv is the patient-release ceiling for any other individual, and the public annual limit for continuous exposure is 1 mSv. This item asks which public annual limit 3 mSv exceeds. It exceeds 1 mSv.

### Q4
Stem: A corridor survey reads 0.20 mSv in 1 hour at 30 cm from a source container. Which NRC posting fits?
A. Radiation area, not yet a high radiation area
B. No posting, because 0.20 mSv is under 0.05 mSv
C. Very high radiation area
D. High radiation area only, because 0.20 exceeds 1 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.2
Why: NRC posting: a radiation area is above 0.05 mSv (5 mrem) in 1 hour at 30 cm, a high radiation area is above 1 mSv (100 mrem) in 1 hour at 30 cm, and a very high radiation area is 5 Gy in 1 hour at 1 meter. 0.20 mSv is between 0.05 and 1, so it is a radiation area and not a high radiation area. Correct choice: Radiation area, not yet a high radiation area. 0.20 is greater than 0.05. A radiation area starts at 0.05 mSv in 1 hour at 30 cm. Very high is 5 Gy in 1 hour at 1 m, not 0.20 mSv. High radiation area starts at 1 mSv in 1 hour at 30 cm. 0.20 is below that and above 0.05.

### Q5
Stem: A declared pregnant worker's badge for the month is 0.80 mSv to the fetus monitor. Which statement matches the monthly limit?
A. 0.80 mSv is under 0.5 mSv
B. 0.80 mSv exceeds 0.5 mSv
C. The monthly limit is 5 mSv
D. The monthly limit is 50 mSv
Answer: B
Objective: VI.2
Why: The monthly embryo/fetus guidance is 0.5 mSv. 0.80 − 0.50 = 0.30 mSv over that monthly value. The gestation cap is 5 mSv, which this single month has not reached, but the month itself is already over. Correct choice: 0.80 mSv exceeds 0.5 mSv. 0.80 is larger than 0.50. 5 mSv is the gestation total, not the month. 50 mSv is the adult annual occupational limit.

### Q6
Stem: An adult is likely to receive 8 mSv TEDE in the year from external sources and will not enter a high radiation area. Does 10 CFR 20.1502 require individual monitoring for this adult?
A. No monitor, because 8 mSv is under 50 mSv
B. A monitor, because every adult is badged at 1 mSv
C. A monitor, because 8 mSv exceeds 5 mSv
D. No monitor, because 8 mSv is under 15 mSv
Answer: C
Objective: VI.2
Why: Ten percent of the 50 mSv TEDE limit is 0.10 × 50 = 5 mSv. Then 8 − 5 = 3 mSv over that adult monitoring threshold, so a badge is required. Correct choice: A monitor, because 8 mSv exceeds 5 mSv. Staying under the 50 mSv annual limit does not remove the requirement. The 1 mSv figure is the minor and declared-pregnant deep-dose trigger. Fifteen millisieverts is 0.10 × 150, ten percent of the lens limit, and this estimate is a TEDE.

### Q7
Stem: An adult is likely to receive 2 mSv TEDE in the year and must enter a high radiation area. Which 10 CFR 20.1502 result fits?
A. No monitor, because 2 mSv is under 5 mSv and the area adds nothing
B. A monitor, because the person enters a high radiation area
C. A monitor only after the recorded dose has already passed 50 mSv
D. A monitor only if the person is a declared-pregnant worker
Answer: B
Objective: VI.2
Why: The adult TEDE monitoring threshold is 0.10 × 50 = 5 mSv. This estimate is under it by 5 − 2 = 3 mSv. Correct choice: A monitor, because the person enters a high radiation area. Entry into a high radiation area is its own monitoring requirement. The decision is made before the dose reaches 50 mSv. Declared-pregnant monitoring uses a 1 mSv deep-dose trigger over the pregnancy.

### Q8
Stem: A 17-year-old worker is likely to receive a deep dose of 2.2 mSv in the year. Monitoring of a minor is required when that deep dose is likely to exceed 1 mSv. By how much does 2.2 mSv exceed 1 mSv, and is monitoring required?
A. 2.2 mSv is under 1 mSv, so monitoring is not required
B. 2.8 mSv under the 5 mSv adult threshold, so a minor is not monitored
C. 1.2 mSv over 1 mSv, so monitoring is required
D. 47.8 mSv under 50 mSv, so monitoring is not required
Answer: C
Objective: VI.2
Why: 2.2 − 1.0 = 1.2 mSv. The minor deep-dose trigger is 1 mSv, and 2.2 mSv is above it, so monitoring is required. Correct choice: 1.2 mSv over 1 mSv, so monitoring is required. The adult threshold is 0.10 × 50 = 5 mSv, and 5.0 − 2.2 = 2.8 mSv under that adult threshold. That adult gap does not apply to the minor. The distance to the adult annual limit is 50 − 2.2 = 47.8 mSv, which is not the monitoring test.

### Q9
Stem: After a written declaration, the estimated deep dose for the rest of the pregnancy is 0.6 mSv. Declared-pregnant monitoring is required when the deep dose is likely to exceed 1 mSv over the pregnancy. Which comparison is correct?
A. 0.6 mSv exceeds 1 mSv, so this estimate already meets the monitoring trigger
B. 0.6 mSv is 0.4 mSv under 1 mSv, so this estimate alone does not meet the 1 mSv monitoring trigger
C. 0.6 mSv exceeds the 5 mSv gestation limit
D. 0.6 mSv exceeds the 50 mSv occupational TEDE limit
Answer: B
Objective: VI.2
Why: 1.0 − 0.6 = 0.4 mSv. The estimate is under the 1 mSv declared-pregnant monitoring trigger. Correct choice: 0.6 mSv is 0.4 mSv under 1 mSv, so this estimate alone does not meet the 1 mSv monitoring trigger. The NRC gestation limit is 5 mSv, and 0.6 is under 5. The adult TEDE limit is 50 mSv, and 0.6 is under 50. The NCRP monthly recommendation of 0.5 mSv is a different comparison from this monitoring trigger.

### Q10
Stem: A declared-pregnant worker is likely to receive 2.5 mSv deep dose over the pregnancy and will not enter a high radiation area. Her adult TEDE estimate for the year is 3 mSv. Which monitoring statement is correct?
A. No monitoring, because 3 mSv is under 5 mSv
B. Monitoring is required, because 2.5 mSv exceeds the 1 mSv declared-pregnant trigger
C. Monitoring is required because 3 mSv exceeds 1 mSv for every adult
D. No monitoring, because 2.5 mSv is under the 5 mSv gestation limit
Answer: B
Objective: VI.2
Why: The declared-pregnant deep-dose trigger is 1 mSv over the pregnancy. 2.5 − 1.0 = 1.5 mSv over that trigger, so monitoring is required. Correct choice: Monitoring is required, because 2.5 mSv exceeds the 1 mSv declared-pregnant trigger. The adult trigger is 0.10 × 50 = 5 mSv, and 3 mSv is under it by 5 − 3 = 2 mSv. Three millisieverts is not the adult threshold. The 5 mSv gestation limit is the dose ceiling. Staying under 5 mSv does not cancel monitoring once 1 mSv is likely.

### Q11
Stem: A clinic’s radiation protection program sets ALARA investigational level I and level II in millisieverts. A badge crosses level I and remains under the 50 mSv annual TEDE limit. What is true?
A. Level I and level II are the same federal doses in every clinic
B. Crossing level I starts a local review, and the 50 mSv TEDE limit is a separate ceiling
C. Level II replaces the 50 mSv TEDE limit
D. Crossing level I means the badge may be stopped for the rest of the year
Answer: B
Objective: VI.1
Why: Investigational levels are written in the local program. This lesson assigns no universal millisievert value to level I or level II. Level I is the lower local threshold and starts a review. Level II is the higher local threshold and starts a formal investigation. Correct choice: Crossing level I starts a local review, and the 50 mSv TEDE limit is a separate ceiling. Neither local level replaces the 50 mSv TEDE limit in 10 CFR 20.1201. Crossing level I is not a reason to stop individual monitoring.

## Treatment Vault Shielding Requirements

### Q1
Stem: Which barrier is struck by the useful beam?
A. The secondary barrier
B. Only the door, never a wall
C. The primary barrier
D. The maze only
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: Primary barriers intercept the useful beam and are thicker. Secondary barriers are sized for head leakage and patient scatter. Use factor U is 1 for a barrier the beam can aim at full time, and lower if the beam rarely points there. Correct choice: The primary barrier. Secondary barriers see leakage and scatter, not the useful beam aimed at them. The door is often a secondary barrier. A primary wall is whatever the beam can point at. A maze is a design that lets the door see scattered radiation rather than the useful beam.

### Q2
Stem: Vault design numbers are given so the transmission can be computed. Design limit P = 4e-05 Gy/week at the protected point, distance d = 5 m from the target, workload W = 200 Gy/week at 1 m, use factor U = 1, occupancy T = 0.5. Required primary transmission B = P d² / (W U T). What is B?
A. 1.000e-05, dimensionless
B. 5.000e-06, dimensionless
C. 4.000e-07, dimensionless
D. 1.000e+05, dimensionless
Answer: A
Objective: VI.3
Why: d² = 5² = 25. WUT = 200 × 1 × 0.5 = 100. P × d² = 4e-05 × 25 = 0.001. B = 0.001 / 100 = 1e-05. Transmission is dimensionless: the Gy/week in P and in W cancel. A smaller B means a thicker barrier. Dropping occupancy T gives 5.000e-06. Dropping d² gives 4.000e-07. Inverting the formula gives 1.000e+05, which is greater than 1 and cannot be a required transmission.

### Q3
Stem: The barrier calculation required a transmission of 1×10^−5. The required transmission is 10^−5. How many tenth-value layers is that? Each TVL multiplies transmission by 0.1.
A. 16.6 HVL
B. 5 TVL
C. 10 TVL
D. 1 TVL
Answer: B
Objective: VI.3
Why: Transmission = 10^(−5) = (0.1)^5, so the TVL count n = 5. That is 5 TVL. One TVL is about 3.32 HVL because log10(2) = 0.3010 and 1/0.3010 = 3.32, so 5 TVL is about 16.61 HVL, but the question asks for TVL. Converting to HVL (dividing the exponent by log10(2)) gives about 16.6 HVL, which is the companion count, not the TVL count. Doubling the exponent gives 10 TVL. 1 TVL would be transmission 0.1 only when one TVL is required.

### Q4
Stem: What material is added specifically for thermal neutrons at a high-energy linac door?
A. More lead only
B. A thicker wedge
C. Boronated polyethylene
D. Air gap with no hydrogenous material
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: Linacs above about 10 MV produce photoneutrons. The door stack uses hydrogenous material to slow them and boron (boronated polyethylene) to capture the thermal neutrons, plus metal for the capture gammas. Correct choice: Boronated polyethylene. Lead is for photons. Thermal neutrons need a capture material such as boron. A wedge is a beam modifier inside the head, not door shielding. Neutrons are moderated in hydrogenous material and then captured. An empty gap does not do that job.

### Q5
Stem: A primary wall section must transmit no more than 1/16 of the narrow-beam intensity. A narrow beam is transmitted at a fraction 0.0625 of the incident intensity. How many HVL is that? Transmission = (1/2)^(number of HVL).
A. 16 HVL
B. 2.0 HVL
C. 3.3 HVL
D. 4 HVL
Answer: D
Objective: VI.3
Why: (1/2)^n = 0.0625. n = ln(0.0625) / ln(1/2) = 4.0000, so n = 4 HVL. Check: 2^4 = 16, and 1/16 = 0.0625. Reporting the denominator of the transmission fraction as if it were the HVL count gives 16 HVL. Taking half of the true HVL count gives 2.0 HVL. 3.3 HVL confuses one TVL (about 3.3 HVL, transmission 0.1) with this HVL count.

### Q20
Stem: A secondary barrier is a wall the useful beam does not strike. Which contributions is it designed to shield?
A. The useful beam alone, with leakage omitted
B. Head leakage and patient scatter
C. Only the wedge transmission inside the treatment head
D. The door’s neutron capture, with patient scatter omitted
Answer: B
Objective: VI.3
Why: Head leakage and patient scatter are the two secondary sources this wall is built for. Leakage leaves the head in all directions while the beam is on. Scatter leaves the patient. The useful beam is the load on a primary barrier. A wedge factor belongs in the monitor-unit calculation.

### Q21
Stem: Leakage from the treatment head is present whenever the beam is on, including when the gantry points at a different wall. Which use factor is used for that leakage term?
A. U = 1
B. U = 0.25, taken from a cardinal primary wall
C. U = 0, because leakage is excluded from the workload
D. U equal to the occupancy factor of the corridor
Answer: A
Objective: VI.3
Why: Leakage does not stop when the useful beam is aimed elsewhere, so the leakage use factor is 1. A primary cardinal wall may have U = 0.25 because the useful beam points there only part of the time. Using that 0.25 on leakage makes the required transmission too large and the secondary barrier too thin. Occupancy T is a separate factor from U.

### Q22
Stem: A survey measures 0.08 mSv in one hour at 30 cm from a source housing. Which posting matches that reading?
A. High radiation area, because 0.08 mSv in one hour at 30 cm is above 1 mSv in one hour at 30 cm
B. Very high radiation area, because 0.08 mSv exceeds 5 Gy in one hour at 1 m
C. Caution, radiation area: 0.08 mSv in one hour at 30 cm is above 0.05 mSv in one hour at 30 cm and below 1 mSv in one hour at 30 cm
D. No posting, because 0.08 mSv in one hour at 30 cm is below 0.05 mSv in one hour at 30 cm
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: A caution radiation area is a reading above 0.05 mSv in one hour at 30 cm. A high radiation area begins above 1 mSv in one hour at 30 cm. 0.08 mSv in one hour at 30 cm is above 0.05 and below 1, so the posting is caution, radiation area. Very high radiation area is above 5 Gy in one hour at 1 m.

### Q23
Stem: Primary transmission is B = P d² / (W U T). P and W are both given in Gy/week. What is B?
A. A weekly dose, in Gy/week, because P is in Gy/week
B. A weekly dose per square meter
C. A concrete thickness in centimeters
D. A dimensionless transmission; the Gy/week in P and in W cancel
Answer: D
Objective: VI.3
Why: P and W carry the same dose unit, so that unit cancels in the ratio. B is the fraction of the incident radiation the barrier may let through. It is not a dose in Gy/week and it is not the wall thickness. Thickness is the number of tenth-value layers times the TVL, after B is known.

### Q24
Stem: A primary wall receives the useful beam for one quarter of the workload. The design limit at the protected point is 0.0001 Gy per week. The point is 5 m from the target. The useful-beam workload is 500 Gy per week at 1 m. Occupancy at the protected point is full. What transmission may this primary wall allow?
A. 0.00002, dimensionless
B. 0.000004, dimensionless
C. 0.000005, dimensionless
D. 50000, dimensionless
Answer: A
Objective: VI.3
Why: Distance squared is 5 × 5 = 25. Design limit times that square is 0.0001 × 25 = 0.0025. One quarter of the workload is 0.25 × 500 = 125 Gy per week aimed at the wall. Full occupancy is 1, so the divisor is 125. Transmission is 0.0025 / 125 = 0.00002. Correct choice: 0.00002, dimensionless. Using 5 m only once gives 0.0001 × 5 = 0.0005, then 0.0005 / 125 = 0.000004. Using the whole 500 Gy per week gives 0.0025 / 500 = 0.000005. Inverting the ratio gives 125 / 0.0025 = 50000, which is greater than 1 and cannot be a required transmission.

### Q25
Stem: The useful beam delivers 800 Gy per week at 1 m. Head leakage at 1 m is 0.1 percent of that useful beam. What is the leakage workload at 1 m?
A. 0.8 Gy per week
B. 8 Gy per week
C. 80 Gy per week
D. 0.08 Gy per week
Answer: A
Objective: VI.3
Why: 0.1 percent is 0.1 / 100 = 0.001. Then 0.001 × 800 = 0.8 Gy per week. Correct choice: 0.8 Gy per week. Using 1 percent gives 0.01 × 800 = 8 Gy per week. Using 10 percent gives 0.10 × 800 = 80 Gy per week. Moving the decimal one place further gives 0.0001 × 800 = 0.08 Gy per week.

### Q26
Stem: A wall is being checked for head leakage only. The design limit at the protected point is 0.0001 Gy per week. The point is 5 m from the target. The useful-beam workload is 250 Gy per week at 1 m. Leakage at 1 m is 0.1 percent of the useful beam. The leakage use factor is 1 and the occupancy is 1. What transmission may that leakage barrier allow?
A. 0.00001, dimensionless
B. 0.002, dimensionless
C. 0.01, dimensionless
D. 0.04, dimensionless
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: 0.1 percent of 250 is 0.001 × 250 = 0.25 Gy per week. Distance squared is 5 × 5 = 25. Design limit times that square is 0.0001 × 25 = 0.0025. Divide by the leakage workload at use factor 1 and occupancy 1: 0.0025 / 0.25 = 0.01. Correct choice: 0.01, dimensionless. Using the full 250 Gy per week gives 0.0025 / 250 = 0.00001. Using 5 m only once gives 0.0001 × 5 = 0.0005, then 0.0005 / 0.25 = 0.002. Applying a use factor of 0.25 to the leakage workload gives 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.0625, then 0.0025 / 0.0625 = 0.04.

### Q27
Stem: Two calculations use the same weekly design limit, the same distance, the same useful-beam workload, and full occupancy. The primary wall uses a use factor of 0.25. A. 2.5
B. 25
C. 0.00025
D. 250
Answer: D
Objective: VI.3
Why: The design limit, distance, and occupancy cancel. The primary product of workload and use factor carries 0.25. The leakage product carries 0.001 × 1. The ratio is 0.25 / 0.001 = 250, so the primary wall may transmit 250 times less. Correct choice: 250. Dividing 0.25 by 0.01, as if 0.1 percent were 1 percent, gives 25. Dividing 0.25 by 0.1 gives 2.5. Multiplying 0.25 × 0.001 = 0.00025 is the product of the two factors, not the ratio.

## Radiation Protection and Personnel Monitoring Quiz

### Q1
Stem: Quiz. wR is stated as 10 for this neutron field. Absorbed dose is 4 mGy from neutrons. The problem states wR = 10. Dose equivalent H = wR × absorbed dose. Do not apply a tissue weighting factor; that would make effective dose, which is not asked. What is H?
A. 4.0 mSv
B. 0.4 mSv
C. 14.0 mSv
D. 40.0 mSv
Answer: D
Objective: VI.1
Why: Substitute H = 10 × 4 mGy = 40 mSv, reported as 40.0 mSv. With wR stated, you do not replace it by 1 unless the radiation actually has wR = 1 (photons and electrons). Reporting the absorbed dose unchanged gives 4.0 mSv and drops wR. Dividing by wR gives 0.4 mSv. Adding wR to the absorbed dose gives 14.0 mSv.

### Q6
Stem: Quiz. Compute primary transmission B. Design limit P = 0.00025 Gy/week at the protected point, distance d = 4 m from the target, workload W = 200 Gy/week at 1 m, use factor U = 0.25, occupancy T = 0.8. Required primary transmission B = P d² / (W U T). What is B?
A. 8.000e-05, dimensionless
B. 6.250e-06, dimensionless
C. 1.000e-04, dimensionless
D. 1.000e+04, dimensionless
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: d² = 4² = 16. WUT = 200 × 0.25 × 0.8 = 40. P × d² = 0.00025 × 16 = 0.004. B = 0.004 / 40 = 0.0001. Transmission is dimensionless. A smaller B means a thicker barrier. Dropping occupancy T gives 8.000e-05. Dropping d² gives 6.250e-06. Inverting the formula gives 1.000e+04, which is greater than 1 and cannot be a required transmission.

### Q7
Stem: Quiz. A door is thickened by exactly two HVL. What percent is transmitted? A shield is exactly two HVL thick in a narrow beam. What percent of the incident intensity is transmitted?
A. 50%
B. 10%
C. 25%
D. 75%
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: Transmission after n HVL is (1/2)^n. For n = 2, (1/2)^2 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. Substitute 100% × 1/4 = 25%. Correct choice: 25%. 50% is one HVL, not two. The second HVL halves the beam again. 10% is about one TVL (roughly 3.3 HVL), not two HVL. 75% is the attenuated fraction for two HVL (100%−25%), not the transmitted fraction.

### Q8
