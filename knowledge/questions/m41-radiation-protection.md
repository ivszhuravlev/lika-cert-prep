# Module 41. Radiation Protection

Original CMD practice items. Not retired MDCB questions and not a commercial quiz.

## ALARA and maximum permissible dose equivalent based on NRCP recommendations, regulatory guidelines

### Q1
Stem: What does ALARA require beyond the numeric limit?
A. Keep doses as low as reasonably achievable
B. Stop all work once any badge reads zero
C. Ignore the numeric limits if the task is clinical
D. Double the occupational limit for experienced staff
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: ALARA means as low as reasonably achievable, economic and social factors included. It sits on top of the NCRP and regulatory limits. It does not replace them and it does not require zero dose. Correct choice: Keep doses as low as reasonably achievable. ALARA does not demand zero. It demands reasonable further reduction. Limits still bind. ALARA is additional. Experience does not raise the limit.

### Q2
Stem: NCRP 116 annual occupational effective-dose limit is which value?
A. 1 mSv
B. 150 mSv
C. 500 mSv
D. 50 mSv
Answer: D
Objective: VI.1
Why: NCRP 116 sets the occupational effective dose at 50 mSv in a year, with a cumulative guidance of 10 mSv times age in years. The public continuous limit is 1 mSv, the lens is 150 mSv, and the skin and extremities are 500 mSv. Correct choice: 50 mSv. 1 mSv is the annual public limit for continuous exposure, not the occupational limit. 150 mSv is the NCRP 116 annual lens limit. 500 mSv is the NCRP 116 annual skin, hand, and foot limit.

### Q3
Stem: A worker is 44 years old. What is the NCRP 116 cumulative effective-dose guidance?
A. 44 mSv
B. 50 mSv
C. 880 mSv
D. 440 mSv
Answer: D
Objective: VI.1
Why: Cumulative guidance = 10 mSv × age = 10 × 44 = 440 mSv. The 50 mSv annual limit still applies in any single year. Correct choice: 440 mSv. That forgot the factor of 10 mSv per year of age. 50 mSv is the annual limit, not the cumulative guidance. Doubling 10 × age is not the guidance.

### Q4
Stem: Once a pregnancy is declared, which NCRP-style limits are used for the embryo or fetus?
A. 50 mSv in a month
B. 0.5 mSv in a month and 5 mSv for the gestation
C. 5 mSv in a month and 50 mSv for the gestation
D. No limit, because the worker is occupational
Answer: B
Objective: VI.1
Why: The protection recommendation used for a declared pregnancy is 0.5 mSv per month and 5 mSv over the gestation. It is not the 50 mSv occupational annual limit. Correct choice: 0.5 mSv in a month and 5 mSv for the gestation. 50 mSv is the adult occupational annual limit, not the fetal monthly limit. The monthly and total numbers are swapped and too high. A declared pregnancy has its own limit.

### Q5
Stem: Radiation-protection planning for stochastic effects uses which dose-response assumption?
A. A sharp threshold at 50 mSv with zero risk below it
B. Hormesis as the regulatory model
C. Linear no-threshold
D. Quadratic only above 1 Gy, and zero below
Answer: C
Objective: VI.1
Why: Regulatory and NCRP protection practice uses a linear no-threshold model for stochastic risk: risk is taken as proportional to dose, with no safe cutoff built into the planning. That is why ALARA still applies below the numeric limit. Correct choice: Linear no-threshold. 50 mSv is a limit, not a proof that risk is zero underneath it. Protection programs do not plan on a benefit from small doses. That is not the protection assumption used to set public and occupational limits.

## Radiation Monitoring for Personnel and Patients

### Q1
Stem: Where is a collar badge worn when a lead apron is used?
A. Under the apron at the waist only
B. In the control room, not on the person
C. Inside the lead glove
D. Outside the apron at the collar
Answer: D
Objective: VI.2
Why: The collar badge sits outside the apron so it sees the unshielded head and neck and is the usual lens monitor. A second badge under the apron, if used, estimates the protected body. Correct choice: Outside the apron at the collar. An under-apron badge estimates the protected torso. It misses head, neck, and lens dose. A badge left in the console does not measure the worker. The glove is an extremity shield. The collar badge is for the neck and lens.

### Q2
Stem: A contamination event gives an absorbed dose with a stated wR. Absorbed dose is 2.5 mGy from alpha particles. The problem states wR = 20. Dose equivalent H = wR × absorbed dose. Do not apply a tissue weighting factor; that would make effective dose, which is not asked. What is H?
A. 50.0 mSv
B. 2.5 mSv
C. 0.1 mSv
D. 22.5 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: Substitute H = 20 × 2.5 mGy = 50 mSv, reported as 50.0 mSv. With wR stated, you do not replace it by 1 unless the radiation actually has wR = 1 (photons and electrons). Reporting the absorbed dose unchanged gives 2.5 mSv and drops wR. Dividing by wR gives 0.1 mSv. Adding wR to the absorbed dose gives 22.5 mSv.

### Q3
Stem: A family member not trained as a radiation worker receives 3 mSv in a year from a released patient. Which limit is exceeded?
A. The 50 mSv occupational limit
B. The 150 mSv lens limit
C. The 1 mSv public limit
D. No limit, because 3 mSv is under 5 mSv
Answer: C
Objective: VI.2
Why: Members of the public are limited to 1 mSv in a year for continuous exposure under NCRP 116. A 3 mSv annual dose exceeds that public limit even though it is under the 50 mSv occupational limit and under the 5 mSv patient-release criterion. Correct choice: The 1 mSv public limit. 3 mSv is under 50 mSv, but the person is not an occupational worker. 3 mSv is not a lens overage. 5 mSv is the patient-release ceiling for any other individual, and the public annual limit for continuous exposure is 1 mSv. This item asks which public annual limit 3 mSv exceeds. It exceeds 1 mSv.

### Q4
Stem: A corridor survey reads 0.20 mSv in 1 hour at 30 cm from a source container. Which NRC posting fits?
A. Radiation area, not yet a high radiation area
B. No posting, because 0.20 mSv is under 0.05 mSv
C. Very high radiation area
D. High radiation area only, because 0.20 exceeds 1 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.2
Why: NRC posting: a radiation area is above 0.05 mSv (5 mrem) in 1 hour at 30 cm, a high radiation area is above 1 mSv (100 mrem) in 1 hour at 30 cm, and a very high radiation area is 5 Gy in 1 hour at 1 meter. 0.20 mSv is between 0.05 and 1, so it is a radiation area and not a high radiation area. Correct choice: Radiation area, not yet a high radiation area. 0.20 is greater than 0.05. A radiation area starts at 0.05 mSv in 1 hour at 30 cm. Very high is 5 Gy in 1 hour at 1 m, not 0.20 mSv. High radiation area starts at 1 mSv in 1 hour at 30 cm. 0.20 is below that and above 0.05.

### Q5
Stem: A declared pregnant worker's badge for the month is 0.80 mSv to the fetus monitor. Which statement matches the monthly limit?
A. 0.80 mSv is under 0.5 mSv
B. 0.80 mSv exceeds 0.5 mSv
C. The monthly limit is 5 mSv
D. The monthly limit is 50 mSv
Answer: B
Objective: VI.2
Why: The monthly embryo/fetus guidance is 0.5 mSv. 0.80 − 0.50 = 0.30 mSv over that monthly value. The gestation cap is 5 mSv, which this single month has not reached, but the month itself is already over. Correct choice: 0.80 mSv exceeds 0.5 mSv. 0.80 is larger than 0.50. 5 mSv is the gestation total, not the month. 50 mSv is the adult annual occupational limit.

## Treatment Vault Shielding Requirements

### Q1
Stem: Which barrier is struck by the useful beam?
A. The secondary barrier
B. Only the door, never a wall
C. The primary barrier
D. The maze only
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: Primary barriers intercept the useful beam and are thicker. Secondary barriers are sized for head leakage and patient scatter. Use factor U is 1 for a barrier the beam can aim at full time, and lower if the beam rarely points there. Correct choice: The primary barrier. Secondary barriers see leakage and scatter, not the useful beam aimed at them. The door is often a secondary barrier. A primary wall is whatever the beam can point at. A maze is a design that lets the door see scattered radiation rather than the useful beam.

### Q2
Stem: Vault design numbers are given so the transmission can be computed. Design limit P = 4e-05 Gy/week at the protected point, distance d = 5 m from the target, workload W = 200 Gy/week at 1 m, use factor U = 1, occupancy T = 0.5. Required primary transmission B = P d² / (W U T). What is B?
A. 1.000e-05, dimensionless
B. 5.000e-06, dimensionless
C. 4.000e-07, dimensionless
D. 1.000e+05, dimensionless
Answer: A
Objective: VI.3
Why: d² = 5² = 25. WUT = 200 × 1 × 0.5 = 100. P × d² = 4e-05 × 25 = 0.001. B = 0.001 / 100 = 1e-05. Transmission is dimensionless: the Gy/week in P and in W cancel. A smaller B means a thicker barrier. Dropping occupancy T gives 5.000e-06. Dropping d² gives 4.000e-07. Inverting the formula gives 1.000e+05, which is greater than 1 and cannot be a required transmission.

### Q3
Stem: The barrier calculation required a transmission of 1×10^−5. The required transmission is 10^−5. How many tenth-value layers is that? Each TVL multiplies transmission by 0.1.
A. 16.6 HVL
B. 5 TVL
C. 10 TVL
D. 1 TVL
Answer: B
Objective: VI.3
Why: Transmission = 10^(−5) = (0.1)^5, so the TVL count n = 5. That is 5 TVL. One TVL is about 3.32 HVL because log10(2) = 0.3010 and 1/0.3010 = 3.32, so 5 TVL is about 16.61 HVL, but the question asks for TVL. Converting to HVL (dividing the exponent by log10(2)) gives about 16.6 HVL, which is the companion count, not the TVL count. Doubling the exponent gives 10 TVL. 1 TVL would be transmission 0.1 only when one TVL is required.

### Q4
Stem: What material is added specifically for thermal neutrons at a high-energy linac door?
A. More lead only
B. A thicker wedge
C. Boronated polyethylene
D. Air gap with no hydrogenous material
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: Linacs above about 10 MV produce photoneutrons. The door stack uses hydrogenous material to slow them and boron (boronated polyethylene) to capture the thermal neutrons, plus metal for the capture gammas. Correct choice: Boronated polyethylene. Lead is for photons. Thermal neutrons need a capture material such as boron. A wedge is a beam modifier inside the head, not door shielding. Neutrons are moderated in hydrogenous material and then captured. An empty gap does not do that job.

### Q5
Stem: A primary wall section must transmit no more than 1/16 of the narrow-beam intensity. A narrow beam is transmitted at a fraction 0.0625 of the incident intensity. How many HVL is that? Transmission = (1/2)^(number of HVL).
A. 16 HVL
B. 2.0 HVL
C. 3.3 HVL
D. 4 HVL
Answer: D
Objective: VI.3
Why: (1/2)^n = 0.0625. n = ln(0.0625) / ln(1/2) = 4.0000, so n = 4 HVL. Check: 2^4 = 16, and 1/16 = 0.0625. Reporting the denominator of the transmission fraction as if it were the HVL count gives 16 HVL. Taking half of the true HVL count gives 2.0 HVL. 3.3 HVL confuses one TVL (about 3.3 HVL, transmission 0.1) with this HVL count.

## Radiation Protection and Personnel Monitoring Quiz

### Q1
Stem: Quiz. wR is stated as 10 for this neutron field. Absorbed dose is 4 mGy from neutrons. The problem states wR = 10. Dose equivalent H = wR × absorbed dose. Do not apply a tissue weighting factor; that would make effective dose, which is not asked. What is H?
A. 4.0 mSv
B. 0.4 mSv
C. 14.0 mSv
D. 40.0 mSv
Answer: D
Objective: VI.1
Why: Substitute H = 10 × 4 mGy = 40 mSv, reported as 40.0 mSv. With wR stated, you do not replace it by 1 unless the radiation actually has wR = 1 (photons and electrons). Reporting the absorbed dose unchanged gives 4.0 mSv and drops wR. Dividing by wR gives 0.4 mSv. Adding wR to the absorbed dose gives 14.0 mSv.

### Q4
Stem: NCRP 116 cumulative guidance for a 36-year-old worker is which value?
A. 36 mSv
B. 50 mSv
C. 360 mSv
D. 500 mSv
Answer: C
Objective: VI.1
Why: 10 mSv × 36 = 360 mSv. Substitute 10 × 36 = 360. Correct choice: 360 mSv. That drops the 10 mSv per year of age. That is one annual limit, not 10 × 36. 500 mSv is the extremity annual limit, not 10 × age.

### Q5
Stem: An occupational badge reads 62 mSv in one year. How far over the 50 mSv annual limit is that reading?
A. 12 mSv
B. 62 mSv
C. 50 mSv
D. 0 mSv
Answer: A
Objective: VI.1
Why: Excess = 62 − 50 = 12 mSv. The cumulative age guidance is a separate check and does not erase the annual limit. Correct choice: 12 mSv. 62 is the reading, not the excess. 50 is the limit, not the excess. 62 is not under 50.

### Q6
Stem: Quiz. Compute primary transmission B. Design limit P = 0.00025 Gy/week at the protected point, distance d = 4 m from the target, workload W = 200 Gy/week at 1 m, use factor U = 0.25, occupancy T = 0.8. Required primary transmission B = P d² / (W U T). What is B?
A. 8.000e-05, dimensionless
B. 6.250e-06, dimensionless
C. 1.000e-04, dimensionless
D. 1.000e+04, dimensionless
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: d² = 4² = 16. WUT = 200 × 0.25 × 0.8 = 40. P × d² = 0.00025 × 16 = 0.004. B = 0.004 / 40 = 0.0001. Transmission is dimensionless. A smaller B means a thicker barrier. Dropping occupancy T gives 8.000e-05. Dropping d² gives 6.250e-06. Inverting the formula gives 1.000e+04, which is greater than 1 and cannot be a required transmission.

### Q7
Stem: Quiz. A door is thickened by exactly two HVL. What percent is transmitted? A shield is exactly two HVL thick in a narrow beam. What percent of the incident intensity is transmitted?
A. 50%
B. 10%
C. 25%
D. 75%
Answer: C
Objective: VI.3
Why: Transmission after n HVL is (1/2)^n. For n = 2, (1/2)^2 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%. Substitute 100% × 1/4 = 25%. Correct choice: 25%. 50% is one HVL, not two. The second HVL halves the beam again. 10% is about one TVL (roughly 3.3 HVL), not two HVL. 75% is the attenuated fraction for two HVL (100%−25%), not the transmitted fraction.
