# Module 4 — Production of X-rays

Original items for x-ray production, spectra, tubes, and attenuation.

## X-Ray Production

### Q1
Stem: Most of the kinetic energy of electrons that strike a diagnostic x-ray target becomes:
A. Characteristic x-rays
B. Bremsstrahlung
C. Heat
D. Pair production
Answer: C
Objective: I.2
Why: Even in a high-Z target, only a small fraction of the electron energy leaves as x-rays. The rest is heat in the anode. Bremsstrahlung is the larger share of the x-rays, but it is still a small share of the incident electron energy. Pair production is not how an x-ray tube makes its beam.

### Q2
Stem: For a tungsten target (Z = 74) at 100 kV, the approximate radiative efficiency is 9 × 10^(−10) × Z × V, with V in volts. That efficiency is:
A. 0.666%
B. 6.66%
C. 66.6%
D. 99%
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: 9 × 10^(−10) × 74 × 100000 = 9 × 74 × 10^(−5) = 666 × 10^(−5) = 6.66 × 10^(−3), or 0.666%. About 99% is heat, which is the complement, not the x-ray fraction. 6.66% and 66.6% are decimal-place errors.

### Q3
Stem: Bremsstrahlung is produced when an incident electron:
A. Is captured by the nucleus and emits an alpha particle
B. Is deflected and slowed in the nuclear Coulomb field
C. Fills a K-shell vacancy
D. Annihilates with a positron in the target
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Bremsstrahlung is the continuous spectrum from deceleration of a charged particle in the nuclear field. Filling a K-shell vacancy produces characteristic radiation. Annihilation and alpha emission are not the tube’s production mechanisms.

### Q4
Stem: A K-shell binding energy is 70 keV and an L-shell binding energy is 11 keV. The Kα characteristic photon energy is:
A. 81 keV
B. 70 keV
C. 11 keV
D. 59 keV
Answer: D
Objective: I.2
Why: The characteristic photon energy is the difference of the binding energies: 70 − 11 = 59 keV. Adding them gives 81 keV. Reporting either binding energy alone forgets that the photon carries the difference.

### Q5
Stem: Tungsten’s K-shell binding energy is about 70 keV. Which tube potential can produce tungsten K characteristic x-rays?
A. 50 kVp
B. 40 kVp
C. 110 kVp
D. 25 kVp
Answer: C
Objective: I.2
Why: The tube voltage must supply electrons with at least the K-shell binding energy, so the kVp must exceed about 70. A 110 kVp beam can eject K-shell electrons. The lower potentials cannot, and their spectra are bremsstrahlung only.

## X-Ray Energy Spectrum

### Q1
Stem: A tube is operated at 124 kVp. The minimum wavelength in the bremsstrahlung spectrum is:
A. 0.100 Å
B. 1.24 Å
C. 12.4 Å
D. 124 Å
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: The Duane–Hunt limit is λmin (Å) = 12.4 / kVp = 12.4 / 124 = 0.100 Å. The other options skip the division or move the decimal. That wavelength belongs to the maximum photon energy, 124 keV.

### Q2
Stem: Added filtration is increased and kVp is not changed. What happens to the spectrum?
A. λmin decreases and the average energy decreases.
B. λmin is unchanged, intensity falls, and the average energy rises.
C. λmin is unchanged and the average energy falls because low-energy photons pass more easily.
D. Characteristic peak energies move up by the amount of filtration.
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Filtration preferentially removes low-energy photons, so the beam is hardened (average energy up) and the output falls. λmin is fixed by the kVp. Characteristic energies are atomic energy differences and do not shift with filtration.

### Q3
Stem: mA is doubled and kVp is held constant. The spectrum changes by:
A. Twice the area, with the same λmin and the same characteristic energies
B. A lower λmin and the same area
C. A shift of the K characteristic peaks to twice the energy
D. No change at all, because mA does not affect the number of electrons
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: mA scales the number of electrons and therefore the number of photons. It does not change the maximum energy or the characteristic energies. Those energies move only if kVp or the target atom changes.

### Q4
Stem: For a lightly filtered diagnostic beam, the average bremsstrahlung photon energy is closest to:
A. The full kVp, because every electron gives up all of its energy in one photon
B. About one-third to one-half of the kVp
C. 511 keV, regardless of kVp
D. The K-shell binding energy only
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Electrons can lose any fraction of their energy in a bremsstrahlung event, so the continuous spectrum runs from near zero up to the kVp and the average sits well below the peak, classically near one-third of kVp before heavy filtration and higher after filtration. It is not 511 keV and it is not only the characteristic energy.

### Q5
Stem: The heel effect on a radiograph is seen as:
A. Higher intensity on the cathode side of the field
B. Higher intensity on the anode side of the field
C. A harder beam on the cathode side and a softer beam on the anode side, with no intensity difference
D. A change in λmin from one side of the field to the other
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Photons produced deeper in the angled target must cross more target material to exit toward the anode side, so intensity is lower on the anode side and higher on the cathode side. λmin is still set by the kVp across the whole field.

## X-Ray Tube

### Q1
Stem: The line-focus principle uses a target angle whose sine is 0.20. The actual focal-spot length along the target is 10 mm. The effective focal-spot length is:
A. 2.0 mm
B. 50 mm
C. 10.2 mm
D. 8.0 mm
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Effective length = actual length × sin(θ) = 10 × 0.20 = 2.0 mm. 50 mm divides by the sine instead of multiplying. 10.2 mm adds the sine. 8.0 mm subtracts it. The smaller effective spot is what the image sees; the larger actual spot spreads heat.

### Q2
Stem: Why is the anode a high-Z metal such as tungsten, and why is it rotated in many diagnostic tubes?
A. High Z raises bremsstrahlung efficiency, and rotation spreads the heat over a larger area.
B. High Z is required for pair production at 100 kVp, and rotation changes the kVp.
C. Low melting point is preferred, and rotation lowers the mA.
D. Tungsten is used because its K edge is 511 keV.
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Radiative yield rises with Z, and tungsten also tolerates heat. Rotation increases the area that absorbs the electron beam so the focal spot can stay small. Pair production does not occur at diagnostic kVp. Tungsten’s K edge is near 70 keV, not 511 keV.

### Q3
Stem: Inherent filtration differs from added filtration because inherent filtration is:
A. A removable aluminum plate in the collimator
B. Filtration the beam cannot avoid, such as the glass envelope and oil
C. The flattening filter of a linac
D. The HVL measured after the beam has left the housing
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Inherent filtration is built into the tube and housing (envelope, oil, window). Added filtration is the intentional Al or Cu placed in the beam. A linac flattening filter is not part of a diagnostic x-ray tube. HVL is a measurement of the resulting quality, not the name of the inherent filter.

### Q4
Stem: The focusing cup around the filament is there to:
A. Increase the kVp
B. Shape the electron stream onto the focal spot
C. Rotate the anode
D. Convert bremsstrahlung into characteristic x-rays
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: The cup is held at filament potential and pinches the electron beam so it hits the target in a small spot. kVp is set by the high-voltage circuit. Anode rotation is a mechanical drive. The target, not the cup, produces the x-rays.

### Q5
Stem: A smaller target angle, with the actual focal-spot size held fixed, produces:
A. A larger effective focal spot and less heel effect
B. A smaller effective focal spot and a stronger heel effect
C. No change in effective focal spot, because angle affects only kVp
D. A lower λmin
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Effective spot size scales with sin(θ), so a steeper (smaller) angle shrinks the apparent source. The same geometry increases absorption toward the anode side, which is a stronger heel effect. λmin depends on kVp, not on the angle.

## Mass Attenuation Coefficient

### Q1
Stem: Linear attenuation coefficient μ and mass attenuation coefficient μ/ρ differ because:
A. μ/ρ removes the effect of density, so two samples of the same composition share μ/ρ even if one is compressed.
B. μ/ρ is larger when the sample is compressed, and μ stays fixed.
C. μ has units of cm^2/g and μ/ρ has units of cm^−1.
D. They are identical numbers with different names.
Answer: A
Objective: I.4
Why: μ (cm^−1) scales with density. Dividing by density gives μ/ρ (cm^2/g), which depends on composition and energy, not on how tightly the material is packed. The units in option C are reversed.

### Q2
Stem: A narrow beam has μ = 0.0462 mm^−1. What is the half-value layer?
A. 15 mm
B. 21.6 mm
C. 0.032 mm
D. 49.8 mm
Answer: A
Objective: I.10
Why: HVL = 0.693 / μ = 0.693 / 0.0462 = 15 mm. 21.6 mm is the mean free path, 1/μ. 0.032 mm is 0.693 × μ, the product instead of the quotient. 49.8 mm is the tenth-value layer, 2.30 / μ.

### Q3
Stem: The narrow-beam half-value layer is 2.0 mm Al. What fraction of the intensity is transmitted through 6.0 mm Al?
A. 1/2
B. 1/3
C. 1/6
D. 1/8
Answer: D
Objective: I.10
Why: Number of HVLs = 6.0 / 2.0 = 3. Transmission = (1/2)^3 = 1/8. One-half is a single HVL. One-third and one-sixth treat the thickness ratio as a linear fraction instead of an exponential.

### Q4
Stem: A narrow-beam measurement and a broad-beam measurement are made through the same slab. The broad-beam transmission is higher because:
A. Scatter created in the slab reaches the detector.
B. The broad beam has a smaller μ.
C. Pair production turns off in a broad beam.
D. The HVL is defined only for broad beams.
Answer: A
Objective: I.4
Why: Narrow-beam geometry stops scatter from reaching the detector, so it measures the primary attenuation. A broad beam adds scatter to the reading, so the apparent transmission is higher and the apparent HVL is larger. μ of the material did not change.

### Q5
Stem: Ice and liquid water have the same composition. Which statement is correct?
A. They have the same linear attenuation coefficient and different mass attenuation coefficients.
B. They have the same mass attenuation coefficient. The denser liquid has the larger linear attenuation coefficient.
C. Both coefficients are exactly zero below 1 MeV.
D. Mass attenuation coefficient scales with density, and the linear coefficient does not.
Answer: B
Objective: I.4
Why: μ/ρ is a property of composition and energy. The liquid is denser, so its μ = (μ/ρ) × ρ is larger. Option D reverses which coefficient carries the density.

## X-Ray Tube Design Mini-Quiz

### Q1
Stem: The cathode assembly of a diagnostic x-ray tube contains:
A. The filament and the focusing cup
B. The rotating tungsten disk only
C. The flattening filter and the ion chamber
D. The primary collimator and the MLC
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Electrons are boiled off the filament and shaped by the focusing cup. The rotating disk is the anode. Flattening filters, ion chambers, and MLCs belong to a linac, not to the tube cathode.

### Q2
Stem: Heat units for a single-phase diagnostic exposure are estimated by kVp × mA × seconds. Why does the anode still need a heat-capacity rating?
A. Almost all of the electron energy is deposited as heat, not as x-rays.
B. The formula already subtracts the x-ray energy, so the rating is optional.
C. Heat units measure only the characteristic x-rays.
D. The anode is cooled by the x-ray beam leaving the tube.
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Radiative efficiency at diagnostic kV is under 1% in tungsten, so the kVp × mAs product is essentially a heat load. The rating protects the target and bearings from melting or cracking.

### Q3
Stem: A tube angle is reduced and the filament length is left unchanged. Image sharpness and field coverage tend to change as:
A. Sharper effective focal spot, and less coverage on the anode side because of the heel effect
B. Larger effective focal spot, and the heel effect disappears
C. No geometric change, because sharpness is set only by mA
D. λmin becomes shorter across the anode side of the film
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: A smaller angle reduces the effective focal spot (sharper) and increases anode-side absorption (stronger heel effect and a more limited field). mA does not set the focal-spot geometry. λmin is set by kVp.

### Q4
Stem: Which statement about the vacuum inside the tube envelope is correct?
A. The vacuum lets the electron beam reach the target without ionizing a gas path.
B. The vacuum is the source of the characteristic x-rays.
C. Air is required inside the envelope so that the electrons can scatter onto the anode.
D. The vacuum replaces added filtration.
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: A gas path would collide with the electrons and could arc. Characteristic x-rays come from the target atoms. Added filtration is outside the useful beam path, not a substitute for the vacuum.

### Q5
Stem: The light field on a simulator or a linac is aligned to the radiation field. On a diagnostic tube, the corresponding idea is that the collimator light must match the x-ray field. A mismatch causes:
A. Anode heat to fall by half
B. Anatomy at the edge of the light field to be cut off, or tissue outside the light field to be irradiated
C. λmin to change
D. The mass attenuation coefficient of the patient to change
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: The light is only a geometric stand-in for the x-ray aperture. If the two diverge, the image or the treatment portal will not match the skin marks. It does not change the spectrum limit or the patient’s μ/ρ.

## Production of X-Rays Quiz

### Q1
Stem: A 90 kVp beam has a maximum photon energy of:
A. 90 keV
B. 90 MeV
C. 30 keV, because the average energy is the maximum
D. 511 keV
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Peak kilovoltage sets the maximum photon energy at 90 keV. Calling it 90 MeV is a thousand-fold unit error. About 30 keV would be a rough average for a lightly filtered beam, not the maximum. 511 keV is annihilation radiation.

### Q2
Stem: Characteristic peaks sit on top of the bremsstrahlung continuum. They appear only if:
A. The kVp is above the binding energy of the shell being ionized
B. The mA is above 100
C. Added filtration is zero
D. The target Z is less than 10
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: A shell cannot be ionized if the electron energy is below that shell’s binding energy, so no characteristic photons from that shell are produced. mA scales the height of peaks that already exist. Filtration can reduce their height but does not set the threshold. High Z, not Z below 10, is what makes K characteristic energies clinically visible.

### Q3
Stem: Increasing kVp from 80 to 120, with mAs fixed, does all of the following except:
A. Decrease λmin
B. Increase the maximum photon energy
C. Increase the number of bremsstrahlung photons
D. Leave the quantity and the quality unchanged
Answer: D
Objective: I.2
Why: Higher kVp shortens λmin (λmin = 12.4/kVp), raises the maximum energy, and increases output roughly with the square of kVp. Quantity and quality both change. Option D is the change that does not happen.

### Q4
Stem: Narrow-beam transmission through 3 half-value layers is 12.5%. Through one additional HVL the transmission becomes:
A. 25%
B. 6.25%
C. 12.5%
D. 3.1%
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: One more HVL halves the transmission again: 12.5% / 2 = 6.25%. 25% goes backward one HVL. 3.1% would be about two extra HVLs (12.5 / 4). The transmission does not stay at 12.5% when another HVL is added.

### Q5
Stem: Which statement about the dominant photon interaction is correct?
A. In soft tissue at 6 MV, Compton scatter accounts for most interactions. In bone near 30 keV, the photoelectric effect is far more important than it is in soft tissue.
B. Pair production is the main interaction in soft tissue at 6 MV.
C. Coherent scatter accounts for most of the absorbed dose at 6 MV.
D. The photoelectric effect is the only interaction that can occur below 1 MeV.
Answer: A
Objective: I.4
Why: Compton scatter dominates megavoltage interactions in soft tissue. At low diagnostic energy the photoelectric effect rises steeply with Z, which is why bone and iodine contrast so strongly while soft tissue at the same kV is much less photoelectric. Pair production is negligible in tissue at 6 MV. Coherent scatter deposits essentially no energy. Photoelectric absorption is not the only interaction below 1 MeV; Compton scatter is already important.

### Q20
Stem: An orthovoltage unit delivers 80 cGy on a 1.00 min setting and 180 cGy on a 2.00 min setting. Dose is proportional to (set time + δ). The setting that delivers a true 1.00 min of beam is:
A. 0.80 min
B. 1.00 min
C. 1.20 min
D. 1.40 min
Answer: C
Objective: I.2
Why: 80 = R(1+δ) and 180 = R(2+δ), so 180/80 = 2.25 = (2+δ)/(1+δ). Then 2.25 + 2.25δ = 2 + δ, so 0.25 = −1.25δ and δ = −0.20 min. A true 1.00 min of beam needs set time + δ = 1.00, so the setting is 1.20 min. Check: R = 80/0.80 = 100 cGy/min, and 1.20 − 0.20 = 1.00 min. 0.80 min is the beam-on time of the 1.00 setting, not the setting that gives a full minute. 1.40 min overcorrects.
