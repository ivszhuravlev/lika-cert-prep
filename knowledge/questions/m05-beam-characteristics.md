# Module 5 — Characteristics of X-Rays and Particle Beams

Original items. Radiation weighting factors follow ICRP 103: photons and electrons 1, protons 2, alpha particles 20, neutrons energy-dependent.

## Electromagnetic Radiation vs. Particulate Radiation

### Q1
Stem: Which list contains only electromagnetic radiation?
A. Photons, electrons, and protons
B. X-rays, gamma rays, and annihilation photons
C. Neutrons, alphas, and photons
D. Beta particles and characteristic x-rays
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: X-rays, gamma rays, and the 511 keV annihilation photons are all uncharged electromagnetic radiation. Electrons, protons, betas, alphas, and neutrons are particulate. Characteristic x-rays are electromagnetic, but a beta particle in the same list is not.

### Q2
Stem: A clinical proton beam and a clinical photon beam of similar range in the patient differ most clearly in that the protons:
A. Are attenuated exponentially with no finite range
B. Deposit dose with a Bragg peak near the end of a finite range
C. Have wR = 1, the same protection factor as photons
D. Produce no ionization
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Heavy charged particles have a finite range and a Bragg peak. Photons are attenuated exponentially and have no finite range. ICRP 103 assigns protons a radiation weighting factor of 2, not 1. Protons ionize directly.

### Q3
Stem: Stopping power describes:
A. The exponential attenuation of a photon beam
B. The energy lost by a charged particle per unit path length
C. The Hounsfield unit of cortical bone
D. The decay constant of Ir-192
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Mass stopping power is the quantity used for electrons, protons, and other charged particles (collision plus radiative loss). Photon beams are described by attenuation coefficients, not stopping power. HU and λ are unrelated.

### Q4
Stem: Electrons lose energy in tissue by collision and by bremsstrahlung. The radiative share becomes more important when:
A. The electron energy is higher and the medium Z is higher
B. The electron energy is only a few keV in water
C. The particles are neutrons
D. The beam is a kV x-ray beam with no electrons in it
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: Radiative yield grows with electron energy and with Z. That is why high-energy electrons on a high-Z target make x-rays, and why high-energy electron beams in tissue carry a photon contamination component. Thermal-range electrons in water lose energy almost entirely by collision. Neutrons are not charged and are not described by electron radiative yield.

### Q5
Stem: Gamma rays from Co-60 and bremsstrahlung x-rays from a 6 MV linac are both used as treatment beams. After they are produced, the difference that still matters clinically is:
A. Only the gamma rays can undergo Compton scatter.
B. They are both photons. The practical differences are the energy spectrum, the source size, and the penumbra, not the interaction types available to them.
C. X-rays have mass and gamma rays do not.
D. Gamma rays follow a Bragg peak and x-rays do not.
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Once emitted, both are photons and both can undergo photoelectric, Compton, and, if the energy allows, pair interactions. Co-60 is approximately monoenergetic at 1.17 and 1.33 MeV and has a physical source size. A 6 MV beam is a bremsstrahlung spectrum from a small target. Neither has a Bragg peak, and neither photon has mass.

### Q6
Stem: A small sealed source is labeled isotropic for an in-air estimate. What does that label require of the emission?
A. Equal intensity in every direction
B. All of the intensity aimed along a single axis
C. Intensity only in the forward hemisphere
D. A finite particle range and no sideways emission
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: An isotropic source has equal intensity in all directions. Output confined to one axis, or to the forward hemisphere only, is a directional pattern. A finite range describes how far a charged particle travels, not whether the source emits equally around itself.

### Q7
Stem: Four beams are compared at the skin: 4 MeV electrons, 6 MV photons, 10 MV photons, and 16 MeV electrons. Which beam has the highest percent surface dose?
A. 16 MeV electrons, because electron surface dose rises with energy and the photon beams are still in buildup
B. 10 MV photons, because surface dose rises as photon energy rises
C. 4 MeV electrons, because electron surface dose is highest at the lowest energy
D. 6 MV photons, because buildup places the maximum dose on the skin
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: On a clinical electron beam the percent surface dose increases as the energy increases, so 16 MeV is higher at the skin than 4 MeV. Megavoltage photon beams have not reached dmax at the surface. The 6 MV and 10 MV beams are still in buildup, and their percent surface dose is lower than either electron beam.

### Q8
Stem: For a clinical electron beam, how are the surface most-probable energy and the mean energy at depth related to range?
A. The most probable energy at the surface comes from the practical range, and the mean energy falls about linearly with depth
B. The most probable energy at the surface comes from a quadratic in depth, and the mean energy falls as depth squared
C. Both energies remain equal to the nominal energy at every depth
D. The mean energy falls linearly, but the most probable energy at the surface does not depend on range
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: The practical range determines the most probable electron energy at the surface. Farther into the phantom, the mean energy decreases about in proportion to depth, a linear fall, not a quadratic one. The nominal energy is not still the energy at every depth.

### Q9
Stem: What happens to percent depth dose when megavoltage electron energy or megavoltage photon energy is raised, and which depth-dose features belong only to electrons?
A. Percent depth dose increases with energy for both. A finite range and a high surface dose are electron features
B. Percent depth dose increases only for photons. Electron percent depth dose falls as energy rises
C. Both beams gain a finite practical range and a surface dose near the full prescription
D. Both beams spare the surface the same way, and neither has a finite range
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: A higher energy increases percent depth dose for megavoltage electrons and for photons. Electrons still stop at a practical range and enter with a high surface dose. Photon beams are not stopped at a practical range, and a clean megavoltage photon beam is still building up at the skin.

## X-Ray Spectrum

### Q1
Stem: An unfiltered bremsstrahlung spectrum and the same spectrum after several millimeters of aluminum are compared. Filtration:
A. Removes low-energy photons and raises the average energy
B. Lowers λmin
C. Moves the characteristic peaks to higher energy
D. Increases the number of photons at every energy
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Filters harden the beam by taking out the soft photons, so average energy rises and fluence falls. λmin is fixed by the kVp. Characteristic energies are fixed by the target atom. Filtration does not add photons.

### Q2
Stem: Beam quality for a superficial x-ray unit is specified by:
A. HVL in mm Al, or mm Cu for a harder orthovoltage beam
B. TPR 20/10
C. The flattening-filter material
D. The proton range in water
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: kV beams are specified by kVp and HVL (mm Al for superficial energies, mm Cu once the beam is in the orthovoltage range). TPR 20/10 and percent depth dose at 10 cm specify megavoltage photon beams. Flattening filters and proton range belong to other machines.

### Q3
Stem: The homogeneity coefficient is the first HVL divided by the second HVL. A value close to 1 means:
A. The beam is nearly monoenergetic, because the second HVL is almost equal to the first
B. The beam is unfiltered and very soft
C. Pair production has become the only interaction
D. The tube current is unstable
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: In a heterogeneous beam the first HVL removes the softest photons, so the second HVL is thicker and the ratio is less than 1. Heavy filtration brings the two HVLs together. The ratio is not a measure of mA stability, and it is not a pair-production flag.

### Q4
Stem: On a typical diagnostic spectrum plot, characteristic radiation appears as:
A. A discrete peak or peaks sitting on the continuous bremsstrahlung curve
B. A second λmin at twice the kVp
C. A Bragg peak at the end of the electron range
D. A flat line from zero energy up to the kVp
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Bremsstrahlung is the continuous curve ending at λmin. Characteristic photons are discrete energies and show up as peaks if the kVp is above the relevant binding energy. A Bragg peak is not part of an x-ray spectrum.

### Q5
Stem: Two beams have the same kVp. Beam A has a larger HVL than beam B. Beam A is:
A. Softer
B. Harder
C. Lower in maximum energy
D. A particle beam
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: A larger HVL means more material is required to cut the intensity in half, which is a harder, more penetrating beam. The maximum energy is the same if the kVp is the same. HVL here is still a photon-beam quality index.

## Neutrons 101 and Radiation Weighting Factors

### Q1
Stem: Fast neutrons lose energy most efficiently by elastic scatter with:
A. Lead nuclei, because lead has the highest Z
B. Hydrogen nuclei, because the masses are similar
C. Photons in the room
D. Stationary electrons only, as in Compton scatter
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Elastic scatter transfers the most energy when the target nucleus is close in mass to the neutron. Hydrogen is the best moderator. Lead is a poor moderator and is used afterward for the capture gammas. Neutrons do not lose energy by Compton scatter the way photons do.

### Q2
Stem: Thermal-neutron shielding in a high-energy linac door is built with hydrogenous material plus a boron or lithium absorber because:
A. Boron has a high capture cross section for slow neutrons, and the hydrogen slows the fast neutrons first
B. Boron is a high-Z gamma shield and replaces concrete
C. Thermal neutrons are stopped by lead alone
D. Neutrons are charged and are ranged out like electrons
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: Fast neutrons are moderated in polyethylene or concrete (hydrogen). Boron then captures the thermal neutrons. Lead may be added for the gamma rays from capture, but lead alone is not the neutron shield. Neutrons have no charge, so they do not have an electron-like range.

### Q3
Stem: In ICRP 103, the radiation weighting factor for photons and for electrons is:
A. 1 for both
B. 20 for photons and 1 for electrons
C. 2 for photons and 20 for electrons
D. Energy-dependent for photons, fixed at 20 for electrons
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: Photons and electrons are assigned wR = 1 at all energies. Alpha particles are 20. Protons are 2. The energy-dependent factor is for neutrons, not for photons.

### Q4
Stem: A tissue receives 0.02 Gy from alpha particles only. Using wR = 20, the equivalent dose is:
A. 0.02 Sv
B. 0.40 Sv
C. 0.001 Sv
D. 20 Sv
Answer: B
Objective: III.3
Why: Equivalent dose H = wR × D = 20 × 0.02 Gy = 0.40 Sv. 0.02 Sv uses wR = 1, the photon factor. 0.001 Sv divides by 20. 20 Sv drops the absorbed dose and reports the weighting factor.

### Q5
Stem: Clinical proton dose is prescribed with an RBE of 1.1, while ICRP 103 lists a proton radiation weighting factor of 2. Both numbers can be right because:
A. RBE is a biological effectiveness for a stated endpoint, and wR is a radiation-protection factor. They are not the same quantity.
B. The RBE replaced wR in 2019, so 1.1 is now the legal equivalent-dose factor.
C. wR = 2 applies only to neutrons and RBE = 1.1 applies to photons.
D. One of the two values must be a decimal error.
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: Treatment planning folds a clinical RBE (1.1 for protons) into the reported biologic dose. Equivalent dose for protection uses wR, which is 2 for protons. They answer different questions. Neutron wR is a separate, energy-dependent function, and photon wR is 1.

### Q6
Stem: Above about 10 MV, a linac head produces neutrons mainly by:
A. Photoelectric absorption in the patient
B. Photonuclear reactions in high-Z materials such as the target, the primary collimator, and the flattening filter
C. Pair production in air
D. The ion chamber, which counts neutrons as monitor units
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Photons above the nuclear binding threshold can knock neutrons out of high-Z head components. That neutron fluence is a shielding and implanted-device issue. It is not the photoelectric effect, not pair production in air, and not what the monitor chamber is calibrated to count.

## Characteristics of X-Ray and Particle Beams Mini-Quiz

### Q1
Stem: Which beam has a finite practical range set by its initial energy, rather than an exponential attenuation?
A. 6 MV photons
B. 16 MeV electrons
C. Cs-137 gamma rays
D. A superficial 100 kVp x-ray beam
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Electrons are charged and stop after a practical range of roughly half the energy in centimeters of water. Photon beams, including megavoltage, Cs-137, and kV x-rays, are attenuated exponentially and do not have a practical range of that kind.

### Q2
Stem: Linear energy transfer is highest for which of these radiations?
A. 6 MV photons
B. 15 MeV electrons
C. Alpha particles
D. 100 kVp x-rays
Answer: C
Objective: III.3
Why: Alphas are slow, heavy, and doubly charged, so ionization is dense and LET is high. Photons, kV x-rays, and clinical electrons are low LET. That is why alpha wR is 20 and photon and electron wR are 1.

### Q3
Stem: A neutron of about 1 MeV has a radiation weighting factor on the order of 20, while a 6 MV photon has wR = 1. For the same absorbed dose, the neutron equivalent dose is:
A. Much larger
B. The same
C. Smaller by a factor of 20
D. Zero, because neutrons have no charge
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: H = wR D, so a weighting factor near 20 multiplies the equivalent dose by about 20 relative to photons. Uncharged does not mean biologically irrelevant. Neutrons act through recoil protons and nuclear interactions.

### Q4
Stem: The first HVL of a superficial beam is 1.0 mm Al and the second HVL is 1.6 mm Al. The homogeneity coefficient is:
A. 1.6
B. 0.63
C. 2.6
D. 0.6 mm Al
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Homogeneity coefficient = first HVL / second HVL = 1.0 / 1.6 = 0.625, or 0.63. 1.6 inverts the ratio. 2.6 adds the HVLs. The coefficient is a ratio, not a thickness in mm Al. A value this far below 1 means the beam is still heterogeneous.

### Q5
Stem: Which statement about attenuation versus stopping power is correct?
A. Both words describe the exponential loss of photon fluence.
B. Attenuation describes photon removal. Stopping power describes charged-particle energy loss per path length.
C. Stopping power is used for neutrons because they have charge.
D. Attenuation applies only to alpha particles.
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Photon beams are characterized by μ, μ/ρ, and HVL. Charged-particle beams are characterized by stopping power and range. Neutrons are uncharged. Alphas are not described by a photon attenuation coefficient as their primary specification.
