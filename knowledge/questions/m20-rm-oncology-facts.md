# Module 20. Site-Specific Clinical Oncology

Original items. Clinical oncology facts added from the RM Review gap list: staging shorthand, metastatic routes, head and neck subsites, lymphoma, thoracic and upper GI sites, sarcoma, skin, and CNS histologies.

## Staging and Spread Primer

### Q1
Stem: A pathology report reads pTis N0 M0. What does the Tis category say about the primary tumor?
A. The primary tumor could not be assessed
B. The tumor is carcinoma in situ and has not crossed the basement membrane
C. No primary tumor was found
D. The tumor is smaller than 1 cm but invasive
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Tis is carcinoma in situ, a malignant change confined above the basement membrane with no invasion. TX is the category for a primary that cannot be assessed, and T0 means no evidence of a primary. Size alone does not define Tis; a small invasive tumor is T1, not Tis.

### Q2
Stem: After preoperative chemoradiation and surgery, a rectal cancer is reported as ypT0 N0. What does the prefix y indicate?
A. The stage was estimated clinically before any treatment
B. The stage describes a recurrence after a disease-free interval
C. The stage was assigned at autopsy
D. The stage was assigned after neoadjuvant therapy, so no residual tumor was found in the specimen
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: The y prefix marks a stage assigned after neoadjuvant therapy, and ypT0 N0 describes a pathological complete response with no tumor left in the resected rectum or nodes. The clinical pretreatment stage carries a c prefix, recurrence carries an r prefix, and autopsy staging carries an a prefix. Neoadjuvant means the therapy came before the definitive operation.

### Q3
Stem: A study guide states that N3 always means contralateral nodes. Why is that rule unreliable on the exam?
A. N3 is never used in the AJCC system
B. N categories count only the number of nodes, never their location
C. N definitions are site-specific; for the head and neck N3 is a node larger than 6 cm or gross extranodal extension, while for the colon it does not exist
D. N3 means distant metastasis in every site
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: Nodal categories are defined separately for each site. Head and neck N3 is defined by node size above 6 cm or gross extranodal extension, breast N categories use axillary, internal mammary, and supraclavicular location and node number, and colon uses N1 and N2 by node count with no N3. Distant disease is the M category, not N3.

### Q4
Stem: A patient with colon cancer develops distant disease. Which organ is the usual first site, and why?
A. Brain, because the colon drains to the vertebral venous plexus
B. Liver, because the colon drains through the portal vein
C. Bone, because colon cancer produces osteoblastic lesions
D. Adrenal gland, because the colon shares arterial supply with the adrenals
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Venous blood from the colon returns through the portal vein to the liver, so the liver is the first filter and the most common site of colorectal metastasis. A low rectal cancer can also reach the lung through systemic veins. Bone is the prostate pattern, and brain and adrenal metastases are typical of lung cancer.

### Q5
Stem: Prostate cancer that has spread beyond the pelvis most often appears where, and with what radiographic character?
A. Lung, as cannonball nodules
B. Liver, as hypodense masses
C. Brain, as ring-enhancing lesions
D. Bone, as osteoblastic lesions of the spine and pelvis
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: Prostate cancer spreads through the vertebral venous plexus to the axial skeleton, where it forms dense osteoblastic lesions. Cannonball lung nodules are classic for renal cell carcinoma and sarcoma, liver spread is the colorectal pattern, and brain metastases are typical of lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma.

### Q6
Stem: A high-grade soft tissue sarcoma of the thigh is staged for distant disease. Which site needs imaging, and why are regional nodes not the main concern?
A. The lung, because sarcoma spreads through the blood and rarely to nodes
B. The liver, because the thigh drains through the portal system
C. The inguinal nodes, because sarcoma spreads mainly by lymph
D. The brain, because sarcoma seeds the CSF
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: Soft tissue sarcomas spread hematogenously, and the lung is the dominant site of distant failure, so a chest CT is part of staging. Nodal spread is rare except for a few histologies such as epithelioid, synovial, clear cell, angiosarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma. The thigh does not drain to the portal vein and sarcoma does not seed the CSF.

### Q7
Stem: A left testicular seminoma is staged. Where are the first lymph nodes it reaches?
A. Left inguinal nodes below the inguinal ligament
B. Left external iliac nodes
C. Left para-aortic nodes at the level of the renal hilum
D. Bilateral supraclavicular nodes
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: The testis descended from the posterior abdominal wall and keeps its lymphatic drainage along the gonadal vessels to the para-aortic and paracaval nodes near the renal vessels. Inguinal nodes are involved only if the scrotal skin has been violated by prior surgery or tumor. Iliac nodes are involved later, and supraclavicular nodes are a distant sign, not the first station.

### Q8
Stem: Why do primary brain tumors have no TNM stage?
A. They are graded by the WHO classification instead, because they almost never spread to nodes or distant organs
B. The brain has no T category because tumor size cannot be measured
C. They are staged by the FIGO system
D. They are staged by Ann Arbor like lymphomas
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: Primary brain tumors are classified by WHO grade and molecular markers rather than TNM, because nodal and distant spread are so rare that N and M categories are meaningless. FIGO is the gynecologic staging system and Ann Arbor is the lymphoma system. Tumor size is measured on MRI but does not define a T category for gliomas.

## Head and Neck Subsites and Prognosis

### Q1
Stem: Ohngren's line is used in planning for a maxillary sinus cancer. How is the line drawn and what does it predict?
A. From the nasion to the mastoid tip, dividing tumors that need neck dissection from those that do not
B. From the tragus to the oral commissure, marking the course of the parotid duct
C. From the medial canthus to the angle of the mandible, with tumors above and behind it carrying a worse prognosis
D. From the hyoid to the sternal notch, marking the superior border of level VI
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: Ohngren's line runs from the medial canthus of the eye to the angle of the mandible and divides the maxillary sinus into an anteroinferior infrastructure and a posterosuperior suprastructure. Tumors in the suprastructure grow toward the orbit, pterygoid plates, and skull base, are harder to resect, and have a worse prognosis. The tragus to commissure line is the parotid duct landmark, and the other lines are not named oncologic landmarks.

### Q2
Stem: A tumor is described as arising from the retromolar trigone. Where is that structure and what is its special risk?
A. The mucosa behind the last lower molar over the anterior ramus, with early invasion of the mandible
B. The posterior pharyngeal wall behind the soft palate, with early cord involvement
C. The lateral floor of mouth under the tongue, with early invasion of the hyoid
D. The pyriform sinus beside the larynx, with early invasion of the thyroid cartilage
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: The retromolar trigone is the triangle of oral cavity mucosa behind the last mandibular molar, covering the anterior edge of the mandibular ramus up to the maxillary tuberosity. Because the mucosa rests directly on bone, tumors invade the mandible early and spread to the tonsillar pillar and buccal mucosa. The pyriform sinus is a hypopharyngeal subsite and the floor of mouth sits over the mylohyoid, not the hyoid.

### Q3
Stem: A squamous cell carcinoma of the anterior floor of mouth crosses the midline. Which nodal levels does the elective neck volume cover?
A. Ipsilateral levels II through IV only
B. Bilateral levels I through III
C. Ipsilateral level V and the retropharyngeal nodes
D. Bilateral level VI
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: The floor of mouth drains first to the submental and submandibular nodes in level I and then to levels II and III, and a lesion at or across the midline drains to both sides. Level V and the retropharyngeal nodes are not first-echelon stations for the oral cavity. Level VI is the anterior compartment used for larynx, hypopharynx, and thyroid.

### Q4
Stem: Two oropharyngeal cancers have the same tumor size and the same ipsilateral nodes. One is p16 positive. How does current staging treat the p16-positive case?
A. It is staged with the same tables and has the same prognosis
B. It is upstaged one level because HPV marks aggressive disease
C. It is not staged at all, because HPV disease is treated as benign
D. It uses a separate HPV staging table and falls in a lower stage group with a better prognosis
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: The eighth edition of AJCC gives p16-positive oropharyngeal cancer its own staging system because its outcome is much better than HPV-negative disease of the same anatomic extent. The same nodal burden therefore lands in a lower stage group. The disease is still a cancer treated to about 70 Gy with concurrent chemotherapy; de-escalation remains a trial question.

### Q5
Stem: Radiosurgery is planned for trigeminal neuralgia with a 4 mm collimator. Which maximum dose is typical?
A. 12 to 16 Gy, the same as a brain metastasis
B. 70 to 90 Gy to the nerve root in a single fraction
C. 54 Gy in 30 fractions
D. 8 Gy in one fraction, as for bone pain
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Trigeminal neuralgia radiosurgery delivers a maximum dose of about 70 to 90 Gy to a few millimeters of the trigeminal root with a small collimator, because the intent is to injure a nerve rather than to control a tumor. A 12 to 16 Gy margin dose is a metastasis or small meningioma dose, 54 Gy in 30 fractions is a fractionated benign tumor course, and 8 Gy once is bone palliation.

### Q6
Stem: A parotid cancer has been resected. Which finding most clearly adds postoperative radiotherapy to the plan?
A. A low-grade tumor smaller than 2 cm with clear margins
B. A benign pleomorphic adenoma removed intact
C. A negative sentinel node and no perineural invasion
D. High-grade histology with perineural invasion or facial nerve involvement
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: Surgery is the primary treatment for parotid cancer, and postoperative radiotherapy is added for high-grade histology, close or positive margins, perineural invasion, facial nerve involvement, extraglandular extension, or positive nodes. A small low-grade tumor with clear margins is observed after surgery. A pleomorphic adenoma is benign and is not irradiated after a complete excision.

## Lymphoma, Thoracic, and Upper GI Site Facts

### Q1
Stem: A young adult has a mediastinal mass and a node biopsy shows large binucleated cells with prominent nucleoli among reactive lymphocytes. Which diagnosis and subtype fit best?
A. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma, nodular sclerosis subtype
B. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma, lymphocyte-depleted subtype
C. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
D. Small cell lung cancer
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: The binucleated cell is the Reed-Sternberg cell that defines classical Hodgkin lymphoma, and nodular sclerosis is the most common subtype, typical of young adults with a mediastinal mass. Lymphocyte-depleted disease is the rarest subtype and tends to appear in older patients with advanced disease. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma lacks Reed-Sternberg cells, and small cell carcinoma is an epithelial tumor of the lung.

### Q2
Stem: A lymphoma patient's stage is written IIB. Which finding earned the letter B?
A. Pruritus
B. Fatigue
C. Drenching night sweats, fever above 38 degrees Celsius, or loss of more than 10 percent of body weight in six months
D. A mediastinal mass wider than one third of the chest
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: The B symptoms are fever above 38 degrees Celsius, drenching night sweats, and unexplained weight loss above 10 percent in six months; any one of them adds the letter B to the Ann Arbor stage. Pruritus and fatigue are common but are not B symptoms. Bulky mediastinal disease is noted separately as bulk, often with the letter X, and affects the treatment group rather than the B designation.

### Q3
Stem: A patient with a right upper lobe lung mass develops facial swelling, distended neck veins, and headache that worsens when bending forward. What is the diagnosis and the modern first step in a stable patient?
A. Pancoast syndrome; urgent brachial plexus decompression
B. Superior vena cava syndrome; obtain a tissue diagnosis because small cell carcinoma and lymphoma respond to chemotherapy, and stent if relief must be immediate
C. Spinal cord compression; start dexamethasone and irradiate the spine
D. Tumor lysis syndrome; start fluids and allopurinol
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Compression of the superior vena cava by a right-sided mediastinal mass produces facial and arm swelling, venous distension, plethora, and positional headache. It is a classic oncologic emergency and radiotherapy is a standard urgent treatment, but a stable patient first gets a tissue diagnosis because small cell lung cancer and lymphoma are chemosensitive, and an endovascular stent gives the fastest relief. Pancoast syndrome involves the apex, the brachial plexus, and Horner's syndrome, not venous obstruction.

### Q4
Stem: Which pairing of esophageal location and histology is correct?
A. Upper third, adenocarcinoma from Barrett's esophagus
B. Middle third, small cell carcinoma as the dominant histology
C. Distal third, squamous cell carcinoma from tobacco and alcohol
D. Distal third and gastroesophageal junction, adenocarcinoma arising in Barrett's esophagus
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: Adenocarcinoma arises in the distal esophagus and the gastroesophageal junction from Barrett's esophagus, the intestinal metaplasia caused by chronic reflux, and it is now the most common esophageal cancer in the United States. Squamous cell carcinoma favors the upper and middle thirds and is linked to tobacco and alcohol. Small cell carcinoma of the esophagus is rare.

### Q5
Stem: A pancreatic head tumor is scheduled for a Whipple procedure. Which structures are removed, and which vessel relation decides whether the tumor is resectable?
A. The pancreatic head, duodenum, gallbladder, and distal bile duct; involvement of the superior mesenteric artery behind the uncinate process
B. The pancreatic tail and spleen; involvement of the splenic vein
C. The whole pancreas and the stomach; involvement of the celiac trunk only
D. The gallbladder alone; involvement of the cystic duct
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: The Whipple pancreaticoduodenectomy removes the pancreatic head, the duodenum, the gallbladder, the distal common bile duct, and often the distal stomach. The uncinate process hooks behind the superior mesenteric vessels, and encasement of the superior mesenteric artery is what makes a head tumor borderline or unresectable. Distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy is the operation for body and tail tumors.

### Q6
Stem: A stage IIA right-sided seminoma is treated with a hockey-stick field. What does that field cover?
A. The para-aortic nodes only, from T11 to L5
B. Both groins and the scrotum
C. The para-aortic nodes from T11 to the bifurcation, then the right common and external iliac nodes down to the top of the acetabulum
D. The whole abdomen and pelvis to 36 Gy
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: The hockey-stick, or dog-leg, field is a para-aortic strip from the top of T11 to the aortic bifurcation that bends along the ipsilateral common and external iliac nodes to the upper acetabulum. It is used for stage II disease with retroperitoneal nodes, with about 20 to 25 Gy to the field and a boost to a total near 30 Gy for IIA or 36 Gy for IIB. A para-aortic strip alone is the stage I option, groins are not in a seminoma volume unless the scrotum was violated, and whole-abdomen treatment is not used.

## Sarcoma, Skin, and CNS Site Facts

### Q1
Stem: A 14-year-old has a small round blue cell tumor in the diaphysis of the femur. Which genetic finding confirms Ewing sarcoma?
A. BRCA1 mutation
B. The t(11;22) translocation producing the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion
C. Loss of p53 only
D. The Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22)
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Ewing sarcoma carries the t(11;22) translocation that fuses EWSR1 with FLI1, and the tumor favors the diaphysis of long bones and the pelvis in the second decade of life. It is radiosensitive and chemosensitive, with definitive radiotherapy near 55.8 Gy when surgery is not possible. The Philadelphia chromosome is chronic myeloid leukemia, and BRCA1 is hereditary breast and ovarian cancer.

### Q2
Stem: Which statement correctly contrasts osteosarcoma with multiple myeloma?
A. Osteosarcoma is radiosensitive and myeloma is radioresistant
B. Both arise in the diaphysis of long bones in children
C. Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone malignancy in adults and myeloma in children
D. Osteosarcoma peaks in teenagers in the metaphysis around the knee and is radioresistant, while myeloma is the most common primary bone malignancy in adults and responds to palliative doses
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone malignancy of children and adolescents, arises in the metaphysis of the distal femur and proximal tibia, and is treated with chemotherapy and surgery because it is relatively radioresistant. Multiple myeloma, a plasma cell malignancy, is the most common primary bone malignancy of adults, produces lytic punched-out lesions, and is palliated with 8 Gy once to 30 Gy in 10 fractions. Ewing sarcoma, not osteosarcoma, is the diaphyseal tumor.

### Q3
Stem: After a lymph node dissection for melanoma, which finding is an indication for adjuvant radiotherapy to the nodal basin?
A. Extranodal extension or several large involved nodes
B. A single microscopic sentinel node with no other disease
C. A thin primary with a Breslow depth under 1 mm
D. A negative sentinel node biopsy
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: Adjuvant nodal radiotherapy for melanoma is reserved for high regional recurrence risk: extranodal extension, several involved nodes, a node larger than about 3 to 4 cm, or parotid node involvement, and it reduces regional recurrence without improving survival. A single microscopic sentinel node, a thin primary, or a negative sentinel node does not meet that threshold. When melanoma is irradiated the course is often hypofractionated because of the broad shoulder on its survival curve.

### Q4
Stem: A child has a fourth-ventricle ependymoma resected. When is craniospinal irradiation added instead of focal tumor-bed radiotherapy?
A. Always, because ependymoma seeds the whole axis like medulloblastoma
B. Never, because ependymoma is treated with chemotherapy alone
C. Only when spinal MRI or CSF cytology shows dissemination
D. Only when the patient is older than 18 years
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: Ependymoma is treated with maximal safe resection and focal conformal radiotherapy to the tumor bed, about 54 to 59.4 Gy. Craniospinal irradiation is reserved for documented dissemination on spinal MRI or CSF cytology. Medulloblastoma is the posterior fossa tumor that always receives craniospinal irradiation, and age does not decide the volume.

### Q5
Stem: Which cancer is the most common in the United States when all sites are counted?
A. Lung cancer
B. Basal cell carcinoma of the skin
C. Prostate cancer
D. Colorectal cancer
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Skin cancer is the most common cancer overall and basal cell carcinoma is the single most common type; it almost never metastasizes. Excluding skin, breast in women and prostate in men lead in incidence, lung is second, and colorectal is third. Lung cancer leads in cancer deaths, not in incidence.
