# Module 4. Production of X-rays

Original items. Generator rectification and ripple, the direction of each spectral change with added filtration, mean energy of a bremsstrahlung beam, and production efficiency at kV versus MV.

## Generator Rectification and What Filtration Changes

### Q1
Stem: Which generator type produces the smallest voltage ripple?
A. Single-phase full-wave rectified
B. Three-phase six-pulse
C. High-frequency inverter
D. Single-phase half-wave rectified
Answer: C
Objective: I.2
Why: A high-frequency generator rectifies and smooths a kilohertz waveform and delivers a nearly constant potential with ripple under about 2%. Three-phase six-pulse ripple is about 13% and twelve-pulse about 4%. Both single-phase schemes drop to zero between pulses, so their ripple is 100%; full-wave rectification only doubles the pulse rate from 60 to 120 per second.

### Q2
Stem: Two units are set to the same kVp and mAs. Unit X has a single-phase full-wave generator and unit Y a three-phase twelve-pulse generator. Compared with X, the beam from Y has:
A. A higher mean energy and a higher output, because the potential stays closer to the peak
B. The same mean energy, because kVp alone sets the spectrum
C. A lower mean energy, because the exposure is spread over more pulses
D. A higher maximum energy, because twelve pulses add their peaks
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Less ripple keeps the tube potential near the set kVp for more of the exposure, so more of the photons are produced near the peak: mean energy, output and HVL all rise for the same nominal setting. The kVp sets the maximum energy, which is the same for both units, so option D is wrong. The spectrum below the maximum depends on the waveform, which is why option B is wrong, and more pulses raise rather than lower the mean energy.

### Q3
Stem: Two millimeters of aluminum are added to a 100 kVp tungsten tube. Which quantity stays the same?
A. Half-value layer
B. Energy of the K-alpha characteristic line
C. Entrance skin dose for the same mAs
D. Subject contrast
Answer: B
Objective: I.4
Why: Characteristic line energies are fixed by the tungsten shell binding energies (K-alpha about 59 keV), and the maximum energy is fixed by the kVp; filtration only lowers the heights of all parts of the spectrum, removing proportionally more soft photons. The HVL therefore rises, the entrance skin dose falls because fewer soft photons are absorbed in the first centimeters, and subject contrast falls because a harder beam is less differentially attenuated.

### Q4
Stem: For a lightly filtered diagnostic bremsstrahlung beam the mean photon energy is closest to:
A. Equal to the kVp, because the endpoint sets the beam quality
B. About two-thirds of the kVp
C. About one-third of the kVp
D. About one-tenth of the kVp
Answer: C
Objective: I.2
Why: Electrons lose any fraction of their energy in a bremsstrahlung event, so the spectrum is a continuum from near zero to the kVp and, after inherent filtration, peaks and averages around a third of the endpoint; heavier filtration pushes the mean toward one-half. The kVp is the maximum, not the average. Two-thirds would need very heavy filtration. One-tenth is below the energies that typical filtration has already removed.

### Q5
Stem: Approximately what fraction of the electron beam energy becomes x-rays at a 100 kVp tungsten target and at a 6 MV linac target, respectively?
A. About 1% and about 1%, because efficiency depends only on the target material
B. About 1% and about 30 to 50%
C. About 50% and about 1%, because high-energy electrons mostly scatter
D. About 10% and about 90%
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: Radiative efficiency scales with Z and with electron energy; in tungsten at 100 kV it is under 1%, which is why the anode must rotate and carry a heat rating, while at 6 MV it is a few tens of percent and rises further at higher MV, so the thin linac target is simply water-cooled. Option A ignores the energy dependence. Option C reverses it. Option D overstates both ends.
