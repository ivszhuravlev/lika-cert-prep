# Module 21. Radiobiology

Original items. Classical radiobiology added from the RM Review gap list: radiosensitivity laws, cell biology, whole-body and fetal effects, therapeutic ratio, and altered fractionation.

## Radiosensitivity and Cell Biology

### Q1
Stem: According to the law of Bergonié and Tribondeau, which cell population is the most radiosensitive?
A. Mature neurons, because they never divide
B. Differentiated muscle fibers, because they are large
C. Undifferentiated cells that divide often and have many divisions ahead of them
D. Any cell in G0, because it cannot repair damage
Answer: C
Objective: III.3
Why: The law states that sensitivity rises with mitotic activity, with the length of the dividing future, and with lack of differentiation, which describes stem cells of marrow, gut, skin, and testis. Neurons and mature muscle fit none of the three conditions and are among the most resistant cells. Cells resting in G0 are not automatically sensitive; many are resistant until recruited into the cycle.

### Q2
Stem: Which blood cell is the earliest laboratory indicator of an acute whole-body exposure, and why is it a famous exception to the Bergonié and Tribondeau law?
A. The lymphocyte, because it is highly sensitive even though it is differentiated and does not divide
B. The red cell, because it has the shortest lifespan
C. The platelet, because it is produced fastest
D. The neutrophil, because it is the most undifferentiated cell in blood
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: Mature lymphocytes die in interphase by apoptosis within hours of a few tenths of a gray, so the lymphocyte count falls within one to two days and is the first indicator of dose. That sensitivity contradicts the law because lymphocytes are differentiated and non-dividing. Red cells live about 120 days and fall late, platelets and neutrophils fall over the following weeks as their marrow precursors are lost.

### Q3
Stem: A tissue has a high mitotic index. What does that predict about its radiation response?
A. It will show injury only years after treatment
B. It has a low α/β and is spared strongly by small fractions
C. It is radioresistant because it repairs between divisions
D. It will express injury within days to weeks and behaves as an early-responding tissue
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: The mitotic index is the fraction of cells in mitosis, and a high value means rapid turnover. Tissues with rapid turnover such as mucosa, skin, marrow, and gut express radiation injury early and have a high α/β. Late-responding tissues such as cord, kidney, and lung have slow turnover, a low α/β, and show injury months to years later.

### Q4
Stem: In a typical cultured mammalian cell, which phase of the cycle is the shortest and which is the most variable in length?
A. S is the shortest; M is the most variable
B. M is the shortest, about 1 hour; G1 is the most variable
C. G2 is the shortest; S is the most variable
D. G1 is the shortest; G2 is the most variable
Answer: B
Objective: III.3
Why: Mitosis takes about an hour, S phase about 6 to 8 hours, and G2 about 2 to 4 hours, while G1 ranges from an hour or two to many hours and sets the difference in cycle time between cell types. The whole cycle is about 10 to 24 hours. Cells are most sensitive in M and late G2 and most resistant in late S.

### Q5
Stem: For x-rays, roughly what fraction of DNA damage comes from indirect action, and what carries it?
A. About two thirds, carried by free radicals formed from water, chiefly the hydroxyl radical
B. About one tenth, carried by secondary electrons
C. Nearly all, carried by neutrons produced in tissue
D. About one third, carried by heat
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: Low-LET radiation ionizes water far more often than DNA, and the hydroxyl radical diffuses a few nanometers to attack the DNA, accounting for about two thirds of the damage; direct ionization of the DNA accounts for the remaining third. High-LET radiation reverses the balance toward direct action. Heat is negligible and clinical photon beams do not produce significant neutrons in tissue.

### Q6
Stem: Why does the oxygen enhancement ratio fall toward 1 for alpha particles and neutrons?
A. Because high-LET particles cannot penetrate oxygenated tissue
B. Because oxygen is destroyed by the high ionization density
C. Because high-LET damage is mostly direct and does not need oxygen to fix radical damage into permanent lesions
D. Because high-LET particles produce only single-strand breaks
Answer: C
Objective: III.3
Why: Oxygen enhances low-LET killing by reacting with radical-damaged DNA sites and making the damage permanent, the oxygen fixation hypothesis. High-LET radiation produces dense direct damage that is lethal with or without oxygen, so the ratio approaches 1. The particles penetrate oxygenated tissue normally and produce more, not fewer, double-strand breaks.

### Q7
Stem: After a 2 Gy fraction most tumor cells that will die are still alive the next day. How do they eventually die?
A. By immediate necrosis within minutes of exposure
B. By apoptosis in interphase within an hour
C. By being phagocytosed before the next fraction
D. By mitotic death, when a chromosome aberration makes a later division impossible
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: Most irradiated mammalian cells undergo mitotic or reproductive death: they survive the exposure, often divide once or more, then die when a dicentric, ring, or acentric fragment blocks a later mitosis. That is why survival is measured by colony formation and why tumors regress over weeks. Apoptosis within hours is the mode for lymphocytes, germ cells, and some lymphomas, not the bulk of solid tumor cells.

### Q8
Stem: A culture receives 5 cGy and is then given 2 Gy a few hours later; it survives better than a culture given 2 Gy alone. Separately, unirradiated cells next to irradiated cells show chromosome damage. Which two phenomena are described, in that order?
A. Bystander effect, then reoxygenation
B. Adaptive response, then bystander effect
C. Hormesis, then redistribution
D. Repopulation, then adaptive response
Answer: B
Objective: III.3
Why: Reduced sensitivity after a small priming dose is the adaptive response, attributed to induced repair. Damage expressed in unirradiated neighbors through gap junctions and secreted signals is the bystander effect. Both are low-dose laboratory findings that complicate the linear no-threshold model but do not replace it in radiation protection.

### Q9
Stem: A cell line has D0 = 2 Gy. What dose along the exponential part of the survival curve lowers survival by one decade?
A. 2 Gy
B. 0.74 Gy
C. 4.6 Gy
D. 20 Gy
Answer: C
Objective: III.3
Why: D10 = ln 10 × D0 = 2.303 × 2 = 4.6 Gy. D0 itself lowers survival to 37 percent, one natural-log unit, and two decades would need about 9.2 Gy. Dividing D0 by e gives 0.74 Gy, which has no meaning here, and 20 Gy would be ten times D0, about 4.3 decades of kill.

### Q10
Stem: Which DNA lesion is considered the lethal event after irradiation?
A. A single-strand break
B. Base damage
C. A DNA-protein crosslink
D. An unrepaired or misrepaired double-strand break
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: One gray of low-LET radiation produces about a thousand single-strand breaks and about forty double-strand breaks per cell; single-strand breaks and base damage are repaired almost completely using the intact strand as a template. Double-strand breaks that are unrepaired or misrepaired form the dicentrics, rings, and translocations that cause mitotic death or carcinogenesis. Crosslinks occur but are not the dominant lethal lesion.

## Whole-Body and Fetal Effects

### Q1
Stem: What acute whole-body dose kills about half of exposed people within 60 days when no medical care is given?
A. 1 Gy
B. 3 to 4 Gy
C. 10 Gy
D. 0.5 Gy
Answer: B
Objective: III.3
Why: The human LD50/60 without supportive care is about 3 to 4 Gy; with antibiotics, transfusions, and growth factors it rises to about 6 to 7 Gy. Older texts quote an LD50/30 of 3.5 to 4.5 Gy, and the bank uses the 60-day figure because human marrow death comes later than in small animals. One gray produces measurable count depression but little mortality, and 10 Gy is in the gastrointestinal range.

### Q2
Stem: A person receives an acute whole-body dose of 8 Gy. Which acute radiation syndrome dominates, and what is the expected time course?
A. Hematopoietic syndrome with death at 3 to 8 weeks
B. Cerebrovascular syndrome with death within 2 days
C. No syndrome; 8 Gy is below the threshold
D. Gastrointestinal syndrome with denuded crypts and death in about 1 to 2 weeks
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: In the bands this bank uses, the hematopoietic syndrome begins at about 2 to 3 Gy, the gastrointestinal syndrome dominates from about 5 to 12 Gy, and the cerebrovascular syndrome dominates above about 12 Gy. At 8 Gy the crypt stem cells are destroyed, the villi are lost in about a week, and death from fluid loss and sepsis follows in 1 to 2 weeks unless intensive support succeeds. The marrow is also destroyed, but the gut fails first.

### Q3
Stem: An acute whole-body dose above about 12 Gy, and certainly at tens of gray, produces which outcome?
A. Cerebrovascular syndrome with cerebral edema and death in 1 to 2 days
B. Full recovery after a 2-week latent period
C. Delayed marrow failure at 6 weeks as the only effect
D. Only skin erythema
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: Above the gastrointestinal band, vascular damage and cerebral edema cause the cerebrovascular or central nervous system syndrome, with convulsions, coma, and death within about 1 to 2 days. The latent period shrinks and survival shortens as dose rises, so recovery does not occur. Marrow failure at 6 weeks is the hematopoietic time course at much lower doses.

### Q4
Stem: After a suspected radiation accident, why does the triage team record the time from exposure to the first vomiting?
A. Because vomiting occurs only above 20 Gy
B. Because vomiting marks the end of the latent period
C. Because the time to vomiting shortens as dose rises, from hours at 1 to 2 Gy to under an hour at about 6 Gy and above
D. Because vomiting proves the exposure was to the skin only
Answer: C
Objective: III.3
Why: Prodromal nausea and vomiting begin within hours of a whole-body exposure and start sooner the larger the dose, so onset under an hour suggests a dose of about 6 Gy or more. The prodromal stage precedes, not follows, the latent period. A falling lymphocyte count over the next one to two days is the laboratory companion to this clinical clue.

### Q5
Stem: An embryo is irradiated during the first 10 days after conception. What is the expected pattern of effect?
A. Malformations of the limbs and heart
B. All or none: either embryonic death with resorption or normal development
C. Intellectual disability
D. Growth retardation without malformation
Answer: B
Objective: III.3
Why: During preimplantation the few undifferentiated cells are not committed, so radiation either kills the embryo, which is resorbed, or spares it to develop normally. Malformations are the organogenesis effect of days 11 to 42, and intellectual disability is the fetal effect concentrated at weeks 8 to 15. Growth retardation accompanies the later stages, not this one.

### Q6
Stem: Which developmental window carries the greatest risk of radiation-induced organ malformations?
A. Days 1 to 10, preimplantation
B. Weeks 25 to 40, the third trimester
C. Weeks 8 to 15, the fetal period
D. Days 11 to 42, organogenesis
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: Organ systems form between about day 11 and day 42, roughly weeks 2 to 8, and radiation in that window causes malformations and growth retardation with a threshold near 100 to 200 mGy. Preimplantation gives an all or none response, weeks 8 to 15 are the window for intellectual disability rather than structural malformation, and the third trimester carries mainly growth and stochastic risk.

### Q7
Stem: A pregnant patient's estimated fetal dose from a diagnostic study is 40 mGy. How is that dose interpreted against the modern threshold?
A. It is below the 100 mGy threshold, so it is not a justification for terminating the pregnancy
B. It exceeds the threshold for malformations, which is 10 mGy
C. It guarantees intellectual disability
D. It is above the occupational fetal limit, so the fetus has been harmed
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: The deterministic effects on the conceptus have thresholds at or above about 100 mGy, and the current guidance is that a fetal dose below 100 mGy is not grounds for termination; the added childhood cancer risk at 40 mGy is small. The occupational limit of 5 mSv for a declared pregnant worker is a protection rule, not a biological threshold, and exceeding it does not mean the fetus was harmed.

## Therapeutic Ratio and Altered Fractionation

### Q1
Stem: On a plot of tumor control probability and normal tissue complication probability against dose, what does the therapeutic ratio describe?
A. The slope of the tumor control curve alone
B. The dose at which both curves reach 100 percent
C. The separation between the two curves, so that a ratio above 1 means the tumor is controlled before the normal tissue is injured
D. The ratio of physical dose to biologically effective dose
Answer: C
Objective: III.3
Why: The therapeutic ratio compares the dose that produces a given complication probability with the dose that produces the same tumor control probability; the wider the gap, the more favorable the ratio. Fractionation and conformal delivery move the complication curve to the right, and sensitizers move the control curve to the left. It has nothing to do with the physical to biological dose conversion.

### Q2
Stem: Why does hyperfractionation allow a higher total tumor dose without more late toxicity?
A. Because smaller fractions deliver more dose per minute
B. Because late-responding tissues have a low α/β and are spared more by small fractions than tumors with a high α/β are
C. Because twice-daily treatment prevents reoxygenation
D. Because the overall treatment time is doubled
Answer: B
Objective: III.3
Why: Late-responding tissues have a low α/β, so their damage depends strongly on dose per fraction and falls when fractions shrink to 1.1 to 1.2 Gy; tumors with a high α/β are spared much less, so the total dose can be raised. The price is more acute mucositis. Overall time is kept the same, and reoxygenation continues normally.

### Q3
Stem: In a twice-daily hyperfractionated course, what is the minimum interval between the two fractions, and why?
A. 1 hour, to limit repopulation
B. 30 minutes, to keep the patient on the table
C. 24 hours, which makes it once daily
D. At least 6 hours, so late-responding tissues complete sublethal damage repair
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: Sublethal damage repair in late-responding tissues takes several hours, and trials with intervals under 4 to 6 hours showed increased late effects, so protocols require at least 6 hours between fractions. One hour or 30 minutes would leave the second fraction interacting with unrepaired damage from the first. A 24-hour interval is conventional once-daily treatment.

### Q4
Stem: A spinal cord received 40 Gy in 20 fractions and is to be re-irradiated with 16 Gy in 4 fractions. Using α/β = 3 Gy for the cord, what is the cumulative biologically effective dose?
A. 104 Gy
B. 56 Gy
C. 93.3 Gy
D. 128 Gy
Answer: A
Objective: III.3
Why: First course: 40 × (1 + 2/3) = 40 × 1.667 = 66.7 Gy. Second course: 16 × (1 + 4/3) = 16 × 2.333 = 37.3 Gy. Sum 104 Gy, which is an EQD2 of 104 / 1.667 = 62.4 Gy. Adding physical doses gives 56 Gy and ignores fraction size; 93.3 Gy comes from wrongly using 2 Gy per fraction for the second course; 128 Gy is the sum at α/β = 2 Gy, not the 3 Gy the stem gave. The ceiling the physician accepts comes from the re-irradiation literature, not from this arithmetic.
