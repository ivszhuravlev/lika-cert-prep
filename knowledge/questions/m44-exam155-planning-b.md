# Timed Exam Planning B

Original planning items. Not retired MDCB questions and not a commercial quiz.

## Timed Exam Planning B

Score only these limits. A cord maximum fails above 50 Gy. Combined-lung V20 fails above 30%. A parotid mean fails above 25 Gy. Rectum V70 fails when it is not under 20%. A one-fraction cord point dose fails above 14 Gy.

### Q1
Stem: A review sheet lists four DVH readings. Cord maximum 54 Gy. Combined-lung V20 22%. Parotid mean 18 Gy. Rectum V70 11%. Fail a cord maximum above 50 Gy, a lung V20 above 30%, a parotid mean above 25 Gy, or a rectum V70 that is not under 20%. Which organ fails?
A. Combined lung, because any V20 reading is a failure
B. Spinal cord
C. No organ, because 54 Gy is under a 60 Gy habit
D. Rectum and spinal cord together
Answer: B
Objective: III.8
Why: The cord maximum is 54 Gy, and 54 minus 50 is 4 Gy over the cord limit, so the cord fails. Combined-lung V20 is 22%, and 30 minus 22 is 8 percentage points under 30%, so the lung passes. The parotid mean is 18 Gy, and 25 minus 18 is 7 Gy under 25 Gy, so the parotid passes. Rectum V70 is 11%, which is under 20%, so the rectum passes. A V20 symbol on the lung does not fail when the percent is under 30. A 60 Gy habit is not the cord limit. The rectum does not fail with the cord.

### Q15
Stem: A differential DVH of one organ is simplified into three bins. 10 cm³ receive 10 Gy, 20 cm³ receive 40 Gy, and 20 cm³ receive 70 Gy. What is the volume that receives at least 40 Gy?
A. 20 cm³
B. 40 cm³
C. 50 cm³
D. 10 cm³
Answer: B
Objective: III.1
Why: Volume receiving at least 40 Gy adds every bin at 40 Gy and above: 20 cm³ plus 20 cm³ is 40 cm³. The 10 cm³ bin at 10 Gy is below 40 Gy and stays out. 20 cm³ is only the single bin sitting at 40 Gy. 10 plus 20 plus 20 is 50 cm³, the whole organ, which is the volume at the low-dose end of a cumulative curve. 10 cm³ is the bin that does not reach 40 Gy.

### Q17
Stem: The cord contour has a maximum of 49 Gy, under the 50 Gy limit. That contour was drawn one vertebral level away from the cord that actually lies in the high dose. What does this passing DVH fail to prove?

Answer: D
Objective: III.1
Why: A DVH keeps how much of a named contour received how much dose. Lung V20 and proton RBE are different questions from contour identity.

### Q18
Stem: The volume enclosed by the prescription isodose is the same size as the PTV, and those two volumes do not overlap. What does a conformity index of 1 mean for this plan?
A. The volumes match in size, and the PTV is still missed, because equal size does not require overlap
B. The PTV is covered, because an index of 1 is proof of overlap
C. Half the prescription dose covers the PTV, because an index of 1 means R50%
D. Combined-lung V20 is 30%, so the lung correlate is the thing that index measures
Answer: A
Objective: III.6
Why: The conformity index compares the prescription-isodose volume with the PTV volume. Equal size scores 1. A cloud the same size as the PTV, shifted entirely beside it, still scores 1 and misses the target. R50% compares the half-prescription volume with the PTV, which is a different ratio. Lung V20 is the lung volume at or above 20 Gy. In this bank that sparing correlate is at or under 30%, and it is not a conformity index.

### Q22
Stem: Which statement matches the usual teaching definitions of treated volume and irradiated volume?
A. Treated volume is the tissue inside an isodose chosen as adequate for treatment, often illustrated by the 95% isodose. Irradiated volume is tissue that receives a dose considered significant, often illustrated by a lower isodose such as 50%. Neither one is the PTV contour
B. Treated volume is the PTV, and irradiated volume is the GTV
C. Treated volume is combined-lung V20, and irradiated volume is rectum V70
D. Treated volume is the one-fraction cord point maximum of 14 Gy
Answer: A
Objective: III.11
Why: Treated volume is an isodose shell chosen as adequate, and the usual teaching illustration of that shell is the 95% isodose. Irradiated volume is a larger region at a dose considered significant, often illustrated by something like the 50% isodose. Both are dose regions. The PTV is a geometric margin on the target, and the GTV is visible disease. Lung V20 and rectum V70 are organ constraints. 14 Gy is a one-fraction cord point maximum, not a target-volume name.

### Q23
Stem: A target is several centimeters thick along a proton beam. One pristine Bragg peak covers only a thin slice at the end of the range. Which description is the spread-out Bragg peak used to cover that thickness?
A. A photon depth-dose curve that has been renamed
B. Several pristine peaks of different energies, weighted and stacked so the high-dose region is flat across the target thickness
C. A uniform geometric shell around the target that replaces range uncertainty
D. An exit beam beyond the patient, matched to a parallel-opposed photon pair
Answer: B
Objective: III.1
Why: A pristine peak is too narrow for a thick target. A spread-out Bragg peak stacks pristine peaks of different energies and weights so the high dose is flat across the thickness. Protons do not deposit dose with a photon depth-dose shape, and they do not leave a photon-style exit beam. A geometric shell is a margin question, not the shape of the high-dose region.

### Q24
Stem: The flat high-dose region of a proton beam is widened so a thicker target is covered along the beam. What else rises, and why?
A. Dose beyond the distal edge, because protons leave an exit beam the way a photon beam does
B. The generic clinical RBE, which leaves 1.1 and becomes a larger factor when the region is wider
C. The entrance dose, because more proximal peaks are added under the plateau
D. The cord maximum, which is then allowed to exceed 50 Gy
Answer: C
Objective: III.1
Why: Widening a spread-out Bragg peak adds more proximal peaks, and those peaks raise the entrance dose under the plateau. Beyond the distal edge the dose falls to nearly nothing. There is no photon-style exit beam. The generic clinical proton RBE used for prescribing stays 1.1 unless a protocol states another factor. A wider high-dose region does not waive the cord limit of 50 Gy.

### Q25
Stem: A proton course is prescribed as 55 Gy (RBE). The only biological factor supplied is the generic clinical proton RBE of 1.1. What is the physical dose that prescription requires?
A. 60.5 Gy
B. 55.0 Gy
C. 1.1 Gy
D. 50.0 Gy
Answer: D
Objective: III.3
Why: Physical dose is the RBE dose divided by 1.1. 55 divided by 1.1 is 50.0 Gy, because 1.1 times 50.0 is 55. Multiplying 55 by 1.1 gives 60.5 Gy and runs the conversion backward. Leaving the prescription at 55.0 Gy treats Gy (RBE) as physical gray. 1.1 is the factor, not the dose. The 50.0 Gy result is the physical dose of this prescription. It is not a spinal-cord constraint.

### Q26
Stem: A proton clinical target receives the same uniform expansion that was used for photon setup error. The planner then marks range uncertainty as finished. What is still required?
A. A range margin along the beam, or robust optimization that varies the range. A geometric shell does not capture range uncertainty the way it captures setup error
B. A second copy of that same shell, named as an internal target volume, with no range term
C. A prescription in physical gray with the RBE removed, because the shell replaced the factor 1.1
D. The cord placed on the distal falloff so the sharp edge can stand in for the margin
Answer: A
Objective: III.7
Why: Proton range uncertainty lies mostly along the beam. The planning tools are a range margin and robust optimization that varies range. A uniform PTV-style shell models setup error in every direction and does not finish the range question. An internal target volume is the motion envelope, not a second setup shell and not a range model. RBE 1.1 remains the prescribing factor. Parking the cord on the distal falloff puts a serial organ where a range error moves the high dose.

### Q27
Stem: On the nominal proton plan the distal falloff sits just proximal to the spinal cord. The range along that beam is uncertain. Why is that a poor place to leave the cord?
A. The cord is scored only by mean dose, so a distal edge can be ignored once the mean is low
B. A range error moves the high dose along the beam and can put the cord into the peak. A serial organ is safer beside a lateral border than immediately downstream
C. A spread-out Bragg peak removes range uncertainty, so the nominal falloff is the falloff that will be delivered
D. The conventional cord limit of 50 Gy already contains proton range error, including for one fraction
Answer: B
Objective: III.7
Why: The distal edge is sharp, and its position is uncertain along the ray. A range error can push the high dose into a cord that the nominal plan shows as just beyond the falloff. The lateral border is where setup error lives, and a serial organ is safer there than downstream of the peak. The cord limit used here is the high-dose end, 50 Gy at conventional fractionation or 14 Gy as a one-fraction point maximum. Neither number is a range margin. Stacking peaks into a spread-out region does not remove range uncertainty.

### Q28
Stem: The cord can move with setup. The cord maximum on the cord contour alone is 48 Gy, under the 50 Gy limit. No planning organ-at-risk volume was made, and the objective was never applied to an expanded cord. What can this plan still do?
A. Prove the cord will remain under 50 Gy after a setup shift, because a DVH is a location

Objective: III.11
Why: A planning organ-at-risk volume is the organ plus a margin for its motion and for setup error. You spare that volume when a serial organ can move into the high dose. 48 Gy on the still contour is 2 Gy under 50 Gy (50 minus 48 is 2) and says nothing about where the cord sits after a shift. A DVH does not prove location. 48 Gy is not a one-fraction cord point dose, and the 14 Gy maximum belongs to one fraction. Lung V20 is a separate structure.

### Q30
Stem: The cord maximum is 54 Gy, which is above the 50 Gy limit. The planner adds a minimum DVH objective on the cord and a maximum DVH objective on the PTV. Why does that pair push the wrong way?
A. A minimum objective on the cord pushes cord dose up, and a maximum objective on the PTV pushes target dose down. The cord needs a maximum-type objective so the high-dose end is pushed down, and the target needs a minimum-type objective for coverage
B. The pair is the right direction, because any objective on the cord lowers the cord
C. An equivalent uniform dose on the cord finds the coordinates of the hot voxel and replaces both objectives
D. The 4 Gy excess disappears if the objectives are named, even when the fluence is left unchanged
Answer: A
Objective: III.12
Why: 54 minus 50 is 4 Gy over the cord limit. A minimum DVH objective pushes a stated fraction of an organ above a dose, so a minimum on the cord drives cord dose up. A maximum DVH objective pushes an organ below a dose, so a maximum on the PTV fights coverage. The cord wants a maximum-type objective. The target wants a minimum-type objective. An equivalent uniform dose summarizes an organ as one weighted dose and still does not locate the hot voxel. Naming an objective does not change a fluence that was not reoptimized.

### Q31
Stem: A target wraps around the spinal cord. An anterior-posterior pair covers that wrap only by also sending dose through the cord. Which planning approach is built for that concave shape?
A. Keep the opposed pair and raise the energy so penetration spares the cord
B. Inverse planning, with a minimum-type objective on the target and a maximum-type objective that pushes the cord maximum down toward the 50 Gy limit
C. Widen a proton spread-out region and aim the distal falloff at the cord
D. Renormalize the opposed pair, which changes the shape of the dose and pulls it off the cord
Answer: B
Objective: III.7
Why: A concave target around a serial organ is an inverse-planning problem: cover the target with a minimum-type objective and push the cord down with a maximum-type objective, scored here against 50 Gy. Higher photon energy does not carve a concavity out of an opposed pair. A proton distal falloff aimed at the cord puts range uncertainty on a serial organ. Renormalization scales the existing distribution. It does not change the shape, so the cord in the opposed pair stays in the high dose.

Objective: III.7
Why: The scale is 60 divided by 50, which is 1.2. The cord maximum becomes 45 times 1.2, which is 54 Gy. 54 minus 50 is 4 Gy over the cord limit, so the cord fails. The old 50 Gy figure was the target coverage, not a cord pass that survives the scale. Leaving the cord at 45 Gy ignores the scale. Setting the cord to 60 Gy replaces the cord with the new target dose. Scaling the cord by 50 divided by 60 gives 45 times 5/6, which is 37.5 Gy, and that runs the scale backward. The apertures did not improve. Every dose, including the cord, moved with the normalization.
