# Module 25. Planning Methodologies

Original items. Named site techniques, surface-dose behavior, APBI criteria, hot-spot expectations, and the conformation number.

## Named Site Techniques: IMN, Off-Cord, and Past-Pointing

### Q1
Stem: A post-mastectomy patient needs the internal mammary nodes in the first three intercostal spaces covered. The planner deepens only the upper portion of the tangent posterior border and keeps the lower border shallow with the MLC. What is this technique called, and what is its cost?
A. A mini-tangent or partially-wide tangent; more lung and heart in the upper part of the field
B. A half-beam block; it removes divergence at the match line
C. A hockey-stick field; it covers the para-aortic nodes
D. A mono-isocentric match; it eliminates the supraclavicular junction
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: Deepening the upper part of the tangent to take in the IMN while sparing the lower border is the partially-wide or mini-tangent technique; the price is additional ipsilateral lung and, on the left, heart within the deepened portion. A half-beam block is a divergence tool, not an IMN coverage method. The hockey-stick is a seminoma para-aortic and iliac field. A mono-isocentric setup addresses the supraclavicular junction, not nodal coverage.

### Q2
Stem: Instead of widening the tangents, a planner adds a separate anterior internal mammary field matched to the medial tangent border and delivers it as a mix of about 70 percent electrons and 30 percent photons. What is the purpose of the mixed beam?
A. To raise the depth of the 90 percent line beyond the heart
B. To cover the nodes near the surface while limiting the deep dose to heart and lung that an all-photon field would deliver
C. To eliminate the junction with the tangents
D. To reduce the skin dose to the sternum
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: An all-photon anterior IMN field exits through the heart and lung; mixing in a large electron component keeps the dose to the superficial nodes while the electron range stops short of the deep structures, and the photon share evens the surface coverage. The mix lowers deep dose rather than pushing a 90 percent line deeper. The junction with the medial tangent still exists and has to be managed. Electrons raise, not lower, the skin dose relative to photons.

### Q3
Stem: A locally advanced lung cancer is treated with AP/PA fields to a planned 60 Gy. At what accumulated dose does the conventional plan switch to an oblique off-cord arrangement, and why?
A. At about 20 Gy, because the lung V20 is reached
B. At 60 Gy, because the tumor dose is complete
C. At about 45 Gy, because the cord is approaching its conventional tolerance and the remaining dose must bypass it
D. At about 70 Gy, because the esophagus limit is reached
Answer: C
Objective: III.7
Why: The opposed pair carries the full dose to the cord every fraction, so the plan comes off the cord at about 40–45 Gy, the conventional cord tolerance used for this switch (QUANTEC puts the 0.2 percent myelopathy risk at 50 Gy, which the 45 Gy habit stays under), and obliques or an anterior plus posterior-oblique arrangement finish the tumor to 60–66 Gy. Lung V20 is a volumetric constraint, not a switch point for the cord. Switching at 60 Gy would leave no dose to deliver off cord. Seventy Gy exceeds the course.

### Q4
Stem: A conformal plan uses a single 200 degree posterior arc. The planner places the isocenter a short distance anterior to the tumor center, on the side away from the arc. What is this called, and why is it done?
A. Half-beam blocking, to remove divergence from the arc
B. Normalization to dmax, to make the entrance 100 percent
C. Off-axis prescription, to spare the skin
D. Past-pointing, because a partial arc shifts the dose maximum toward the entrance side and displacing the isocenter away from the arc moves the maximum back onto the tumor
Answer: D
Objective: III.7
Why: With a partial arc more beams enter from the arc side, so the dose maximum migrates toward that side; placing the isocenter past the tumor center, away from the arc, is past-pointing and recenters the maximum. Half-beam blocking and dmax normalization are unrelated tools. Off-axis prescription is not a named arc technique, and skin sparing is not the goal. VMAT optimizers make the adjustment through fluence instead.

## Surface Dose, APBI, and Plan Quality Indices

### Q1
Stem: A 6 MV field is enlarged from 10 by 10 cm to 25 by 25 cm with all other settings unchanged. What happens to the surface dose?
A. It rises, because more head scatter and contaminant electrons reach the skin
B. It falls, because the larger field spreads the same fluence over a wider area
C. It is unchanged, because surface dose depends only on energy
D. It falls to zero, because the buildup depth increases with field size
Answer: A
Objective: III.7
Why: Surface dose increases with field size because the open collimator and the air column produce more scattered photons and contaminant electrons that deposit dose in the buildup region. Field size does not dilute fluence at the central axis. Energy sets the buildup depth but is not the only determinant of skin dose. Buildup depth does not grow with field size and surface dose never reaches zero.

### Q2
Stem: To tighten the penumbra, a therapist proposes mounting the blocking tray 8 cm from the skin instead of the usual 20 cm. What is the dosimetric objection?
A. The tray will reduce the output by half
B. The tray is a source of contaminant electrons, and bringing it close to the skin raises the surface dose substantially
C. Penumbra increases when the tray is closer to the patient
D. The field size on the skin will double
Answer: B
Objective: III.7
Why: Electrons set in motion in the tray reach the skin unless the air gap absorbs and spreads them, so the minimum tray-to-skin distance of about 15–20 cm is a skin-dose rule; at 8 cm the surface dose rises sharply. The tray transmission factor is a few percent, not 50 percent. Moving a block closer to the patient narrows, not widens, the geometric penumbra, which is the stated motive. Field size at the skin is set by the collimator and the SSD.

### Q3
Stem: A tangential breast field enters the skin at a steep angle. Compared with a beam at normal incidence, what happens to the skin dose?
A. It falls because the path through air is longer
B. It is unchanged because dmax depends only on energy
C. It rises because the buildup region is compressed along the oblique surface
D. It rises only when a wedge is in the beam
Answer: C
Objective: III.7
Why: Oblique incidence shortens the effective buildup depth measured perpendicular to the skin, so the surface dose rises with the angle of incidence; tangents are the everyday example. The air path at the entrance is not what governs skin dose here. Depth of maximum along the beam depends on energy, but the skin sees the compressed buildup. The effect is geometric and does not require a wedge.

### Q4
Stem: Which patient meets the ASTRO suitable criteria for accelerated partial-breast irradiation outside a clinical trial?
A. A 42-year-old with a 1.5 cm node-negative tumor and negative margins
B. A 58-year-old with a 3.5 cm tumor and one positive node
C. A 60-year-old with a 1.8 cm tumor, negative margins, and a positive margin on re-excision
D. A 62-year-old with a 1.5 cm estrogen-receptor-positive invasive ductal carcinoma, negative margins, and negative nodes
Answer: D
Objective: III.12
Why: The suitable group requires age 50 or older, an invasive tumor of 2 cm or less (later guidance allows up to 3 cm), negative margins, node-negative disease, no lymphovascular invasion, and estrogen-receptor positivity; the 62-year-old meets all of them. The 42-year-old fails on age. The 58-year-old fails on tumor size and nodal status. A positive margin is unsuitable regardless of the other factors.

### Q5
Stem: An APBI course is delivered with a multichannel balloon applicator using the standard brachytherapy schedule. What are the dose, the number of fractions, and the timing?
A. 34 Gy in 10 fractions, twice daily at least 6 hours apart, over 5 days
B. 50 Gy in 25 fractions, once daily, over 5 weeks
C. 38.5 Gy in 10 fractions, once daily, over 2 weeks
D. 34 Gy in 5 fractions, once daily, over 1 week
Answer: A
Objective: III.12
Why: The standard brachytherapy APBI schedule is 34 Gy in 10 fractions given twice daily, with a minimum 6-hour interval, over 5 treatment days. Fifty Gy in 25 fractions is conventional whole-breast irradiation. The 38.5 Gy in 10 fractions schedule is the external-beam APBI regimen and it too is twice daily. Thirty-four Gy in 5 fractions is not a recognized APBI schedule; the 5-fraction external-beam regimen is 30 Gy every other day.

### Q6
Stem: A plan check compares the hot-spot location expected for several techniques. Which pairing is correct?
A. Parallel-opposed photons: hot spot at midplane, growing with higher energy
B. Inverse-planned IMRT: hot spot expected inside the PTV, with a hot spot outside the target counted as a plan defect even when it is small
C. Four-field box: hot spot at the isocenter exceeding 120 percent
D. Stereotactic radiosurgery: hot spot kept under 107 percent as in ICRU 50
Answer: B
Objective: III.7
Why: An inverse plan is expected to place its maximum within the PTV, typically up to about 107–110 percent, and any hot spot outside the target indicates misdirected fluence regardless of magnitude. Parallel-opposed fields are hot near dmax at both entrances, and the hot spots shrink as energy rises. A four-field box is homogeneous in the center with small entrance hot spots. Stereotactic plans deliberately run 125–200 percent at the center.

### Q7
Stem: In a plan review, adding beams to a conformal plan improved the conformity of the prescription isodose. What usually happens to the maximum dose inside the target at the same time?
A. It falls, because the dose is spread over more entrances
B. It stays exactly the same, because the prescription is fixed
C. It rises, because tighter conformity comes with a less homogeneous target dose
D. It moves outside the target
Answer: C
Objective: III.7
Why: Conformity and target homogeneity trade against each other: as more beams or arcs sculpt the prescription isodose to the target, the maximum inside the target typically rises. More entrances lower the entrance dose, not the target maximum. The prescription fixes the coverage statement, not the maximum. The maximum should remain inside the target; moving outside would be a defect, not an expected consequence.

### Q8
Stem: A PTV of 80 cc is planned so that 76 cc of it lies inside the prescription isodose, which encloses 95 cc in total. What is the conformation number?
A. 0.80
B. 0.76
C. 1.19
D. 0.95
Answer: B
Objective: III.12
Why: CN = (76 / 80) × (76 / 95) = 0.95 × 0.80 = 0.76. Check: 0.76 / 0.95 = 0.80, the selectivity factor. The 0.80 alone is only selectivity, and 0.95 alone is only coverage. The 1.19 figure is the RTOG conformity index, 95 / 80, which does not report whether the prescribed volume is on the target.
