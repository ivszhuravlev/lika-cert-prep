# Module 24. Special Treatment Procedures

Original items. Numeric targets and procedure facts for TBI, TSET, IORT, hemibody, electron arc, and stereotactic delivery.

## TBI Dose Rate, Uniformity, and Energy Rules

### Q1
Stem: A fractionated total-body program treats at 4 m with the machine dose rate lowered. Which midplane dose-rate band is the classic teaching figure for this technique?
A. 50 to 100 cGy per minute
B. 5 to 10 cGy per minute
C. 0.5 to 1 cGy per minute
D. 200 to 600 cGy per minute
Answer: B
Objective: III.6
Why: The classic midplane dose rate for fractionated TBI is about 5–10 cGy/min, achieved with the extended distance and a reduced machine dose rate, because a low dose rate to the lung is associated with less pneumonitis. Fifty to 100 cGy/min is an ordinary isocentric treatment rate scaled down only slightly. Half a cGy per minute would make a 2 Gy fraction take seven hours. Several hundred cGy/min is a normal linac at 100 cm.

### Q2
Stem: A TBI plan is reviewed before the first fraction. The physics check compares midplane doses at the head, neck, mediastinum, umbilicus, pelvis, knees, and ankles against the prescription point. What dose spread does the planning goal usually allow?
A. Within plus or minus 2 percent of the prescription
B. Within minus 5 and plus 7 percent, as for a conventional photon plan
C. Within plus or minus 10 percent of the prescription
D. Within plus or minus 25 percent, because the body cannot be compensated
Answer: C
Objective: III.6
Why: The accepted TBI uniformity target is a midplane dose within about ±10 percent of the prescription along the body, equivalent to keeping the hottest region under about 110 percent; compensators and lung shielding are the means. Two percent is tighter than the technique or the measurement can hold. The −5/+7 percent rule is the ICRU 50 goal for conventional target volumes and is not applied to TBI. Twenty-five percent would defeat the purpose of compensation.

### Q3
Stem: On the first TBI fraction, diodes are read at the prescription point and at several body sites and compared with the calculated doses. What agreement is normally required before the readings are accepted without adjusting the compensators?
A. Within about 5 percent
B. Within about 1 percent
C. Within about 15 percent
D. Within about 25 percent
Answer: A
Objective: III.6
Why: In-vivo TBI readings are expected to agree with the calculated dose within about ±5 percent; a larger difference triggers a compensator change or a recalculation before the next fraction. One percent is below the reproducibility of a diode on a patient at 4 m. Fifteen or 25 percent would exceed the ±10 percent uniformity goal the measurement is meant to confirm, so neither can be the acceptance window.

### Q4
Stem: A transplant patient has a 42 cm anterior–posterior separation at the pelvis and will be treated with opposed anterior and posterior fields at 3.5 m. Which beam arrangement gives the most homogeneous midplane dose through the trunk?
A. 6 MV anterior and posterior fields
B. 6 MV opposed lateral fields with the arms at the sides
C. Cobalt-60 anterior and posterior fields
D. 15 MV anterior and posterior fields
Answer: D
Objective: III.6
Why: For a thick separation, a higher energy lowers the ratio of entrance dose to midplane dose, so 15 MV (or 18 MV) AP/PA gives the flattest profile through the trunk; a spoiler restores the skin dose. At 42 cm the 6 MV AP/PA profile exceeds the ±10 percent goal. Laterals increase the separation further through the shoulders and pelvis, and cobalt is lower in energy than 6 MV.

### Q5
Stem: A pediatric TBI patient has a maximum separation of 24 cm and will be treated at 3.5 m. According to the usual separation rule, is 6 MV acceptable?
A. No; TBI always requires at least 10 MV
B. Yes; a separation under about 35 cm at 3 m or more keeps 6 MV midplane uniformity acceptable
C. No; 6 MV is acceptable only for separations above 35 cm
D. Yes, but only if lateral fields are used
Answer: B
Objective: III.6
Why: The rule of thumb is that when the largest separation is under about 35 cm and the distance is at least 3 m, 6 MV gives acceptable midplane uniformity; higher energies are reserved for thicker separations or lateral techniques across the shoulders. TBI does not always require 10 MV; many adult programs use 6 MV. The rule runs in the other direction from option C. Lateral fields increase the separation rather than relaxing the energy requirement.

### Q6
Stem: A TBI lung block is to be cut for a lung that measures 20 cm long at the patient midplane. The midplane is 400 cm from the source and the shadow tray is 50 cm from the source. What length should the block be at the tray?
A. 20 cm
B. 160 cm
C. 10 cm
D. 2.5 cm
Answer: D
Objective: III.6
Why: The block is scaled by tray distance over patient distance: 20 × 50 / 400 = 2.5 cm. Check: 2.5 × 400 / 50 = 20 cm at the patient. Twenty cm ignores the magnification entirely. Dividing the other way, 20 × 400 / 50, gives 160 cm, an impossible tray block. Ten cm would come from using 200 cm instead of 400 cm for the patient distance.

### Q7
Stem: A TBI protocol limits the lung to 8 Gy while the body receives 12 Gy. Which approach meets the lung limit while keeping the chest wall under the block treated to full dose?
A. Remove the lungs from the field with full-transmission blocks for every fraction and give no further dose to the chest wall
B. Lower the dose rate over the chest only
C. Use lung blocks for part of the course or partial-transmission blocks, and boost the chest wall under the block with electrons
D. Switch to opposed lateral fields so the arms shield the lung, and omit in-vivo measurement
Answer: C
Objective: III.6
Why: Lung dose is limited with partial-transmission blocks or with full blocks on some fractions, and the chest wall that the block also shadows is brought back to full dose with a matched electron boost; in-vivo dosimetry behind the block confirms the lung dose. Blocking every fraction with no boost underdoses the ribs and chest wall where marrow and disease sit. Dose rate cannot be changed for one region of a single field. Arms as compensators help, but omitting the measurement is wrong and laterals alone do not guarantee 8 Gy.

## TSET Geometry, IORT, Hemibody, and Electron Arc

### Q1
Stem: In the Stanford total-skin technique each of the six patient positions is treated with one beam angled upward and one angled downward rather than with a single horizontal beam at the patient's center. What is the dosimetric reason?
A. The two angled beams double the dose rate at the extended distance
B. A horizontal beam would exceed the collimator's maximum field size
C. The upward beam treats the front and the downward beam treats the back
D. The x-ray contamination of the degraded electron beam is highest on the central axis, so the axes are aimed above the head and below the feet while the electron fields overlap on the patient
Answer: D
Objective: III.6
Why: Bremsstrahlung contamination peaks along the central axis of the degraded beam; angling the pair about 15–20 degrees above and below horizontal puts each axis off the body while the two electron fields overlap to flatten the vertical profile, which keeps the midplane photon dose near 1 percent. Dose rate is not doubled; the beams are delivered in sequence. Field size at 3–4 m is not the constraint. Front and back are treated by the anterior and posterior positions, not by the up and down angles.

### Q2
Stem: Which set of statements describes the beam that actually reaches the patient in a 6 MeV total-skin electron treatment?
A. Energy about 3 to 4 MeV, the depth of maximum dose pulled toward the surface, 80 percent depth of several millimeters, midplane photon dose near 1 percent
B. Energy about 6 MeV, dmax at 1.4 cm, 80 percent depth of 2 cm, photon dose near 10 percent
C. Energy about 9 MeV because the scatterer adds energy, dmax at 2 cm
D. Energy unchanged at 6 MeV, photon dose near zero because the scatterer absorbs x-rays
Answer: A
Objective: I.3
Why: The scatterer and the long air path degrade the nominal 6 MeV to roughly 3–4 MeV at the patient, so dmax moves toward the surface, the 80 percent depth is on the order of 5–10 mm, and the course-long midplane photon dose is kept near 1 percent of the skin dose. Option B describes an undegraded 6 MeV beam at 100 cm. A scatterer cannot raise electron energy. The scatterer generates bremsstrahlung rather than removing it.

### Q3
Stem: A pancreatic tumor bed is treated in the operating room with a 9 MeV electron cone docked to the wound after the bowel is retracted. What single dose is typical for this procedure?
A. 1.8 to 2 Gy
B. 10 to 20 Gy
C. 40 to 50 Gy
D. 60 to 70 Gy
Answer: B
Objective: III.6
Why: Intraoperative radiotherapy delivers one large fraction of about 10–20 Gy to the exposed bed, usually combined with external beam before or after surgery. A 1.8–2 Gy dose is a conventional daily fraction, not an intraoperative dose. Forty to 70 Gy are total doses of fractionated courses and would be far beyond normal-tissue tolerance in one exposure.

### Q4
Stem: An electron IORT cone with a 30 degree bevel is chosen to sit flush on a sloping tumor bed. How does the bevel change the dose distribution compared with a flat-ended cone of the same diameter?
A. It has no effect because the electron energy is unchanged
B. It sharpens the penumbra on both sides of the field
C. The isodose lines tilt with the bevel and the penumbra on the bevelled side is wider
D. It raises the practical range by the bevel angle in centimeters
Answer: C
Objective: III.6
Why: A bevelled cone makes the surface oblique to the beam, so the isodose lines follow the bevel and the side where the cone edge is farther from the tissue has a wider penumbra; the planner accounts for that when choosing the cone and the margin. The energy is unchanged but the geometry is not, so there is an effect. Nothing about a bevel sharpens the penumbra. Practical range depends on energy, not on the cut of the cone.

### Q5
Stem: A patient with diffuse painful bone metastases receives a single large field covering the body from the umbilicus to the feet at an extended distance, with antiemetic premedication. What is the procedure, and what single dose is classic for this half?
A. Lower hemibody irradiation, about 8 Gy
B. Total body irradiation, about 12 Gy
C. Upper hemibody irradiation, about 6 Gy
D. Total skin electron irradiation, about 36 Gy
Answer: A
Objective: III.6
Why: A single large field to half the body for widespread bone pain is hemibody irradiation; the lower half (below the umbilicus) is classically given about 8 Gy and the upper half about 6 Gy because the lungs limit the upper field. TBI is a whole-body marrow-ablative course before transplant, not a palliative single field. Six Gy is the upper-half figure. TSET is a skin-lymphoma electron technique.

### Q6
Stem: Which statement about electron arc therapy for a curved chest wall is correct?
A. The isocenter is placed on the skin surface so that the SSD stays at 100 cm
B. The arc is extended about 10 to 15 degrees beyond each target edge, the field is narrow in the direction of rotation, and dmax is deeper than in a static field of the same energy
C. Lead at the arc limits should be thinner than for a static field because the beam is spread over many angles
D. The arc stops exactly at the target edges to avoid irradiating normal tissue
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: In electron arc therapy the isocenter sits at depth so the virtual source-to-skin distance is similar at every angle, the arc is over-travelled by about 10–15 degrees so dose does not fall off at the ends, the field is about 4–8 cm wide in the rotation direction, and the sweeping incidence (the velocity effect) pushes dmax deeper. Placing the isocenter on the skin would make the SSD change with angle. The lead at the arc edge is thicker, about E/2 plus 1 mm, because obliquely incident electrons penetrate farther. Stopping at the target edge underdoses the ends.

## Stereotactic Tolerances and Prescription Isodose

### Q1
Stem: A monthly TG-142 check finds the imaging isocenter and the treatment isocenter coincident within 1.6 mm. On which machine does that result pass?
A. On a machine used for SRS and SBRT only
B. On any machine, because the TG-142 tolerance is 2 mm regardless of use
C. On a machine not used for SRS or SBRT, where the tolerance is 2 mm; it fails the 1 mm tolerance for a stereotactic machine
D. On no machine, because the tolerance is 1 mm for every linac
Answer: C
Objective: III.6
Why: TG-142 sets the isocenter-coincidence tolerance at 1 mm for a machine used for SRS/SBRT and 2 mm for a non-stereotactic machine, so 1.6 mm passes only on the non-SRS machine. The stereotactic column is the stricter one, not the looser one. The 2 mm figure does not apply to every machine, and neither does the 1 mm figure.

### Q2
Stem: A lung SBRT plan with a 2.2 cm PTV is calculated on a 4 mm dose grid and the conformity index looks acceptable. What does TG-101 recommend about the grid, and why does it matter here?
A. A grid of 2 mm or finer, because a coarse grid misses the maximum and misstates the steep-gradient metrics at the target edge
B. A grid of 5 mm, because small targets need fewer calculation points
C. No specific grid, because the conformity index is independent of grid size
D. A grid equal to the CT slice thickness, which is 4 mm here
Answer: A
Objective: III.6
Why: TG-101 recommends a calculation grid of 2 mm or finer for SBRT (1 mm for very small targets) because the dose falls by tens of percent over a few millimeters, so a 4 mm grid can hide the maximum and shift the conformity and R50 values. Coarser grids make the sampling worse, not better. The conformity index is derived from the grid-sampled isodose surface and does depend on grid size. Slice thickness is a separate acquisition choice and does not set the dose grid.

### Q3
Stem: A single-fraction 18 Gy radiosurgery plan is prescribed to the 60 percent isodose line. What is the maximum dose in the target, and what range of prescription lines is typical for stereotactic plans?
A. 10.8 Gy; stereotactic plans are prescribed to 95 to 100 percent
B. 30 Gy; stereotactic plans are typically prescribed to 50 to 80 percent
C. 18 Gy; the prescription line is always the maximum
D. 22.5 Gy; stereotactic plans are prescribed to the 80 percent line only
Answer: B
Objective: III.6
Why: Maximum dose equals the prescription divided by the isodose fraction: 18 / 0.60 = 30 Gy; check 30 × 0.60 = 18 Gy. Stereotactic plans are prescribed to a line well below the maximum, typically 50–80 percent, which steepens the gradient outside the target and leaves an intentional hot center. Multiplying instead of dividing gives 10.8 Gy. The maximum equals the prescription only for a 100 percent prescription, which is not the stereotactic habit. The 22.5 Gy figure would apply to an 80 percent prescription, not 60 percent.

### Q4
Stem: A 7 mm spherical brain metastasis can be treated either with a 10 mm circular cone or with a high-definition MLC aperture on the same linac. Why does the cone give a sharper field edge?
A. The cone increases the electron energy, which narrows the penumbra
B. The MLC cannot form a field smaller than 3 cm
C. The cone removes the flattening filter, which steepens the field edge
D. The cone is closer to the patient and has a continuous tapered edge, so there is no interleaf leakage, no rounded leaf-end transmission, and a smaller geometric penumbra
Answer: D
Objective: III.6
Why: A tapered circular cone mounted near the patient defines a small field with a continuous edge, so the penumbra is smaller than that of a multileaf aperture with its interleaf leakage and rounded leaf ends. Cones do not alter the beam energy. High-definition MLCs form fields well under 3 cm; they lose on penumbra, not on field size. The flattening filter is a separate beam-line choice and is not removed by inserting a cone.
