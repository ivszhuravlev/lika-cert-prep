# Module 7. Treatment Machine Characteristics

Original items. Steering coils and symmetry, dose rate ranking across photon machines, photon versus electron mode neutron production, and secondary electron behavior.

## Steering, Dose Rate Ranking, Neutrons and Secondary Electrons

### Q1
Stem: A monthly profile scan shows the 6 MV beam 2% high on the gun side and 2% low on the target side at the same depth, with the horns otherwise at baseline. The most likely cause is:
A. A flattening filter that has been installed off center
B. An energy drift that softened the beam
C. A steering coil servo holding the electron beam off the central axis
D. A jaw positioning error on one side
Answer: C
Objective: I.5
Why: A tilted profile with one side high and the other low is a symmetry error, and the servo that positions the electron beam on the target through the steering coils is the usual culprit; the segmented monitor chamber normally corrects this during the beam. Energy drift changes flatness symmetrically by raising or sinking both horns. A filter installed off center would be a commissioning fault, not a new monthly finding. A jaw error moves a field edge, not the slope of the plateau.

### Q2
Stem: Which clinical photon machine delivers the highest dose rate at its treatment distance?
A. A Co-60 teletherapy unit with a new 10 kCi source
B. A Gamma Knife at the focus with new sources
C. An orthovoltage unit at 50 cm SSD
D. A linac in flattening-filter-free mode
Answer: D
Objective: I.5
Why: FFF linac modes reach roughly 1400 to 2400 cGy per minute at isocenter and even flattened linac beams run at 300 to 600 cGy per minute. A fresh cobalt source gives about 150 to 250 cGy per minute at 80 cm and loses about 1% a month. A new Gamma Knife delivers about 300 cGy per minute at the focus. Orthovoltage units are limited by tube heating to tens or a few hundred cGy per minute.

### Q3
Stem: A dual-mode linac is run at 18 MV photons and then at 18 MeV electrons for the same number of monitor units. Compared with the photon beam, neutron production in electron mode is:
A. Much lower, because the thin scattering foil produces little bremsstrahlung in the high-Z head components
B. Much higher, because the electrons strike the foil directly instead of a target
C. About the same, because both beams are above the photonuclear threshold
D. Zero, because electrons cannot cause photonuclear reactions
Answer: A
Objective: I.5
Why: Photoneutrons come from photons above about 8 to 10 MeV being absorbed in the target, filter, primary collimator and jaws; in photon mode the whole electron current makes bremsstrahlung in these high-Z parts, while in electron mode the beam is only spread by thin foils and very little bremsstrahlung is produced, so neutron fluence is one to two orders of magnitude lower. The nominal energy being above threshold is necessary but not sufficient, so option C is wrong. A small amount of bremsstrahlung is still made, so the yield is low rather than zero.

### Q4
Stem: In a Compton interaction the recoil electron is always emitted:
A. In the backward hemisphere relative to the incident photon
B. At 90 degrees to the incident photon regardless of energy
C. In the forward hemisphere, between 0 and 90 degrees from the incident direction
D. In the same direction as the scattered photon
Answer: C
Objective: I.5
Why: Momentum conservation confines the recoil electron to angles from 0 to 90 degrees; it goes straight forward when the photon is backscattered and carries maximum energy, and it emerges near 90 degrees with almost no energy when the photon is barely deflected. The scattered photon, not the electron, can go in any direction. At megavoltage energies the electrons are strongly forward-peaked, which is why dmax moves deeper as energy rises.

### Q5
Stem: A fast electron slowing down in water knocks a bound electron free with enough energy to leave its own ionization track. The ejected electron is called a:
A. Conversion electron
B. Characteristic electron
C. Auger electron
D. Delta ray
Answer: D
Objective: I.5
Why: Delta rays are energetic secondary electrons set in motion by hard collisions of a primary charged particle; they carry energy away from the primary track and are the reason restricted stopping power and the Bragg-Gray cut-off energy are defined. Auger electrons come from atomic de-excitation after an inner-shell vacancy. Conversion electrons come from nuclear de-excitation in place of a gamma ray. There is no particle called a characteristic electron.
