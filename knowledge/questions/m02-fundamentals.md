# Module 2 — Fundamentals

Original items. Scenarios and numbers are written for this bank.

## Fundamental Radiation Concepts

### Q1
Stem: Which pair is indirectly ionizing?
A. Protons and alpha particles
B. Electrons and positrons
C. Photons and neutrons
D. Carbon ions and protons
Answer: C
Objective: I.3
Why: Photons and neutrons have no charge, so they set secondary charged particles in motion. Those charged secondaries do the ionization. Protons, alphas, electrons, positrons, and carbon ions are charged and ionize directly.

### Q2
Stem: A photon raises an orbital electron to a higher shell but the electron stays in the atom. The atom is:
A. Ionized
B. Excited
C. An isomer
D. In radioactive equilibrium
Answer: B
Objective: I.4
Why: Excitation moves an electron to a higher bound state. Ionization removes the electron. Isomers and equilibrium are nuclear ideas, not a change of electron shell.

### Q3
Stem: The dose rate in air from a small source is 80 cGy/min at 100 cm. Attenuation in air is ignored. What is the dose rate at 200 cm?
A. 40 cGy/min
B. 20 cGy/min
C. 10 cGy/min
D. 160 cGy/min
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: Inverse square: 80 × (100/200)^2 = 80 × (1/2)^2 = 80 × 1/4 = 20 cGy/min. 40 cGy/min drops the square. 10 cGy/min uses a factor of 1/8. 160 cGy/min inverts the distance ratio.

### Q4
Stem: The same source in the previous setup is measured at 50 cm instead of 100 cm, still in air with no attenuation. The dose rate at 50 cm is:
A. 40 cGy/min
B. 160 cGy/min
C. 320 cGy/min
D. 80 cGy/min
Answer: C
Objective: I.10
Why: 80 × (100/50)^2 = 80 × 2^2 = 80 × 4 = 320 cGy/min. 160 cGy/min forgets the square (uses a factor of 2). 40 cGy/min moves the rate the wrong way. 80 cGy/min ignores distance.

### Q5
Stem: Two beams deliver the same absorbed dose to a small volume. One is 6 MV photons and one is alpha particles. The alpha dose is more biologically damaging per gray mainly because the alphas have:
A. A lower linear energy transfer
B. A higher linear energy transfer
C. No charge
D. A longer range in tissue than the photons
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Alphas are densely ionizing, so LET and RBE are high. Photons are low LET. Alphas are charged, and their range in tissue is short, not longer than a megavoltage photon beam.

### Q6
Stem: Intensity of a diagnostic x-ray beam is increased by raising mA, while kVp is held fixed. What changes?
A. The number of photons rises, and the maximum photon energy stays the same.
B. The maximum photon energy rises, and the number of photons stays the same.
C. Both the maximum energy and the half-value layer must rise.
D. The beam becomes particulate radiation.
Answer: A
Objective: I.2
Why: Tube current (mA) changes how many electrons hit the target, so it changes quantity. Peak kilovoltage sets the maximum photon energy. The beam is still photons.

## Atomic Structure

### Q1
Stem: A neutral atom has 53 protons and 78 neutrons. What are Z and A?
A. Z = 78 and A = 53
B. Z = 53 and A = 78
C. Z = 53 and A = 131
D. Z = 131 and A = 53
Answer: C
Objective: I.1
Why: Z is the proton number, 53. A is protons plus neutrons, 53 + 78 = 131. Swapping Z with N, or calling A the neutron count, is the usual slip.

### Q2
Stem: How many electrons fill the K shell, and how many fill the L shell?
A. K holds 8 and L holds 2
B. K holds 2 and L holds 8
C. K holds 18 and L holds 8
D. K holds 2 and L holds 18
Answer: B
Objective: I.2
Why: The shell capacity is 2n^2. For n = 1, the K shell holds 2. For n = 2, the L shell holds 8. Eighteen is the M shell (n = 3).

### Q3
Stem: For tungsten, which electron binding energy is the largest?
A. Valence shell
B. M shell
C. L shell
D. K shell
Answer: D
Objective: I.2
Why: Binding energy is greatest for the innermost shell. The K shell is tighter than L, M, or the valence electrons. That is why a K-shell vacancy releases the highest characteristic photon when it is filled from an outer shell.

### Q4
Stem: An atom loses a K-shell electron and an L-shell electron falls in. The energy that does not leave as a characteristic photon is carried by:
A. An Auger electron
B. A neutron
C. An alpha particle
D. A rise in Z
Answer: A
Objective: I.4
Why: After an inner-shell vacancy, the atom relaxes either by a characteristic x-ray or by an Auger electron. Z does not change, and the emitted particle is not a neutron or an alpha from the nucleus.

### Q5
Stem: Fluorescence yield is higher in high-Z atoms than in low-Z atoms. The competing process, which is more likely in a low-Z atom, is:
A. Pair production
B. Auger electron emission
C. Alpha decay
D. Photonuclear emission of a neutron
Answer: B
Objective: I.4
Why: A filled inner-shell vacancy produces either a characteristic photon or an Auger electron. Low-Z atoms favor the Auger path. Pair production and photonuclear reactions are photon interactions with the atom or nucleus, not the relaxation of a shell vacancy. Alpha decay is a nuclear transformation.

## Classification of Atoms

### Q1
Stem: C-12 and C-14 have the same proton number and different mass numbers. They are:
A. Isotopes
B. Isobars
C. Isotones
D. Isomers
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Isotopes share Z and differ in A (and therefore in neutron number). Isobars share A, isotones share N, and isomers share both Z and A but differ in nuclear energy state.

### Q2
Stem: C-14 (Z = 6, A = 14) and N-14 (Z = 7, A = 14) are:
A. Isotopes
B. Isobars
C. Isomers
D. The same nuclide
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: Both have A = 14 and they have different Z, so they are isobars. They are not isotopes (Z differs), not isomers (A and Z would both have to match), and not the same nuclide.

### Q3
Stem: C-14 has Z = 6 and N = 8. N-15 has Z = 7 and N = 8. The pair is:
A. Isotopes
B. Isobars
C. Isotones
D. Isomers
Answer: C
Objective: I.1
Why: Isotones have the same neutron number and different Z. Here both have N = 8. They are not isotopes, and A is 14 versus 15 so they are not isobars or isomers.

### Q4
Stem: Tc-99m and Tc-99 have the same Z and the same A. Tc-99m is a long-lived excited nuclear state. They are:
A. Isomers
B. Isotopes
C. Isobars
D. Isotones
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Same proton number and same mass number, different nuclear energy, is the definition of isomers. The “m” marks the metastable state. Calling them isotopes would require different A.

### Q5
Stem: Which change makes a new isotope of the original element?
A. Add one neutron
B. Add one proton
C. Remove one proton and one neutron so A is unchanged
D. Raise the nucleus to a metastable state without changing Z or A
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Adding a neutron keeps Z and increases A, which is a different isotope. Adding a proton changes the element. Removing one proton and one neutron changes the element and keeps A, which is an isobar. A metastable state of the same nucleus is an isomer.

### Q6
Stem: A metastable nucleus loses its extra energy by internal conversion. Which emission can come next from the atom?
A. An Auger electron
B. A neutron from the nucleus
C. A rise of Z by 1
D. Two 511 keV annihilation photons
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Internal conversion ejects a bound electron and leaves a shell vacancy. That vacancy can relax by Auger electron emission. Z and A stay the same, so this is not beta decay. The nucleus does not emit a neutron, and the event is not positron annihilation.

## Fundamentals Mini-Quiz

### Q1
Stem: The rest energy of an electron is 511 keV. A positron and an electron annihilate at rest. The photon result is:
A. One 511 keV photon
B. Two 511 keV photons emitted in opposite directions
C. One 1.022 MeV photon
D. Two 1.022 MeV photons
Answer: B
Objective: I.4
Why: The two rest energies, 511 keV + 511 keV = 1.022 MeV, leave as two 511 keV photons so momentum is conserved. A single 1.022 MeV photon, or two photons of 1.022 MeV each, does not match annihilation at rest.

### Q2
Stem: Which statement about a 6 MV photon beam versus a 6 MeV electron beam is correct?
A. Both have charge and both have a sharp Bragg peak.
B. The photons have no charge and are attenuated exponentially. The electrons have charge and a finite practical range.
C. The electrons are uncharged and the photons have a finite range of about 6 cm in tissue.
D. Neither beam can ionize tissue.
Answer: B
Objective: I.3
Why: Photons are uncharged electromagnetic radiation and are attenuated exponentially, not stopped at a fixed range. Electrons are charged, lose energy continuously, and have a practical range. A Bragg peak is a heavy-charged-particle feature, not a photon or clinical electron feature. Both beams ionize tissue, the photons indirectly.

### Q3
Stem: Iodine-131 and iodine-125 are:
A. Isotopes
B. Isobars
C. Isotones
D. Isomers of the same mass number
Answer: A
Objective: I.1
Why: Both are iodine, so Z = 53, and the mass numbers 131 and 125 differ. That is an isotope pair. They are not the same A, so they are not isomers or isobars.

### Q4
Stem: A point source reads 45 cGy/min at 80 cm in air. No attenuation. What does it read at 120 cm?
A. 30 cGy/min
B. 20 cGy/min
C. 67.5 cGy/min
D. 101 cGy/min
Answer: B
Objective: I.10
Why: 45 × (80/120)^2 = 45 × (2/3)^2 = 45 × 4/9 = 20 cGy/min. 30 cGy/min uses 80/120 without the square. 67.5 cGy/min inverts the ratio and forgets the square. 101 cGy/min is 45 × (120/80)^2, the ratio inverted and squared.

### Q5
Stem: A neutral atom of Z = 20 must contain how many electrons, and the symbol A tells you what?
A. 20 electrons, and A is the proton number
B. 20 electrons, and A is the nucleon number
C. A electrons, and Z is the neutron number
D. 2 electrons, because only the K shell is occupied in every atom
Answer: B
Objective: I.1
Why: A neutral atom has Z electrons. A is the mass number, protons plus neutrons. Z is not the neutron number. Only hydrogen and helium live in the K shell alone; Z = 20 fills K, L, and part of M.
