# Module 22. Cross-Sectional Anatomy

Original items. Landmarks and atlas borders added from the RM Review gap list: nasopharynx and skull base, abdominal and vertebral landmarks, ribs, the RTOG breast atlas, lung fissures, IASLC stations, and pelvic nodal drainage by site. No item needs an image.

## Skull Base and Nasopharynx Landmarks

### Q1
Stem: A nasopharyngeal carcinoma is described as arising in a slit-like recess on the lateral pharyngeal wall, behind and above the cartilaginous bump that surrounds the Eustachian tube opening. What is that recess?
A. Pyriform sinus
B. Fossa of Rosenmüller
C. Vallecula
D. Sphenoid sinus
Answer: B
Objective: III.4
Why: The fossa of Rosenmüller, or lateral pharyngeal recess, lies posterior and superior to the torus tubarius on the lateral nasopharyngeal wall and is the most common site of origin of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, which explains the unilateral middle-ear effusion from tube blockage. The pyriform sinus is a hypopharyngeal air pocket beside the larynx. The vallecula is the pouch between the base of tongue and the epiglottis in the oropharynx. The sphenoid sinus is a paranasal sinus in the skull base, anterior to the sella, not a pharyngeal recess.

### Q2
Stem: A nasopharyngeal tumor has climbed from the lateral pharyngeal recess directly upward into the cavernous sinus and the patient has a new lateral rectus palsy. Through which skull-base opening did it most likely pass?
A. Foramen magnum
B. Jugular foramen
C. Foramen lacerum
D. Cribriform plate
Answer: C
Objective: III.4
Why: The foramen lacerum sits immediately above the fossa of Rosenmüller at the junction of sphenoid body, petrous apex, and basiocciput; it is closed by fibrocartilage in life and the internal carotid artery crosses its upper surface toward the cavernous sinus, so it is the classic route of nasopharyngeal carcinoma into the sinus where nerves III, IV, V1, V2, and VI run. The sixth nerve is the one most often hit, giving the lateral rectus palsy. The foramen magnum carries the medulla and vertebral arteries at the back of the skull base. The jugular foramen carries nerves IX, X, and XI and the jugular vein, posterior and lateral to the lacerum. The cribriform plate is in the anterior cranial fossa, the route for sinonasal tumors.

### Q3
Stem: A squamous carcinoma of the oral cavity shows perineural spread along the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve toward the middle cranial fossa. Which foramen does that nerve use, and which neighboring opening carries the middle meningeal artery?
A. Foramen ovale for V3; foramen spinosum for the middle meningeal artery
B. Foramen rotundum for V3; foramen ovale for the middle meningeal artery
C. Superior orbital fissure for V3; optic canal for the middle meningeal artery
D. Foramen spinosum for V3; foramen lacerum for the middle meningeal artery
Answer: A
Objective: III.4
Why: V3, the mandibular division, leaves the skull through the foramen ovale in the greater wing of the sphenoid, and the foramen spinosum just posterolateral to it carries the middle meningeal artery. The foramen rotundum carries V2, the maxillary division, to the pterygopalatine fossa, not V3. The superior orbital fissure carries III, IV, VI, and V1 into the orbit, and the optic canal carries the optic nerve and ophthalmic artery; neither carries a trigeminal division to the mandible or a meningeal artery. The foramen lacerum carries no named nerve; the carotid only crosses its roof.

### Q4
Stem: An orbital apex contour must separate two openings. One carries only the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery. The other carries the oculomotor, trochlear, and abducens nerves, the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve, and the ophthalmic veins. Name them in that order.
A. Foramen rotundum, then foramen ovale
B. Superior orbital fissure, then optic canal
C. Inferior orbital fissure, then foramen spinosum
D. Optic canal, then superior orbital fissure
Answer: D
Objective: III.4
Why: The optic canal, in the lesser wing of the sphenoid medial to the fissure, transmits only cranial nerve II and the ophthalmic artery. The superior orbital fissure, lateral and inferior to it, transmits III, IV, VI, V1, and the ophthalmic veins, so a lesion there gives a complete ophthalmoplegia with a numb forehead, while a lesion of the canal gives visual loss alone. Option B has the two reversed. Rotundum carries V2 and ovale carries V3, both to the face, not the orbit. The inferior orbital fissure carries V2 branches and the infraorbital vessels, and spinosum carries the middle meningeal artery.

### Q5
Stem: A posterior skull-base opening transmits the glossopharyngeal, vagus, and spinal accessory nerves together with the internal jugular vein as the sigmoid sinus leaves the skull. Which opening is it, and what does the small canal just anteromedial to it carry?
A. Jugular foramen; the hypoglossal canal carries nerve XII
B. Foramen magnum; the hypoglossal canal carries nerve VII
C. Internal acoustic meatus; the stylomastoid foramen carries nerve XII
D. Foramen lacerum; the carotid canal carries nerve IX
Answer: A
Objective: III.4
Why: The jugular foramen, between the petrous temporal bone and the occipital bone, carries nerves IX, X, and XI and the jugular bulb, so a tumor there gives a hoarse voice, a weak shoulder shrug, and loss of the gag reflex together. The hypoglossal canal lies just anteromedial to it and carries nerve XII to the tongue. The foramen magnum carries the medulla, the vertebral arteries, and the spinal roots of XI, not the jugular vein. The internal acoustic meatus carries VII and VIII into the petrous bone, and the stylomastoid foramen is where VII exits toward the parotid. The carotid canal carries the internal carotid artery, not nerve IX.

### Q6
Stem: On an axial slice at the level of the lateral ventricles, a thick midline band of white matter runs between the frontal horns anteriorly and another band runs between the atria posteriorly. A glioblastoma that spreads across this structure into both hemispheres has a nickname. What is the structure, and which part is the anterior band?
A. Cingulate gyrus; the anterior band is the splenium
B. Corpus callosum; the anterior band is the genu
C. Fornix; the anterior band is the body
D. Pituitary stalk; the anterior band is the rostrum
Answer: B
Objective: III.4
Why: The corpus callosum is the great commissure joining the hemispheres and forming the roof of the lateral ventricles; from front to back its parts are rostrum, genu, body, and splenium, so the anterior band between the frontal horns is the genu and the posterior band between the atria is the splenium. A glioblastoma crossing it to involve both frontal lobes is the butterfly glioma. The cingulate gyrus is the gray-matter gyrus above the callosum, not a white-matter band. The fornix is a thin arched tract beneath the callosum. The pituitary stalk is a thin midline structure between the chiasm and the sella, far below the ventricles.

## Abdominal and Thoracic Landmarks

### Q1
Stem: On an upper-abdominal axial slice a fluid-density oval sits on the undersurface of the liver, between the right lobe and segment IV, anterior to the right kidney and the duodenum and medial to the hepatic flexure. Which organ is it, and in which abdominal quadrant?
A. Spleen, left upper quadrant
B. Gallbladder, left upper quadrant
C. Stomach fundus, right upper quadrant
D. Gallbladder, right upper quadrant
Answer: D
Objective: III.4
Why: The gallbladder lies in a fossa on the undersurface of the right hepatic lobe, between the right lobe and the quadrate lobe (segment IV), near the transpyloric plane at about L1, with its fundus reaching the ninth right costal cartilage; that is the right upper quadrant, close to the duodenum and the pancreatic head. The spleen is posterolateral in the left upper quadrant. The stomach fundus is under the left hemidiaphragm, left of midline. Option B names the organ correctly but puts it on the wrong side.

### Q2
Stem: Two bends of the colon are compared on a planning CT. One turns under the right lobe of the liver; the other turns under the tip of the spleen. Which bend is which, and which one normally sits higher?
A. The right bend is the hepatic flexure, the left bend is the splenic flexure, and the splenic flexure is higher
B. The right bend is the splenic flexure, the left bend is the hepatic flexure, and the hepatic flexure is higher
C. Both bends are called hepatic flexures, and they sit at the same height
D. The right bend is the hepatic flexure, the left bend is the splenic flexure, and the hepatic flexure is higher
Answer: A
Objective: III.4
Why: The hepatic flexure is on the right, where the ascending colon turns into the transverse colon beneath the liver, anterior to the lower pole of the right kidney and the descending duodenum. The splenic flexure is on the left, under the spleen, anchored by the phrenicocolic ligament, and it is higher and more posterior because the liver pushes the right side down. Option B swaps the names. Option D has the names right but the heights reversed. The two flexures have different names and different heights.

### Q3
Stem: An RTOG pelvic protocol asks for a bowel bag rather than individual loops. What is contoured, and which QUANTEC limit applies to that contour?
A. Each small-bowel loop separately; the volume above 15 Gy under about 120 cubic centimeters
B. The rectum and sigmoid together; the volume above 70 Gy under 25 percent
C. The outer boundary of the peritoneal space holding all bowel, excluding identifiable non-bowel structures; the volume above 45 Gy under about 195 cubic centimeters
D. The bladder plus every bowel loop in contact with it; the mean dose under 30 Gy
Answer: C
Objective: III.4
Why: The bowel bag is the peritoneal cavity contour: the outer margin of the space that contains the small and large bowel, drawn from about 1 cm above the PTV to the lowest bowel loop, with bone, muscle, bladder, rectum, uterus, prostate, and major vessels cut out. Because it describes where bowel may be rather than where it was on one scan, QUANTEC gives it its own limit, V45 under about 195 cubic centimeters. The V15 under 120 cubic centimeters limit belongs to individually contoured loops and cannot be applied to a bag. Rectal V70 is a rectal constraint. There is no bladder-plus-bowel organ in any protocol.

### Q4
Stem: Two surface landmarks on the anterior chest and abdomen are palpated on a supine patient: the joint between the body of the sternum and the xiphoid process, and the tip of the xiphoid itself. At about which vertebral levels do they lie?
A. T4 to T5 for both, because they mark the carina
B. T9 for the xiphisternal joint and about T10 for the xiphoid tip
C. L1 for the joint and L3 to L4 for the tip
D. T2 to T3 for the joint and T4 to T5 for the tip
Answer: B
Objective: III.4
Why: The xiphisternal joint is at about T9 and the xiphoid tip at about T10, which is also the level where the esophagus crosses the diaphragm; the xiphoid is therefore the landmark for the lower end of the heart and the dome of the liver. T4 to T5 is the sternal angle with the carina behind it. T2 to T3 is the suprasternal notch. L1 is the transpyloric plane and L3 to L4 is the umbilicus in a slim adult, both well below the sternum.

### Q5
Stem: A horizontal plane is drawn halfway between the suprasternal notch and the pubic symphysis. Which vertebral level is it, and which structures does it pass through?
A. L4; the iliac crests and the aortic bifurcation
B. T12; the celiac trunk and the esophageal hiatus
C. L3; the umbilicus and the inferior mesenteric artery
D. L1; the pylorus, the pancreatic neck, the renal hila, the superior mesenteric artery origin, and the tip of the conus
Answer: D
Objective: III.4
Why: That is the transpyloric plane of Addison at L1. It passes through the pylorus, the neck of the pancreas, the fundus of the gallbladder, the hila of the kidneys, the duodenojejunal flexure, the origin of the superior mesenteric artery, and the end of the adult spinal cord. L4 is the iliac crests and the aortic bifurcation, a hand's breadth lower. T12 is the celiac trunk and the aortic hiatus; the esophageal hiatus is T10. L3 is near the umbilicus and the inferior mesenteric artery origin, below the transpyloric plane.

### Q6
Stem: The three unpaired and paired visceral branches of the abdominal aorta are listed from cranial to caudal for a para-aortic contour: the celiac trunk, the superior mesenteric artery, and the renal arteries. Which set of vertebral levels is correct, and over which span do the kidneys lie?
A. Celiac T12, superior mesenteric L1, renal arteries L1 to L2; kidneys from about T12 to L3
B. Celiac L2, superior mesenteric L3, renal arteries L4; kidneys from about L2 to S1
C. Celiac T8, superior mesenteric T10, renal arteries T12; kidneys from about T8 to T12
D. Celiac L4, superior mesenteric L1, renal arteries T12; kidneys from about L4 to L5
Answer: A
Objective: III.4
Why: The celiac trunk leaves the aorta at about T12 just below the aortic hiatus, the superior mesenteric artery at L1 on the transpyloric plane, and the renal arteries at about L1 to L2; the inferior mesenteric artery follows at L3 and the bifurcation at L4. The kidneys span about T12 to L3, from the eleventh or twelfth rib to just above the iliac crest, with the right kidney about half a vertebra lower. Option B puts everything a full lumbar segment or more too low. Option C is in the chest. Option D lists the branches in the wrong order; the celiac is the highest, not the lowest.

### Q7
Stem: Rib pairs are grouped by how they reach the sternum. Which grouping is correct?
A. Ribs 1 to 10 are true ribs, 11 and 12 are false ribs, and there are no floating ribs
B. Ribs 1 to 5 are true ribs, 6 to 10 are floating, and 11 and 12 are false
C. Ribs 1 to 7 are true ribs, 8 to 10 are false ribs that join the costal margin, and 11 and 12 are floating ribs with no anterior attachment
D. Ribs 1 to 12 are all true ribs because each articulates with a vertebra
Answer: C
Objective: III.4
Why: Ribs 1 to 7 are true, or vertebrosternal, ribs: each reaches the sternum by its own costal cartilage. Ribs 8 to 12 are false. Of these, 8 to 10 are vertebrochondral, their cartilages fusing to the one above to form the costal margin, and 11 and 12 are floating, or vertebral, ribs that end free in the abdominal wall. Articulating with a vertebra is true of all twelve and does not define a true rib. Options A and B misassign the groups; there is no floating group in the middle of the cage.

## Breast Contouring Atlas Borders

### Q1
Stem: Under the RTOG breast atlas, which pair of borders defines the cranial and caudal limits of the whole-breast CTV?
A. Cranial at the clinical reference and the second rib insertion; caudal at the loss of CT-apparent breast tissue
B. Cranial at the clavicle; caudal at the xiphoid
C. Cranial at the caudal edge of the cricoid; caudal at the fourth rib
D. Cranial at the coracoid process; caudal at the inframammary fold wire only, regardless of CT
Answer: A
Objective: III.4
Why: The atlas sets the breast CTV cranially at the clinical reference wire and the second rib insertion and caudally where CT-apparent breast tissue is lost, again guided by the clinical reference. The clavicle and xiphoid are classic tangent field edges from two-dimensional planning, not atlas CTV borders. The cricoid is the cranial border of the supraclavicular CTV, and the fourth rib is the caudal end of the internal mammary CTV. The coracoid is the cranial limit of axillary level III, and the atlas uses the CT appearance of the breast, not the wire alone, for the caudal limit.

### Q2
Stem: Under the RTOG breast atlas, where do the medial and lateral borders of the breast CTV stop, and what does its posterior border exclude?
A. Medial at the midline of the sternum, lateral at the posterior axillary line, posterior border includes the ribs
B. Medial at the contralateral nipple, lateral at the latissimus dorsi, posterior border includes pectoralis major only
C. Medial at the sternal-rib junction, lateral at the clinical reference near the mid-axillary line, posterior border excludes the pectoralis muscles, chest wall muscles, and ribs
D. Medial at the internal mammary vessels, lateral at the humeral head, posterior border excludes the skin
Answer: C
Objective: III.4
Why: The atlas breast CTV runs medially to the sternal-rib junction and laterally to the clinical reference, typically the mid-axillary line, excluding the latissimus dorsi; posteriorly it excludes the pectoralis and chest-wall muscles and the ribs, and anteriorly it excludes the first 5 mm under the skin. The chest-wall CTV after mastectomy shares the same four edges but adds the muscles and ribs, stopping at the rib-pleural interface. The midline and posterior axillary line are tangent-field conventions, not atlas borders. The internal mammary vessels define the IMN CTV, and the skin is an anterior, not posterior, limit.

### Q3
Stem: Under the RTOG breast atlas, which border is the cranial limit of the supraclavicular CTV, and which intercostal spaces does the internal mammary CTV cover?
A. The hyoid; intercostal spaces one through six
B. The caudal edge of the cricoid cartilage; intercostal spaces one through three
C. The sternal angle; intercostal spaces two through five
D. The mastoid tip; intercostal spaces four through six
Answer: B
Objective: III.4
Why: The supraclavicular CTV begins at the caudal edge of the cricoid cartilage and ends at the junction of the brachiocephalic and axillary veins near the caudal edge of the clavicular head, bounded by the sternocleidomastoid anteriorly and the anterior scalene posteriorly. The internal mammary CTV follows the internal mammary vessels in the first three intercostal spaces, from the top of the medial first rib to the top of the fourth rib. The hyoid and mastoid are head-and-neck landmarks above the cricoid. The sternal angle is the carina level, too low for the top of the supraclavicular fossa, and the lower intercostal spaces are not included in the IMN volume.

## Lung Lobes, Nodal Stations, and Drainage by Site

### Q1
Stem: A fissure separates the right upper lobe from the right middle lobe. Which fissure is it, how many fissures does each lung have, and what is the anteroinferior tongue of the left upper lobe called?
A. The oblique fissure; two on each side; the left middle lobe
B. The horizontal fissure; two on the right and two on the left; the cardiac lobe
C. The horizontal fissure; two on the right and one on the left; the lingula
D. The oblique fissure; one on each side; the lingula
Answer: C
Objective: III.4
Why: The horizontal, or minor, fissure exists only on the right, running forward from the oblique fissure at about the fourth costal cartilage to separate the right upper from the right middle lobe. Each lung has an oblique, or major, fissure, so the right has two fissures and three lobes while the left has one fissure and two lobes. The lingula is the part of the left upper lobe that lies against the heart in the position of a middle lobe; it is not a lobe of its own. The oblique fissure separates the lower lobe from the lobes above it on both sides.

### Q2
Stem: On the IASLC lymph-node map, which station numbers are the lower paratracheal, the aortopulmonary window, the subcarinal, and the hilar groups, in that order?
A. 2, 5, 7, 10
B. 4, 5, 7, 10
C. 4, 6, 8, 11
D. 3, 5, 9, 12
Answer: B
Objective: III.4
Why: Station 4 is lower paratracheal, station 5 is the subaortic or aortopulmonary window, station 7 is subcarinal, and station 10 is hilar. Station 2 is the upper paratracheal group above station 4. Station 6 is para-aortic along the ascending aorta, station 8 is paraesophageal, and station 11 is interlobar inside the lung. Station 3 is prevascular and retrotracheal, station 9 is the pulmonary ligament, and station 12 is lobar.

### Q3
Stem: A right upper lobe lung cancer has a positive subcarinal node and a positive right interlobar node. No supraclavicular or left-sided nodes are involved. What is the N category and why?
A. N2, because the subcarinal station 7 counts as an ipsilateral mediastinal node and stations 10 to 14 are N1
B. N3, because the subcarinal node sits at the midline and therefore counts as contralateral
C. N1, because both nodes are within 2 cm of the carina
D. N3, because any involved mediastinal node is N3
Answer: A
Objective: III.4
Why: Stations 10 through 14 lie inside the pleural reflection and are N1, so the interlobar station 11 node alone would be N1. The subcarinal station 7 is a mediastinal station and is treated as ipsilateral for a tumor of either lung, so its involvement makes the stage N2. N3 requires contralateral mediastinal or hilar nodes or any scalene or supraclavicular node, and none is present. Distance from the carina is not a staging rule.

### Q4
Stem: Four pelvic primaries are listed: a mid-rectal adenocarcinoma, a prostate adenocarcinoma with a nodal CTV, a muscle-invasive bladder cancer, and a squamous carcinoma of the anal canal. For which one does the standard nodal CTV include the inguinal nodes?
A. The mid-rectal cancer, because the rectum drains to the groin
B. The prostate cancer, because the nodal CTV follows the femoral vessels
C. The bladder cancer, because the bladder lies behind the pubis
D. The anal canal cancer, because the canal below the dentate line drains to the superficial inguinal nodes
Answer: D
Objective: III.4
Why: The anal canal drains in two directions: above the dentate line to the mesorectal and internal iliac chains, below it to the superficial inguinal nodes, so an anal canal plan covers the mesorectum, the internal and external iliacs, and both groins. The rectum drains to the mesorectal, presacral, and internal iliac nodes and reaches the groin only when a tumor extends into the anal canal. Prostate and bladder drain to the obturator, internal and external iliac, presacral, and common iliac nodes; neither standard volume enters the groin, and the femoral heads below the inguinal ligament are bone, not a nodal chain.

### Q5
Stem: A testicular seminoma is staged. Where are the first-echelon lymph nodes, and when do the inguinal nodes become a target?
A. Obturator and external iliac nodes; inguinal nodes always
B. Mesorectal nodes; inguinal nodes never
C. Para-aortic and paracaval nodes near the renal hilum at about L1 to L2; inguinal nodes only after scrotal violation or prior inguinal surgery
D. Internal iliac nodes; inguinal nodes when the tumor exceeds 4 cm
Answer: C
Objective: III.4
Why: The testis descends from the posterior abdominal wall and keeps its blood supply, so lymph follows the gonadal vessels to the para-aortic and paracaval nodes at the level of the renal vessels, with the left side entering at the left renal vein around L1 to L2 and the right slightly lower at the IVC. That is why a seminoma field is a para-aortic strip, and the pelvic hockey-stick extension is added only for ipsilateral iliac nodes. Inguinal nodes are not a primary echelon; they are at risk only when scrotal skin has been violated or an earlier inguinal or scrotal operation has rerouted drainage. Tumor size is a prognostic factor, not a drainage rule, and the pelvic chains in options A, B, and D are second-echelon at most.

### Q6
Stem: Which gynecologic primaries drain first to the inguinofemoral nodes, and which can reach the para-aortic nodes directly without passing through positive pelvic nodes?
A. Cervix drains to the groin; the ovary never reaches the para-aortic nodes
B. Vulva and the distal third of the vagina drain to the groin; the endometrial fundus and the ovary reach the para-aortic nodes along the ovarian vessels
C. Upper two thirds of the vagina drain to the groin; the cervix never reaches the para-aortic nodes
D. Endometrium drains to the groin; the vulva reaches the para-aortic nodes directly
Answer: B
Objective: III.4
Why: The vulva and the distal third of the vagina drain to the superficial then deep inguinofemoral nodes, the highest of which is the node of Cloquet under the inguinal ligament, and only then to the external iliacs. The upper two thirds of the vagina and the cervix drain like the cervix, to the parametrial, obturator, iliac, and presacral chains, and reach the para-aortic nodes in sequence when the common iliacs are involved, which is when a cervix field is extended to the renal vessels. The endometrial fundus and the ovary drain along the ovarian vessels straight to the para-aortic chain, so an endometrial plan can need para-aortic coverage without positive pelvic nodes. Options A, C, and D each attach the groin to a primary that drains to the pelvis instead.
