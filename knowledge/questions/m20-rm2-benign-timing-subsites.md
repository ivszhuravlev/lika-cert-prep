# Module 20. Site-Specific Clinical Oncology

Original items. Round 2 from the RM Review gap list: benign indications, timing rules, uveal melanoma size management, and subsite statistics.

## Benign Indications, Timing Rules, and Subsite Facts

### Q1
Stem: A keloid on the earlobe is to be excised and irradiated to lower the recurrence rate. When should the radiation be given?
A. Six weeks after excision, once the scar has fully matured
B. Only if the keloid recurs after a second excision
C. Within 24 to 48 hours after excision, before the scar fibroblasts re-enter the cycle
D. Two weeks before the excision, to shrink the lesion
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: Postexcision keloid radiotherapy is given within 24 to 48 hours, using superficial x-rays or low-energy electrons to roughly 12 to 20 Gy in three to five fractions, and it cuts recurrence from about half to roughly one in ten. Waiting six weeks lets the fibroblasts proliferate and misses the window. Reserving radiation for a second recurrence forfeits the benefit of the first excision. Preoperative radiation does not shrink a keloid and is not the standard sequence.

### Q2
Stem: A patient with Graves ophthalmopathy is referred for orbital radiotherapy. Which dose and technique fit?
A. 45 Gy in 25 fractions through anterior fields covering both globes
B. 20 Gy in 10 fractions through lateral opposed fields angled or half-beam blocked behind the lens
C. A single fraction of 8 Gy to the retro-orbital tissue
D. 7 Gy in 1 fraction the day before orbital decompression surgery
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Orbital radiotherapy for Graves disease treats the inflamed extraocular muscles and orbital fat with 20 Gy in 10 fractions through lateral opposed fields whose anterior edge is placed behind the lens, by posterior angulation or a half-beam block. Anterior fields covering the globes would irradiate the lens directly. A single 8 Gy fraction is a palliative schedule, and 7 Gy the day before surgery is the heterotopic ossification prophylaxis schedule.

### Q3
Stem: A man is about to start an antiandrogen for prostate cancer and is offered prophylactic breast radiation. What is the key point about timing?
A. It should be given six months into therapy, once breast tissue is present
B. It is only useful after bilateral mastectomy
C. It should be delayed until gynecomastia becomes painful
D. It is given before or as the antiandrogen starts, because established breast enlargement does not regress with radiation
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: Prophylactic breast irradiation uses 10 to 15 Gy in one to five fractions of electrons to the breast buds given before or at the start of hormone therapy. Once gynecomastia has developed, radiation relieves tenderness but does not reverse the growth, so waiting six months or waiting for pain defeats the purpose. Mastectomy has nothing to do with prophylaxis.

### Q4
Stem: Which schedule is used to prevent heterotopic ossification after hip surgery?
A. A single fraction of 7 to 8 Gy, given the day before surgery or within about 72 hours after it
B. 30 Gy in 10 fractions beginning two weeks after surgery
C. 50 Gy in 25 fractions beginning once ossification is visible on x-ray
D. A single fraction of 20 Gy at six weeks after surgery
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: The target is the pluripotent mesenchymal cell before it differentiates into bone-forming tissue, so a single 7 to 8 Gy fraction is given preoperatively or within about three days after the operation. Beginning two weeks later misses that window. Treating once ossification is visible is too late, and 50 Gy is a therapeutic cancer dose. A 20 Gy single fraction is far above what the indication needs.

### Q5
Stem: A child has undergone nephrectomy for Wilms tumor and needs flank radiation. By when should treatment start?
A. Within 24 hours of surgery
B. Within about 10 days, and no later than postoperative day 14
C. After the full course of adjuvant chemotherapy, at about 3 months
D. At any time within the first year, since the dose is low
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: Flank radiotherapy for Wilms tumor begins by about postoperative day 9 or 10 and never later than day 14, because the National Wilms Tumor Study found more abdominal relapses when radiation was delayed. Starting within 24 hours is unnecessary and the wound has not healed. Waiting until chemotherapy is finished or treating any time in the first year violates the protocol timing.

### Q6
Stem: A patient with oral cavity cancer has several non-restorable teeth. When should they be extracted relative to the radiotherapy course?
A. Within 48 hours before the first fraction
B. Midway through radiotherapy, during the planned break
C. Six months after radiotherapy, once mucositis has resolved
D. About 10 to 14 days, and up to three weeks, before radiotherapy begins
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: Extractions are done 10 to 14 days, and up to three weeks, before the course so the sockets heal in unirradiated bone. Forty-eight hours is not enough healing time. Extracting mid-course or after a mandibular dose of 60 Gy or more invites osteoradionecrosis, which is exactly what the pre-treatment dental clearance is meant to prevent.

### Q7
Stem: A choroidal melanoma measures 6 mm in apical height and 12 mm in basal diameter. Which management does the Collaborative Ocular Melanoma Study support?
A. Iodine-125 episcleral plaque to 85 Gy at the tumor apex, or proton therapy, with survival equal to enucleation
B. Enucleation, because any melanoma over 5 mm thick must be removed
C. External beam photon radiotherapy to the whole orbit, 60 Gy in 30 fractions
D. Observation until the tumor exceeds 16 mm in diameter
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: The tumor falls in the medium category, 2.5 to 10 mm thick and 16 mm or less in diameter, and the COMS medium trial showed that an iodine-125 plaque delivering 85 Gy to the apex gives the same survival as enucleation while keeping the eye; protons are the alternative eye-sparing modality. Enucleation is reserved for large tumors thicker than about 10 mm or wider than about 16 mm. Whole-orbit photon treatment is not a melanoma technique, and observing a medium tumor until it becomes large is not an option.

### Q8
Stem: Six weeks after completing cranial irradiation, a child becomes drowsy and lethargic, eats poorly, and runs a low-grade fever. Imaging shows no new mass or edema. What is the most likely explanation?
A. Acute cerebral edema, which requires urgent high-dose steroids
B. Late radiation necrosis, which requires surgical decompression
C. The somnolence syndrome, a self-limited subacute reaction appearing 1 to 12 weeks after cranial radiation
D. Tumor progression, since radiation effects never produce fever
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: The somnolence syndrome appears 1 to 12 weeks after cranial irradiation, most often at 4 to 8 weeks, especially in children after whole-brain or prophylactic cranial treatment, and resolves on its own over days to weeks; it is attributed to transient demyelination. The acute edema reaction occurs during the course itself and responds to steroids. Radiation necrosis is a late effect at months to years. Progression is excluded by the normal imaging, and low-grade fever is part of the syndrome.

### Q9
Stem: In which quadrant does breast cancer most often arise, and roughly what fraction of cases does it account for?
A. Upper inner quadrant, about 40 percent
B. Upper outer quadrant, about 50 percent
C. Lower outer quadrant, about 35 percent
D. Central subareolar region, about 60 percent
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: About half of breast cancers arise in the upper outer quadrant, which contains the most glandular tissue and the axillary tail. The upper inner quadrant accounts for roughly 15 percent, the lower outer about 10 percent, the lower inner about 5 percent, and the central region the remainder. None of the other quadrants comes close to half.

### Q10
Stem: Which statement about lip cancer is correct?
A. The upper lip is the most common site because it is more exposed to the sun
B. Small lip lesions are cured only by surgery; radiation is reserved for inoperable disease
C. Lip lesions drain first to the level V posterior triangle nodes
D. About 90 percent arise on the lower lip, and small lesions are cured about equally well by surgery or radiation
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: The sun-exposed lower lip accounts for about 90 percent of lip cancers. Lesions under about 2 cm are controlled roughly nine times in ten by either surgery or radiation, so the choice turns on cosmesis and function, with radiation favored for commissure involvement and for lesions spanning more than a third of the lip. The lower lip drains to the submental and submandibular nodes of level I, not to level V.

### Q11
Stem: Which is the most common oral cavity subsite for squamous cell carcinoma, and which is second?
A. Oral tongue first, floor of mouth second
B. Hard palate first, buccal mucosa second
C. Floor of mouth first, retromolar trigone second
D. Alveolar ridge first, oral tongue second
Answer: A
Objective: III.2
Why: The oral tongue, the anterior two thirds, is the most common oral cavity subsite and the floor of mouth is second. The alveolar ridge, buccal mucosa, retromolar trigone, and hard palate follow, with the hard palate the least common. Note that the base of tongue is oropharynx, not oral cavity, and does not enter this count.

### Q12
Stem: A small floor-of-mouth cancer will receive an intraoral electron cone boost plus external beam fields. In which order are they usually delivered, and why?
A. External fields first, so the cone treats only the residual nidus
B. Cone and external fields alternate daily throughout the course
C. Cone boost first, while the tumor is still visible for cone placement and before mucositis and trismus make the cone intolerable
D. Cone boost last, after a two-week rest to let the mucosa recover
Answer: C
Objective: III.2
Why: The intraoral cone is placed over the lesion under direct vision, so the boost is given before the large fields, while the tumor is still visible and palpable and the mouth can open comfortably. After external beam treatment the tumor has flattened, the mucosa is tender, and trismus may prevent cone placement. Alternating daily or boosting after a rest both put the cone into an inflamed mouth. Typical boosts use 6 to 12 MeV electrons for about 15 to 20 Gy.

### Q13
Stem: A patient is diagnosed with a paranasal sinus carcinoma. Which sinus is the most likely site of origin, and roughly what fraction of sinus cancers arise there?
A. Ethmoid sinus, about 60 percent
B. Frontal sinus, about 40 percent
C. Sphenoid sinus, about 30 percent
D. Maxillary sinus, about 70 percent
Answer: D
Objective: III.2
Why: The maxillary sinus accounts for about 70 percent of paranasal sinus cancers, and the ethmoid for most of the remainder. Frontal and sphenoid primaries are rare. Squamous cell carcinoma is the dominant histology, and ethmoid adenocarcinoma is the hardwood dust occupational tumor.

### Q14
Stem: Compare the elective nodal treatment for a T1 glottic cancer with that for a T1 supraglottic cancer.
A. Both require bilateral neck irradiation of levels II through IV
B. The glottic T1 receives no elective nodes because the glottis has almost no lymphatics; the supraglottic T1 receives bilateral levels II and III because the supraglottis drains richly to both sides
C. The glottic T1 receives bilateral level II and III; the supraglottic T1 is treated with larynx-only fields
D. Neither receives elective nodes, since both are T1
Answer: B
Objective: III.2
Why: The glottis has a sparse lymphatic supply, so a T1 glottic cancer has a nodal risk under about 2 percent and is treated with small opposed fields covering the larynx alone. The supraglottis drains bilaterally to levels II and III, and onward to level IV, with nodes present in a quarter to half of cases at diagnosis, so both sides of the neck are treated electively even for small primaries. Reversing the two sites, treating both, or treating neither all misread the lymphatic anatomy.
