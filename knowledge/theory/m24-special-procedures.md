# Module 24. Special Treatment Procedures

TBI, total skin electrons, and SRS/SBRT. Doses are typical regimens to recognize, not prescriptions. A transplant program's dose rate and lung limit come from that protocol. The stereotactic numbers written in this note are the ones this exam teaches. Do not move them onto a different organ or a different protocol.

## Total Body Irradiation (TBI)

**MDCB objectives:** III.6

**What the exam expects**

You recognize a typical TBI prescription of about 12 Gy in 6 to 8 fractions and you know the lung is the organ that forces the technique. You can describe extended SSD, opposed fields, a beam spoiler, compensation for thickness, and why the dose rate is lowered. You do not invent a universal dose-rate cap.

**Core facts**

TBI is used mainly before stem-cell transplant: immunosuppression, marrow ablation, and treatment of residual disease. A typical course is 12 Gy in 6 fractions of 2 Gy or in 8 fractions of 1.5 Gy, twice daily, over several days. Other schedules exist. Recognize 12 Gy and the fraction count in that band.

The patient is treated at an extended SSD, often several meters, so the whole body fits in the field. Opposed anterior–posterior beams are the common arrangement. Opposed laterals are the alternative. Tissue thickness is not constant: the head, neck, lungs, and legs differ. Without compensation the midplane dose varies with separation. Compensators, bolus, or segmented fields even that out. In-vivo dosimetry (diodes or TLDs) checks the result on the day.

Lungs are the planning problem. They are low density, so a calculation that ignores inhomogeneity underestimates lung dose, and even a corrected plan often shows the lungs at or above the prescription unless something is done. Techniques include lung blocks with an electron boost to the chest wall under the block, reduced transmission blocks, or arms placed at the sides as a natural compensator. The lung dose goal is protocol-specific. Confirm it in the transplant protocol. Kidneys are sometimes blocked for the same reason.

Skin dose at a large SSD with a high-energy beam would otherwise be low, and the skin and superficial tissues need dose. A beam spoiler, a slab of plastic near the patient, adds scatter and raises the surface dose.

Dose rate is lowered on purpose. Pulmonary toxicity rises with dose rate on these large fields. Many programs treat at a midplane dose rate on the order of 10 cGy per minute or less, far below an ordinary linac treatment. The cap is written in the transplant protocol. Confirm it there. Do not quote one cGy/min figure as if every program shared it.

The calculation itself is a midplane dose at extended distance: output under the TBI calibration conditions, inverse square from the TBI reference distance, tissue-phantom ratio or an equivalent depth for the separation, and a spoiler or compensator factor if one is in the beam. The full MU arithmetic is practiced with the calculation modules. The special-procedure skill is knowing which factors exist and why the lungs are measured, not only calculated.

At 12 Gy in 6 fractions the dose per fraction is 2 Gy, so EQD2 equals 12 Gy for any α/β. At 12 Gy in 8 fractions, d = 1.5 Gy. Late α/β = 3:

EQD2 = 12 × (1.5 + 3) / (2 + 3) = 12 × 4.5 / 5 = 10.8 Gy.

The fractionation detail matters less on the exam than the lung block and the dose rate. Still show the substitution if a stem asks which TBI schedule is hotter per gray of late effect: 2 Gy fractions are hotter than 1.5 Gy fractions for the same total dose.

**Traps**

- Treating TBI as a normal isocentric plan with a huge field. Geometry, calibration, and dose rate are different.
- Forgetting the lung, or forgetting that lung blocks then need a chest-wall electron boost so the chest wall is not shielded for the whole course.
- Leaving the spoiler out of a discussion of skin dose.
- Quoting a single legal dose rate. Confirm the protocol.
- Ignoring in-vivo measurements on a technique this dependent on thickness and distance.

**Sources**

- AAPM TG-29, the classic total-body irradiation report, for technique and dosimetry
- The transplant protocol in the stem, for fraction schedule, lung dose, and dose rate

## Total Skin Electron Irradiation (TSEB)

**MDCB objectives:** III.6, I.3

**What the exam expects**

You can describe the Stanford technique as six patient positions and say why six positions exist. You know the beam is an electron beam degraded for a superficial skin dose, that some surfaces self-shield, and that eyes and nails are shielded. A single total dose is not the fact being tested.

**Core facts**

Total skin electron irradiation treats cutaneous lymphoma over the whole skin surface while sparing deep organs. The useful range is millimeters to about a centimeter of skin, not a deep 90% line of the kind used for a chest-wall scar. A nominal linac electron energy, often 6 MeV, is degraded with a scatterer or a spoiler at a large SSD so the energy at the patient is lower and the depth-dose matches skin. The degraded energy and the millimeter depth of the 80% dose are set at commissioning. Confirm them in the departmental technique and in AAPM TG-30 rather than memorizing one depth for every booth.

The Stanford technique uses six patient positions: anterior, posterior, and four obliques (RAO, LAO, RPO, LPO). Six positions surround the body so each patch of skin is hit by more than a glancing beam. At each position the Stanford method uses a dual field, one angled up and one angled down, so the vertical profile is flat along a standing patient. Six positions times two angles is twelve fields. The phrase to recognize is six positions. The dual angles explain how the dose stays even from head to knee.

Surfaces the beams do not see well need boosts: vertex of the scalp, soles, perineum, and skin folds such as the inframammary fold and the inner thighs. Eyes are shielded. Nails are often shielded. Hands and feet may be in a separate position or a separate boost. Underdosed strips between fields are a known failure; the six positions and the dual angles exist to prevent them.

A commonly quoted Stanford course is on the order of the mid-30 Gy range, protracted, with boosts on top. Treat that as recognition, not as a prescription. Confirm the total dose, the dose per cycle, and the boost doses in the departmental Stanford technique.

Electron rules from the isodose module still apply. The therapeutic depth is an 80–90% line, here intentionally shallow. Lead thickness in millimeters is about E/2 at the energy that actually reaches the patient, which is the degraded energy, not always the nominal 6 MeV selected on the console. Internal eye shields, if used, stay in the low-energy regime of about 9 MeV and under because of backscatter. Many total-skin setups shield the eyes externally instead.

**Traps**

- Answering “one standing anterior field” or “a single arc” for Stanford. Six positions.
- Forgetting the dual up-and-down fields and then being unable to explain vertical uniformity.
- Prescribing a deep electron energy so the 90% line is several centimeters down. That is a chest-wall energy, not a skin-surface energy.
- Missing the self-shielded boost sites.
- Using the nominal console energy in the lead rule after a degrader has lowered the energy at the patient.

**Sources**

- AAPM TG-30, total skin electron therapy
- The departmental Stanford technique, for dose, degraded energy, and boost fields

## Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)

**MDCB objectives:** III.6, III.1

**What the exam expects**

You can separate single-fraction radiosurgery from fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy and from body stereotactic treatment. You know the margin is tight because the dose falls off fast, and you know why a Gamma Knife plan is prescribed to a low isodose line. You can name Winston-Lutz and the 1 mm tolerance that ties the radiation isocenter to the mechanical isocenter. You can say, in words, what the RTOG 0813 and RTOG 0915 plan metrics ask: conformity index, R50%, D2cm, and lung V20. You can quote chest-wall V30 under 30 cc and the single-fraction brachial-plexus limit of 17.5 Gy. You can describe 4DCT, the internal target volume, and the maximum-intensity projection. You know a detector that is too big underestimates the center of a small field. You can separate three translations from six degrees of freedom, and a frame from frameless imaging. For hippocampal avoidance you can state the RTOG 0933 pair: D100% at or below 9 Gy and maximum dose at or below 16 Gy, on whole-brain 30 Gy in 10 fractions.

**Core facts**

SRS is a single high dose to a small intracranial target, localized in a stereotactic coordinate system. Fractionated stereotactic treatment spreads that idea over a few fractions when the target is larger or sits against a serial organ. SBRT is the body version: high dose, few fractions, tight margin, steep gradient. Peripheral lung schedules to recognize (50–54 Gy in 3 to 5 fractions) are in the oncology module. This lesson is the delivery system and the planning logic.

Platforms:

- Gamma Knife: many cobalt-60 sources, a helmet or cones, a frame or a mask. The prescription isodose is often the 50% line, so the center of the target is about twice the prescription dose. That inhomogeneity is intentional. It is the opposite of the old ICRU 95–107% photon goal.
- Linac SRS: cones or a high-definition MLC, frame or mask, often prescribed near the 80% line rather than 50%.
- Robotic linac (CyberKnife style): non-isocentric beams from many nodes, image guidance throughout.

The margin is small because the penumbra and the falloff are steep. A 5 mm expansion that would be ordinary for a palliative spine field would irradiate a shell of brain or lung the stereotactic plan exists to avoid. The steepness only helps if the aim is right. Image guidance, a frame, or both have to hold the target inside that tight margin. A DVH does not prove the aim. It also does not prove the dose grid was fine enough to describe a steep edge. Stereotactic commissioning uses a finer grid and a small detector.

RTOG 90-05 is the recognizable single-fraction escalation for recurrent brain metastases previously irradiated: 24 Gy for lesions 20 mm or smaller, 18 Gy for 21–30 mm, and 15 Gy for 31–40 mm. Those are typical recognition values from that trial, not a prescription for every new metastasis. Confirm the dose in the protocol you are actually following.

The Winston-Lutz test places a small ball at the mechanical isocenter and images it with a small field from several gantry, collimator, and couch angles. For stereotactic work the radiation center and the mechanical center have to agree within 1 mm. A result larger than 1 mm fails that tolerance. A daily laser check is not a Winston-Lutz test.

Small-field dosimetry is a detector-size problem. A chamber that is large compared with the field averages the high-dose center with the penumbra and reads low. The field is also missing lateral electronic equilibrium, so the output is not a scaled 10 by 10 factor. Commission cones and small multileaf apertures with a detector that is small relative to the field. A Farmer chamber underestimates the center.

A standard couch correction is three translations: lateral, longitudinal, and vertical. Six degrees of freedom add pitch, roll, and yaw. A translation-only shift leaves a rotation in the setup. Away from the isocenter that rotation moves the target by a distance a stereotactic margin does not contain. A frame fixes the coordinate system to the skull. If the frame slips, the coordinates no longer describe the head that will be treated. Frameless delivery uses a mask and images the skull at treatment. The mask limits motion. The images locate the target. Frameless does not relax Winston-Lutz. The radiation and mechanical isocenters still have to agree within 1 mm.

End-to-end QA runs the chain from imaging through localization to dose. Winston-Lutz within 1 mm is the isocenter piece of that chain. One leaf position does not prove the chain.

**SBRT plan metrics.** RTOG 0813 and RTOG 0915 are the lung stereotactic protocols this exam uses for four plan-quality checks. The conformity index compares the volume enclosed by the prescription isodose with the PTV volume. Near 1, the prescription dose hugs the target. Well above 1, prescription dose is spilled outside the PTV. R50% compares the volume enclosed by half the prescription dose with the PTV volume. It is the intermediate-dose falloff. A large value means a big 50% cloud for the size of the target. The value a protocol will accept depends on PTV size. There is no single R50% cutoff here. D2cm is the highest dose 2 cm outside the PTV, judged against the prescription dose. It catches a hot finger of high dose. It is not the same question as R50%, which looks at the whole half-prescription volume. Lung V20 is the portion of lung that receives 20 Gy or more. It sits beside the conformity metrics as a lung-sparing check. It is not a conformity index.

Two numeric constraints from this set are the ones to memorize. Chest wall: the volume receiving 30 Gy stays under 30 cc. Brachial plexus, single fraction, in the RTOG 0915 style this exam cites: 17.5 Gy. Do not move 17.5 Gy onto the chest wall, the cord, or a fraction schedule the stem did not call a single fraction. Do not move the 30 cc chest-wall limit onto another organ.

**Breathing, in one pass.** A 4DCT sorts the breathing cycle into phases. The internal target volume is the union of the target across those phases, the envelope of motion. A maximum-intensity projection keeps the brightest value of each voxel over the phases, so a lung tumor paints its path and the ITV can be drawn from that projection. Check the contour on the individual phases. The projection can add tissue the tumor never occupied, and a bad window can hide a phase. The PTV margin on that ITV is for setup. It is not a second copy of the same breathing motion.

**Hippocampal avoidance.** RTOG 0933 is whole-brain radiotherapy, 30 Gy in 10 fractions, with the hippocampi spared. The constraints are D100% at or below 9 Gy and a maximum dose at or below 16 Gy. D100% means the entire hippocampus is kept to 9 Gy or less. The maximum is the hottest point, kept to 16 Gy or less. The 30 Gy prescription is the whole-brain dose outside that avoidance. Do not replace this pair with the single-fraction metastasis doses from RTOG 90-05, and do not invent another protocol number for them.

**Intraoperative radiation, briefly.** The module title includes IORT. There is no separate lesson. IORT delivers one fraction to an exposed tumor bed in the operating room, with electrons or with a low-energy kilovoltage source on the surface. Dose falls off quickly in tissue. The skin is reflected out of the field and shielded. The planning problem is surface contact, depth, and the structures still in the wound, not a fractionated isocentric course.

**Traps**

- Expanding an SRS or SBRT target with a conventional margin because the falloff “has room.”
- Prescribing a Gamma Knife plan to the 100% isodose and calling the hot center an error. The hot center is the technique when the prescription line is 50%.
- Using a Farmer chamber to commission a cone, and trusting the low reading at the center.
- Calling a laser agreement a Winston-Lutz test, or accepting a Winston-Lutz residual above 1 mm.
- Calling a three-translation couch correction a six-degree-of-freedom correction.
- Treating a frame slip as still valid, or skipping imaging on a frameless plan.
- Treating the conformity index, R50%, and D2cm as three names for one number.
- Drawing the ITV on the maximum-intensity projection and never opening the 4DCT phases, or adding the breathing excursion a second time as the PTV margin.
- Moving 17.5 Gy or the chest-wall 30 cc limit onto the wrong organ.
- Mixing RTOG 90-05 doses with an intact-brain protocol that never authorized them, or quoting a hippocampal limit from a protocol other than RTOG 0933.
- Treating IORT like TBI. One is a superficial intraoperative field. The other is whole-body photons.

**Sources**

- AAPM TG-42, stereotactic radiosurgery
- AAPM TG-101, stereotactic body radiation therapy, for the technical framework. This lesson does not reproduce a full organ table from that report.
- RTOG 90-05, for the size-based single-fraction doses cited above
- RTOG 0813 and RTOG 0915, for the conformity index, R50%, D2cm, and lung V20, for chest-wall V30 under 30 cc, and for the single-fraction brachial plexus limit of 17.5 Gy
- RTOG 0933, hippocampal avoidance during whole-brain 30 Gy in 10 fractions: hippocampal D100% at or below 9 Gy and maximum dose at or below 16 Gy
- ICRU 91, prescribing and reporting stereotactic treatments, for how the inhomogeneous dose is described

## Special Treatment Procedures Quiz

**MDCB objectives:** III.6

**Skills this quiz trains**

- TBI: about 12 Gy in 6–8 fractions, extended SSD, lung consideration, spoiler, lowered dose rate, in-vivo dosimetry. Confirm the rate and the lung dose in the protocol.
- TSEB: Stanford six positions, dual fields for uniformity, degraded electrons, shields, and boosts to self-shielded skin.
- SRS/SBRT: single fraction versus a short course, tight margin because falloff is steep, 50% versus about 80% prescription lines, Winston-Lutz within 1 mm, small-field detectors that underestimate the center when they are too big.
- Recognize 24 Gy, 18 Gy, and 15 Gy as the RTOG 90-05 size bins when the stem is that trial.
- RTOG 0813/0915 metrics in words: conformity index (prescription-isodose volume against the PTV), R50% (half-prescription volume against the PTV), D2cm (highest dose 2 cm outside the PTV), lung V20 (lung receiving 20 Gy or more). Chest wall V30 under 30 cc. Single-fraction brachial plexus 17.5 Gy.
- 4DCT phases, ITV as the motion envelope, MIP as a brightest-voxel composite that still needs a phase check. Six degrees of freedom versus translations. Frame versus frameless.
- RTOG 0933: whole-brain 30 Gy in 10 fractions, hippocampal D100% at or below 9 Gy, hippocampal maximum at or below 16 Gy.
- Say what IORT is in one sentence so it is not confused with TBI or TSEB.

**Traps**

- A universal TBI dose rate.
- Stanford described as one electron energy and one position.
- A wide PTV on a stereotactic plan, or a second breathing margin stacked on an ITV.
- Gamma Knife homogeneity judged by the ICRU 50 ±7%/−5% rule.
- A Winston-Lutz residual above 1 mm called acceptable, or a Farmer chamber used on a cone.
- Conformity index, R50%, and D2cm swapped, or 17.5 Gy assigned to the chest wall.
- Nominal 6 MeV used for total-skin depth after a degrader.

**Check yourself**

Which organ forces compensators or blocks on TBI, how many patient positions does Stanford total skin use, and why is an SRS margin smaller than a conventional brain margin? What does a conformity index far above 1 mean, and what hippocampal limits does RTOG 0933 cite?

Walk-through: Lungs. Six positions. The dose gradient is steep, so a conventional expansion would bathe normal brain that the stereotactic setup is precise enough to spare. The precision has to be real: a frame or image guidance, and Winston-Lutz within 1 mm. A conformity index well above 1 means the prescription isodose is large compared with the PTV. RTOG 0933 is 30 Gy in 10 fractions to the whole brain, with hippocampal D100% at or below 9 Gy and maximum dose at or below 16 Gy.

## TBI Dose Rate, Uniformity, and Energy Rules

**Objectives:** III.6

**What the exam expects**

The TBI lesson above tells you which problems exist. This lesson gives the numbers the exam keys on: the classic midplane dose-rate band, the uniformity target, the in-vivo acceptance window, the energy rule for a thick patient, and how a lung block is sized at an extended distance.

**Core facts**

Dose rate. The classic teaching figure for fractionated TBI is a midplane dose rate of about 5–10 cGy per minute, obtained by treating at 3–4 m or more and by lowering the machine dose rate. Lower dose rate at the lung is associated with less interstitial pneumonitis, which is why the band is quoted. Some programs accept up to about 20 cGy/min with fractionation and lung shielding. The bank's exam figure is 5–10 cGy/min; the protocol is the authority in a clinical stem.

Uniformity. The planning goal is a midplane dose within about plus or minus 10 percent of the prescription dose along the whole body axis, which is the same statement as holding the hottest region under about 110 percent. Compensators over the thinner parts (neck, legs) and lung shielding over the thorax are how the 10 percent is met. The ICRU 50 photon goal of −5/+7 percent is not applied to TBI.

In-vivo dosimetry. Diodes, TLDs, or OSLDs are placed at the prescription point and at representative sites on the first fraction, and the readings are expected to agree with the calculated dose within about plus or minus 5 percent. A reading outside 5 percent triggers a compensator adjustment or a recalculation before the next fraction. The 5 percent is a measurement acceptance window; the 10 percent is the planning uniformity target. Do not swap them.

Energy and separation. For anterior–posterior opposed fields at an extended distance, higher energy flattens the midplane-to-entrance ratio for a thick separation, so among the usual choices 15 MV (or 18 MV) AP/PA gives the most homogeneous dose through the trunk. The practical rule: if the largest separation along the beam is under about 35 cm and the treatment distance is at least 3 m, 6 MV gives acceptable midplane uniformity; thicker separations, or lateral opposed fields that cross the shoulders and pelvis, favor 10–18 MV. Lateral fields are chosen for patient comfort and for keeping the arms as lung compensators, at the cost of a larger separation. A spoiler brings the skin dose back whatever the energy.

Lung blocks. The block has to project onto the lung at the patient, so it is drawn from the simulation film or the DRR and scaled by the ratio of tray distance to patient distance. Divergence is handled the same way as any other block: the block at the tray is smaller than the lung by the ratio of the distances. A partial-transmission block (for example 50 percent transmission) or a full block used for only some of the fractions limits the lung to the protocol dose while the rest of the body receives the prescription. Tissue under a full-transmission block then needs the chest-wall electron boost described in the earlier lesson. In-vivo dosimetry behind the block confirms the lung dose.

**Formulas**

Block scaling at extended distance: block size at the tray = size at the patient × (tray distance / patient distance).

A lung is 22 cm long at the midplane, the midplane is 400 cm from the source, and the shadow tray is 60 cm from the source. Block length = 22 × 60 / 400 = 3.3 cm. Check: 3.3 × 400 / 60 = 22 cm. A block drawn at 22 cm would shadow more than half the body.

**Traps**

- Quoting the in-vivo 5 percent window as the uniformity goal, or the 10 percent uniformity goal as the in-vivo tolerance.
- Choosing 6 MV AP/PA for a 45 cm separation at 3 m and expecting the midplane to stay within 10 percent.
- Drawing the lung block at the lung's actual size when it sits on a tray near the head.
- Treating 5–10 cGy/min as a regulatory limit. It is the classic teaching band; the protocol governs.

**Sources**

- AAPM TG-29, total-body irradiation
- Khan and Gibbons, TBI chapter, for the energy-separation rule and the in-vivo practice
- The transplant protocol in a stem, which overrides every number here

## TSET Geometry, IORT, Hemibody, and Electron Arc

**Objectives:** III.6, I.3

**What the exam expects**

You can explain why the Stanford dual fields are angled off the horizontal in terms of x-ray contamination, state the typical IORT single dose and the devices that deliver it, describe hemibody irradiation as a procedure, and give the handful of electron-arc facts the exam uses.

**Core facts**

TSET beam geometry. The bremsstrahlung contamination of a degraded electron beam is largest along the central axis and falls off toward the edges. If one horizontal field were aimed at the patient's center, that photon core would sit on the trunk. The Stanford technique instead uses a dual field at each of the six positions: one beam angled about 15–20 degrees above horizontal and one the same amount below, so the two central axes pass above the head and below the feet. The electron fields overlap to give a flat vertical profile over the standing patient and the x-ray contamination is pushed off the body. Six positions times two angles is twelve fields. The nominal 6 MeV beam reaches the patient at about 3–4 MeV after the scatterer and the air path, dmax moves toward the surface, the 80 percent depth is on the order of 5–10 mm, and the midplane photon dose over a course is kept to about 1 percent of the skin dose. Eyes are shielded with external or internal shields and nails are shielded because nail loss is a known toxicity.

IORT. Intraoperative radiotherapy delivers a single large fraction, typically 10–20 Gy, to an exposed tumor bed with the bowel and skin moved out of the field. Three delivery families exist: electron beams (a dedicated mobile linac or a conventional linac with a cone docked to the wound), low-energy x-rays (a 50 kV source in a spherical applicator placed in the cavity), and HDR brachytherapy applicators laid on the bed. Electron IORT uses rigid cones; a bevelled cone follows a sloping surface and shifts the isodose lines so the penumbra on the bevel side is wider. Energies of 6–12 MeV cover a bed a few centimeters deep, and the dose is prescribed to the 90 percent line. Lucite cones also absorb bremsstrahlung from the scraper edge. The single dose is combined with external beam in most breast and pancreas programs.

Hemibody. Hemibody irradiation treats widespread painful bone metastases in one large field covering either the upper half (above the umbilicus) or the lower half of the body, at an extended source–axis distance so the field covers the length. Classic single doses are about 6 Gy to the upper half (the lung limits it) and 8 Gy to the lower half. Premedication with antiemetics and steroids is standard, and the two halves are separated by about 4–6 weeks for marrow recovery. The calculation is the extended-distance arithmetic from the calculation modules; the procedure name and the two doses are what this lesson adds.

Electron arc. Electron arc therapy treats a curved surface (chest wall, scalp) with a rotating electron beam. The isocenter is placed at depth inside the patient so that the distance from the virtual source to the skin is similar at every angle, and the arc is extended about 10–15 degrees beyond the target edges to make up for the dose falloff at the ends of the arc. The field is narrow in the direction of rotation, about 4–8 cm. Because each point is swept by the beam at different angles (the velocity effect), dmax is deeper than for the same energy in a static field. Lead shielding on the skin at the arc limits is about E/2 plus 1 mm, slightly thicker than the static-field rule, because the obliquely incident electrons are more penetrating at the edge.

**Traps**

- Placing the TSET central axis on the trunk. The photon core goes above the head and below the feet.
- Quoting IORT as a fractionated course or as a dose of a few gray.
- Giving the lower hemibody dose to the upper half; the lung sets the upper limit at about 6 Gy.
- Using the static E/2 lead rule at the edge of an electron arc, or stopping the arc at the target edge.

**Sources**

- AAPM TG-30, total skin electron therapy
- AAPM TG-48 and TG-72, intraoperative electron beam therapy
- Khan and Gibbons, electron arc therapy and hemibody irradiation sections

## Stereotactic Tolerances and Prescription Isodose

**Objectives:** III.6

**What the exam expects**

You can give the TG-142 isocenter-coincidence tolerance for a stereotactic machine and for an ordinary machine, the TG-101 dose-grid recommendation, the range of isodose lines stereotactic plans are prescribed to, and why a circular cone has a smaller penumbra than an MLC aperture.

**Core facts**

TG-142 tolerances. TG-142 separates machines used for SRS/SBRT from non-stereotactic machines. The monthly coincidence of the radiation isocenter with the mechanical isocenter, and the coincidence of the imaging isocenter with the treatment isocenter, is 1 mm for an SRS/SBRT machine and 2 mm for a non-SRS machine. The same split appears for laser and couch position tolerances. The 1 mm figure is why Winston-Lutz is checked more often on a stereotactic machine and why a 1.5 mm result that is acceptable for conventional treatment fails for SRS.

TG-101 grid. TG-101 recommends a dose calculation grid of 2 mm or finer for SBRT, with 1 mm preferred for very small targets, because the dose falls by tens of percent over a few millimeters at the target edge and a coarse grid misses the maximum and misreports the conformity indices. The same reasoning places the small-field output factors in the hands of a small detector.

Prescription isodose. Stereotactic plans are prescribed to a line well below the maximum, typically 50–80 percent of the maximum dose. Gamma Knife plans sit near 50 percent; linac SRS and SBRT plans are more often 60–80 percent. Prescribing to a lower line steepens the dose gradient outside the target and sharpens the falloff into the adjacent organ, at the price of a hot center that is intentional. The homogeneity criteria of ICRU 50 and 83 do not apply.

Cones versus MLC. A circular cone is a thick, tapered collimator mounted close to the patient, so the geometric penumbra is small, there is no interleaf leakage and no rounded leaf-end transmission, and the small field is defined by a continuous edge. For a spherical target of a few millimeters, the cone gives a sharper beam edge than a multileaf aperture. An MLC wins when the target is irregular, when several targets share one isocenter, or when the field is too large for the cone set.

**Formulas**

Hot-spot dose from the prescription line: maximum dose = prescription dose / prescription isodose fraction.

An 18 Gy single fraction is prescribed to the 50 percent line: maximum = 18 / 0.50 = 36 Gy. The same 18 Gy to the 80 percent line gives 18 / 0.80 = 22.5 Gy. Check: 36 × 0.50 = 18 and 22.5 × 0.80 = 18.

**Traps**

- Applying the 2 mm non-SRS tolerance to a machine that treats SRS.
- Calculating an SBRT plan on a 3–4 mm grid and defending the conformity index it reports.
- Flagging a 200 percent center on a 50 percent prescription as a planning error.
- Choosing an MLC aperture over a cone for a 6 mm spherical metastasis on the grounds of penumbra.

**Sources**

- AAPM TG-142, quality assurance of medical accelerators, SRS/SBRT versus non-SRS columns
- AAPM TG-101, stereotactic body radiation therapy, dose grid and small-field dosimetry
- ICRU 91, prescribing and reporting small photon beam treatments
