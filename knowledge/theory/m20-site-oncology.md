# Module 20. Site-Specific Clinical Oncology

Every dose in this module is a typical U.S. regimen the exam expects you to recognize. None of them is a prescription. The prescription is the one signed for that patient. Constraint numbers belong to the named report or protocol (QUANTEC, TG-101, RTOG, Timmerman, ABS, EMBRACE). Confirm them there.

## Advice for Oncology Section

**MDCB objectives:** III.2, III.8

**What the exam expects**

You can hear a total dose and a fraction number and name the site and the intent. You can keep a palliative schedule from being used as a curative one, and you can name the organs that usually limit the plan. You are not expected to invent a constraint. You are expected to apply the constraint the stem quotes, to the right volume, in the right units.

**Core facts**

Read the site before you celebrate a familiar fraction size. Forty gray in fifteen fractions is a standard hypofractionated breast course and also a short-course glioblastoma schedule. Twenty-five gray in five fractions is short-course rectal radiotherapy and is not a peripheral lung SBRT dose. The fraction size is a clue. The organ in the stem is the answer key.

Group regimens by intent:

- Curative definitive doses sit near the tolerance of the surrounding organs and run for many fractions, or they are deliberately ablative and stereotactic with a steep edge.
- Preoperative and postoperative doses are lower than the definitive dose for the same disease, because surgery is doing part of the work.
- Palliative bone doses (8 Gy once, 20 Gy in 5, 30 Gy in 10) exist to relieve pain. They are not interchangeable with a curative EQD2. The radiobiology module shows the arithmetic.

Pair every site with the organs that actually shape the beam arrangement:

- Whole brain: lenses if the fields allow, and scalp. Hippocampal sparing is a plan variant, not the default 30 Gy course.
- GBM: brainstem, optic chiasm and nerves, eyes, and the uninvolved brain.
- Head and neck: parotids, spinal cord, brainstem, mandible, larynx, brachial plexus, constrictors.
- Breast: heart and the left anterior descending region on a left-sided case, ipsilateral lung, contralateral breast.
- Prostate: rectum, bladder, femoral heads, bowel, penile bulb.
- Cervix: rectum, sigmoid, bladder, bowel, and bone marrow, scored as EQD2 once brachytherapy is added.
- Rectum: small bowel, bladder, femoral heads.
- Lung SBRT: chest wall, brachial plexus, cord, esophagus, heart, great vessels, and the proximal bronchial tree. Peripheral and central are different problems.
- Spine palliation: the cord, especially after prior radiation.

Serial organs are limited by a high dose to a small volume. Parallel organs are limited by mean dose or by a volume above a threshold. Apply the metric the stem names. A mean-lung limit does not answer a cord-max question.

OAR numbers move between QUANTEC (conventional fractionation), TG-101 and Timmerman-style stereotactic tables, and individual protocols. This module teaches recognition of the tumor dose. When a question pairs that dose with a limit, use the limit printed in the stem and confirm anything you quote from memory against the named report.

Anatomy is half of this section. If you cannot place the rectum on an axial prostate slice, you cannot explain why a lateral beam or an arc spares it. That map is module 22.

**Traps**

- Memorizing one dose per organ and pasting it onto every fractionation.
- Treating 60 Gy in 20 fractions and 60 Gy in 30 fractions as the same prostate treatment.
- Calling every 8 Gy fraction stereotactic. A single 8 Gy is ordinary bone palliation.
- Ignoring laterality. A left breast tangent and a right breast tangent do not see the same heart.
- Quoting a universal pacemaker dose or a universal cord dose in a site question. Device limits are TG-203 and device-specific. Cord limits are the named conventional or stereotactic source.

**Sources**

- The disease-site protocols named in the question (RTOG, ABS, EMBRACE, departmental pathways)
- QUANTEC and AAPM TG-101 for the class of constraint, with the number confirmed in the report
- ICRU 83, because modern site plans are judged on D98, D50, D2 and on DVHs

## Overview (Notes)

**MDCB objectives:** III.2, III.8, III.3

**What the exam expects**

You can recognize the regimens below, state the intent, and name the planning problem each one creates. One numeric conversion is shown here so a hypofractionated prostate dose is not mistaken for a low-dose course. The rest of the BED arithmetic is in the radiobiology module.

**Core facts**

**Whole brain.** Typical palliative regimen: 30 Gy in 10 fractions. Poor-performance patients are sometimes treated with a shorter course such as 20 Gy in 5. The fields cover the whole brain, usually with a block or a collimator edge that respects the lenses when the technique allows. This is not a GBM dose.

**Glioblastoma.** Typical definitive course: 60 Gy in 30 fractions of 2 Gy to the planning volume defined by the protocol, often with a lower-dose region around edema or a smaller boost volume. Elderly or frail patients may receive a shorter course, including 40 Gy in 15. That 40 Gy in 15 is the trap overlap with breast hypofractionation. Read the site. The plan is limited by brainstem and optic structures if the tumor is near them.

**Head and neck, definitive.** Typical gross disease dose is about 70 Gy. Two recognizable deliveries are 70 Gy in 35 fractions and 69.96 Gy in 33 fractions of 2.12 Gy, the second often as a simultaneous integrated boost with a lower dose per fraction to intermediate-risk and elective nodes. Postoperative doses are lower, often about 60 Gy, with a higher pocket where margins are positive. The classic planning problem is a concave target wrapped around the cord and the parotids. Laterality decides which parotid can be spared.

Check the 33-fraction schedule against 2 Gy fractions so you can see it is still a “about 70 Gy” regimen. With α/β = 10,

EQD2 = 69.96 × (2.12 + 10) / (2 + 10) = 69.96 × 12.12 / 12 = 69.96 × 1.01 = 70.7 Gy.

It is about 70 Gy in 2 Gy fractions for a tumor α/β of 10, which is why both schedules are in the same recognition bin. The full BED method is module 21.

**Head and neck, simultaneous integrated boost.** The 35-fraction boost treats three volumes at once: 70 Gy to gross disease, 63 Gy to intermediate-risk disease, and 56 Gy to elective nodes. Per fraction that is 2.0 Gy, 1.8 Gy, and 1.6 Gy. The 33-fraction schedule above is a different boost. Its gross-disease fraction is 2.12 Gy, its intermediate dose is 59.4 Gy, and its elective dose is 54.12 Gy. Do not hang 63 Gy and 56 Gy on those 33 fractions. Postoperative dose at the primary site stays near 60 Gy.

The technique is 6 MV IMRT or VMAT, usually one isocenter, so the low neck does not need a separate supraclavicular match. Eighteen MV is a poor neck energy. Buildup is deeper, superficial nodes are underdosed, and the air column in the larynx adds lateral disequilibrium.

Elective nodes follow the primary:

- Nasopharynx: bilateral levels II–V and the retropharyngeal nodes.
- Oral cavity: levels I–III, bilateral when the primary approaches midline.
- Oropharynx: levels II–IV. Midline disease and base of tongue take both necks. A well-lateralized tonsil can take the ipsilateral neck.
- Hypopharynx, and larynx with subglottic extension: bilateral II–IV plus level VI, the anterior compartment between the carotids.
- Early glottic cancer on one vocal cord: no elective nodes. Opposed lateral 6 MV photons to about 63 Gy in 28 fractions. The arytenoids limit the posterior edge.

A comprehensive neck is limited by parotid mean dose, cord and brainstem near-maximum, mandible, constrictors, and the brachial plexus in the low neck.

**Breast.** Typical conventional whole-breast dose: 45–50.4 Gy, then a boost to the cavity. Typical hypofractionation: 40 Gy in 15 fractions, or 42.5 Gy in 16 fractions. Boost doses and whether a boost is used are protocol- and risk-dependent. Confirm the boost in the prescription you were given.

The planning problem is a pair of tangents that cover the breast and spare lung and heart, with a non-divergent posterior edge and a match to a supraclavicular field when nodes are treated. Left-sided cases are the cardiac cases. Deep-inspiration breath hold and heart blocking are techniques, not different diseases. Postmastectomy chest wall may be photons, electrons, or both. A photon–electron skin match is hot on the photon side and cold on the electron side.

**Prostate.** Typical conventional definitive dose: the high 70s Gy in 1.8–2 Gy fractions (courses such as 78 Gy or 79.2 Gy are the ones to recognize). Typical moderate hypofractionation: 60 Gy in 20 fractions of 3 Gy. Stereotactic prostate courses exist and use still larger fractions; do not file them under 60 Gy in 20.

The 60 Gy figure looks low next to 78 Gy until you convert it. Prostate tumors are often modeled with a low α/β, about 1.5 Gy, not the generic tumor value of 10. Using α/β = 1.5,

EQD2 = 60 × (3 + 1.5) / (2 + 1.5) = 60 × 4.5 / 3.5 = 60 × 9 / 7 = 77.1 Gy.

That sits in the same high-70s bin as a conventional course. Using the generic tumor α/β of 10 by mistake,

EQD2 = 60 × (3 + 10) / (2 + 10) = 60 × 13 / 12 = 65 Gy,

which makes 60 Gy in 20 look like a clear underdose. If the stem does not give α/β, say which value you used. The organs that limit the plan are rectum and bladder, then femoral heads and bowel.

**Prostate bed.** After prostatectomy the target is the fossa, not an intact gland. The volume runs from just below the vesicourethral anastomosis up through the seminal-vesicle bed, between the rectum and the bladder. The recognition dose is about 64–66 Gy at 1.8–2 Gy per fraction. Courses to know are 64.8 Gy in 36 fractions and 66 Gy in 33 fractions. That is lower than an intact course in the high 70s. The postoperative hypofractionated course is 52.5 Gy in 20 fractions. It is not the intact 60 Gy in 20. Rectum and bladder still limit the plan. Elective pelvic nodes, when the risk calls for them, are about 45 Gy to the iliac and obturator nodes. They are not inguinal nodes, and they do not raise the fossa to an intact-gland prescription.

**Cervix.** Typical external beam: 45 Gy to the pelvis, then brachytherapy that brings the high-risk CTV to a high EQD2. The external beam alone is not the treatment. Image-guided brachytherapy goals discussed with ABS and EMBRACE sit in the mid-to-high 80s Gy EQD2 (α/β = 10) to the high-risk CTV. Confirm the goal and the rectal, sigmoid, and bladder EQD2 limits in ABS guidance or the EMBRACE protocol. One worked combination is in the BED lesson: 45 Gy in 25 fractions plus an illustrative 7 Gy × 4 HDR course.

**Rectum.** Two typical preoperative regimens: 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions of 1.8 Gy, or short course 25 Gy in 5 fractions. They are both standard and they are not biologically equal. Short course is a one-week regimen. Long course is given with chemotherapy over about five and a half weeks. The planning volumes are the rectum and the mesorectum plus the nodal regions the stage requires, with small bowel as the organ that often limits the high dose.

**Lung, peripheral SBRT.** Typical peripheral schedules cluster at 50–54 Gy in 3 to 5 fractions. Recognizable examples are 54 Gy in 3, 50 Gy in 5, and 48 Gy in 4. Central and ultracentral tumors sit next to the bronchial tree, esophagus, and great vessels and use a milder schedule specified by the protocol. Confirm that central dose in the named protocol (the RTOG central-tumor experience is the reference class). Do not put a 3-fraction peripheral dose on an ultracentral target because the numbers were familiar.

The margin is tight because the falloff is steep. The dose grid has to be fine enough to describe that falloff. A DVH of a coarse grid does not prove the chest wall or the bronchus was resolved.

**Palliative bone.** Three typical schedules: 8 Gy in 1 fraction, 20 Gy in 5, and 30 Gy in 10. All are used for pain. They do not have the same late EQD2. For α/β = 3 the radiobiology lesson computes about 18 Gy, 28 Gy, and 36 Gy EQD2 respectively. Spine fields still respect prior cord dose. A single 8 Gy fraction to a bone met is not lung SBRT.

**Total body irradiation.** Often 12 Gy in 6 to 8 fractions, with an explicit lung consideration (blocks, compensators, or a reduced lung dose). Dose rate is lowered by protocol. Details are in the special-procedures module. Recognize the dose and the lung problem when they appear in an oncology question.

**Total skin electron irradiation.** Stanford technique uses 6 patient positions. Recognize the technique rather than a single universal skin dose. Details are in the special-procedures module.

**Other regimens.** These are worth recognizing so they do not get sorted into the list above.

- Esophagus, typical combined-modality dose: 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions. Same numbers as long-course rectum, different organs (heart, lungs, cord).
- Seminoma, typical stage I para-aortic dose: 20 Gy in 10 fractions. A low dose in the abdomen is not automatically palliative bone met coverage.
- Endometrial pelvic radiation often uses 45 Gy external beam, in the same recognition bin as cervix external beam, with a different brachytherapy applicator and a different boost question.

**Lymphoma, involved site.** After chemotherapy, involved-site radiotherapy treats the nodes that originally contained disease, with a margin, shaped to the post-chemotherapy anatomy. It does not add uninvolved mantle sites. The technique is 6 MV photons, an anterior-posterior pair or IMRT. Favorable early Hodgkin lymphoma is 20 Gy in 10 fractions. Unfavorable early Hodgkin lymphoma is 30 Gy in 15 fractions. Both are about 2 Gy per fraction and sit in the 20–30 Gy band. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma in complete response is often consolidated at 30 Gy. A mediastinal field is limited by heart, lungs, and breast. A neck field is limited by parotid, thyroid, and cord. An abdominal field is limited by the kidneys.

**Craniospinal irradiation.** Average-risk medulloblastoma is 23.4 Gy to the axis in 13 fractions of 1.8 Gy. High-risk disease is 36 Gy in 20 fractions of 1.8 Gy. The primary is then boosted to about 54 Gy, and that boost stays off the cord so the cord remains at the axis dose. Cranial fields cover the whole intracranial meninges, including the cribriform plate. A lens block that clips the cribriform plate is a geographic miss. Those cranial fields are matched to a posterior spine field whose inferior border covers the thecal sac on MRI. The junction is feathered by moving it through the course, so one cord segment does not hold the match. Spine energy is 6 MV. The canal is posterior, and a higher energy increases exit dose through thyroid, heart, bowel, and kidney. The kidneys limit that exit. A tall patient may also feather a second, spine-to-spine junction.

**Lung, conventional.** Definitive non-small-cell lung cancer with concurrent chemotherapy is 60 Gy in 30 fractions of 2 Gy. The technique is 6 MV IMRT or VMAT on a 4DCT, so motion is inside the volume. The target is the primary and the involved nodes, not a routine elective bath of the uninvolved mediastinum. Lungs (mean dose and the volume above 20 Gy), esophagus, heart, and cord limit the plan. An apical tumor adds the brachial plexus. This course is not peripheral stereotactic lung radiotherapy and it is not limited-stage small-cell radiotherapy.

**Lung, limited-stage small cell.** The twice-daily course is 45 Gy in 30 fractions of 1.5 Gy, two fractions a day at least 6 hours apart, with concurrent chemotherapy. A once-daily alternative is 66 Gy in 33 fractions. Cord, lungs, and esophagus limit the chest plan. The anterior-posterior pair is dropped, or the plan becomes IMRT, before the cord carries the full tumor dose. After a response, prophylactic cranial irradiation is 25 Gy in 10 fractions to the whole brain. It has no spine field. It is a different prescription from palliative whole-brain treatment of 30 Gy in 10. The lenses stay out of those cranial fields when the technique allows.

**Anal canal.** Squamous cell carcinoma is definitive chemoradiation with mitomycin and 5-FU. Capecitabine can stand in for 5-FU. The technique is 6 MV IMRT or VMAT. The volume includes the anal primary, the mesorectum, the pelvic nodes, and the inguinal nodes. A pelvic field that stops at the inguinal ligament misses the groins. Gross disease that is T3, T4, or node-positive is often about 54 Gy in 30 fractions of 1.8 Gy. A node-negative T2 primary is often 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions, with the same drugs and the same nodes. Small bowel and perineal skin limit the plan. Femoral heads limit the groin dose.

**Bladder.** Organ preservation follows a maximal transurethral resection and uses concurrent chemotherapy. Two recognition schedules are about 64 Gy in 32 fractions and 55 Gy in 20 fractions. The technique is 6 MV IMRT or VMAT. Whole-bladder treatment is often simulated with an empty bladder. A partial-bladder boost is often simulated with a full bladder so small bowel is pushed away. Node-negative organ preservation treats the bladder. Pelvic nodes are added for node-positive disease. Inguinal nodes are not the bladder volume. Electrons do not reach the bladder. Small bowel limits the high dose.

**Pancreas.** Conventional chemoradiation is 45–50.4 Gy, commonly 50.4 Gy in 28 fractions of 1.8 Gy, with chemotherapy. The technique is 6 MV IMRT, and motion is handled with a 4DCT or with breath hold. The 50.4 Gy target is the gross tumor. Regional nodes on that course are the celiac and superior mesenteric stations, not the inguinal nodes. The stereotactic alternative is often 33 Gy in 5 fractions to the gross target only, with no elective nodes. The duodenum limits both. Stomach, small bowel, kidneys, and liver are the other abdominal limits.

**Stomach.** Postoperative chemoradiation is 45 Gy in 25 fractions of 1.8 Gy with 5-FU. A tumor-bed boost can bring the total to 50.4 Gy. The volume covers the preoperative tumor bed, the anastomosis or the residual stomach, and the regional nodes along the celiac axis and the porta hepatis. It does not include inguinal nodes. The technique is 6 MV IMRT or a multi-field arrangement. A simple anterior-posterior pair exits through a kidney. At least one kidney has to stay out of the high dose. Liver, cord, and small bowel are the other limits. A proximal field can also see the heart.

**Soft-tissue sarcoma.** Extremity preoperative dose is 50 Gy in 25 fractions. Postoperative dose is higher: about 60 Gy after a negative margin and about 66 Gy after a positive margin. The technique is 6 MV IMRT or conformal photons along the limb. An untreated longitudinal strip of soft tissue stays outside the high dose so lymph can still drain. Treating the full circumference is what produces severe lymphedema. Weight-bearing bone and the adjacent joint limit the plan. Retroperitoneal sarcoma is often preoperative, about 50 Gy, and there the limits are bowel and kidney.

**Skin, local electrons.** A small skin cancer is not total-skin electron irradiation. The beam is en face electrons. Energy is chosen so the 90% depth covers the deep edge and then stops. Six MeV or 9 MeV fits a superficial lesion. A higher energy treats the tissue under the cancer. Bolus, often about 0.5–1 cm, brings dose to the skin surface. A lead cutout shapes the field. Recognition doses are about 50 Gy in 20 fractions and 60 Gy in 30 fractions. The organ at the deep edge is the limit. For an eyelid, that organ is the lens, and the internal eye shield is coated with wax so lead backscatter does not dose the lid. For a scalp lesion, the limit is the brain.

**Vulva.** Locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma is 6 MV IMRT with concurrent cisplatin. One plan covers the vulva, the inguinal-femoral nodes, and the pelvic nodes when the groins or the primary require them. Elective nodal dose is about 45–50.4 Gy. Gross primary or gross nodal disease is about 60–70 Gy. Deep inguinal nodes lie centimeters under the skin, so a 6 MeV groin field underdoses them. A perineal photon field matched to electron groins leaves a junction in the groin. IMRT removes that match. Superficial groin nodes still need buildup. Femoral heads, small bowel, rectum, bladder, and perineal skin limit the plan.

**Vagina.** External beam to the pelvis is about 45 Gy in 25 fractions. Brachytherapy then raises the vaginal primary above that external-beam dose. Upper vaginal disease drains to the pelvic nodes. Disease in the lower third also drains to the inguinal nodes, and those groins are in the volume. Rectum, bladder, and small bowel limit the plan. Femoral heads limit it when the groins are treated.

**Spine SBRT.** Stereotactic treatment of a vertebral metastasis is a few large fractions, with rigid immobilization and image guidance, using 6 MV IMRT or a dedicated stereotactic unit. A recognizable single-fraction tumor dose is 16–18 Gy. A multi-fraction course is used when cord geometry or prior radiation calls for it. The target is the involved vertebra. The cord has a planning-risk volume, and the ablative dose stays off that volume. The cord is serial. Its limit is the TG-101 spinal-cord cell for that number of fractions. A conventional cord maximum near 45 Gy does not transfer to a single fraction. Prior cord dose is reconciled with that TG-101 cell before the schedule is accepted. In the thorax the esophagus can be the other serial limit. In the lumbar spine the cauda equina and the kidneys matter.

**Pituitary.** A nonfunctioning adenoma, and any adenoma sitting against the optic chiasm, is fractionated 6 MV photons to 45–50.4 Gy at 1.8 Gy per fraction. Functioning adenomas are often taken toward 50.4–54 Gy when the chiasm allows. Single-fraction radiosurgery is the alternative only when a gap separates the target from the chiasm. The chiasm is the organ that makes that choice.

**Meningioma.** Fractionated grade 1 disease is 54 Gy in 30 fractions of 1.8 Gy. High-risk disease, including residual higher-grade meningioma, is carried to about 60 Gy. A small grade 1 tumor away from the optic pathway can be a single fraction of about 12–16 Gy. The technique is 6 MV IMRT, arcs, or radiosurgery. At the skull base the optic nerves, the chiasm, or the brainstem limit the plan. That is why a cavernous-sinus meningioma is fractionated.

**Arteriovenous malformation.** Treatment is single-fraction radiosurgery to the nidus. Feeding arteries and draining veins are not the target. A small nidus is often treated with a margin dose from the high teens into the mid-20s of Gy. A larger nidus gets a lower margin dose. The quantity that limits the plan is the volume of brain receiving 12 Gy. Obliteration is judged about two to three years later.

**Liver SBRT.** A limited number of liver lesions can be treated in 3 to 5 fractions, with motion management and image guidance, at 6 MV. A recognizable schedule, when the liver and the nearby bowel allow it, is 50 Gy in 5 fractions. The liver is parallel. For a Child-Pugh A liver on this fractionation, the mean dose to liver minus target is kept below about 15 Gy, and about 700 cm³ of liver is kept at or below 15 Gy. The conventional whole-liver mean, near 30 Gy, belongs to standard fractionation and does not transfer here. Child-Pugh B function tightens the liver budget. The class A prescription is not copied onto class B. Stomach or duodenum against the lesion can force the tumor dose down before the liver mean is reached.

**Wilms tumor.** Favorable histology treated with flank radiotherapy receives 10.8 Gy in 6 fractions of 1.8 Gy, anterior-posterior 6 MV photons. The flank field crosses the midline far enough to cover the full width of the vertebral bodies, so one side of the growth plate is not treated alone. The contralateral kidney limits that field. Diffuse peritoneal spill changes the volume to the whole abdomen: 10.5 Gy in 7 fractions of 1.5 Gy. Both kidneys and the liver then limit the plan. Bulky gross residual in favorable histology is boosted to a total of 21.6 Gy. Diffuse anaplasia is a higher-dose histology decision and is not assigned the favorable flank number. Age under one year is a separate dose reduction. That infant dose is not 10.8 Gy copied forward.

**Neuroblastoma.** High-risk disease, after induction chemotherapy and resection, receives 21.6 Gy in 12 fractions of 1.8 Gy to the primary site. The volume is the post-chemotherapy, pre-surgery extent. Gross residual disease is boosted to 36 Gy. An adrenal primary is limited by the kidney and the liver. This 21.6 Gy dose is the high-risk dose. It is not the Wilms flank dose.

**Traps**

- 40 Gy in 15 assigned to the wrong site.
- 25 Gy in 5 called lung SBRT. Peripheral lung SBRT is higher dose per course in 3 to 5 fractions, not 25 Gy.
- Prostate 60 Gy in 20 scored with α/β = 10 and called inadequate, when an α/β near 1.5 puts the EQD2 in the high 70s.
- Cervix judged on the 45 Gy external beam alone.
- One SBRT schedule used for both peripheral and central lung.
- A DVH accepted as proof the rectal contour, the lung grid, or the brain-stem PRV was right.
- Twenty to 30 Gy involved-site lymphoma read as bone palliation, or a mantle called involved-site.
- 23.4 Gy craniospinal irradiation read as whole-brain palliation, or the 54 Gy boost drawn down the whole cord.
- 60 Gy in 30 used for limited-stage small cell, or 45 Gy twice daily used for non-small cell.
- Prophylactic cranial irradiation of 25 Gy in 10 read as 30 Gy in 10, or given a spine field.
- Anal canal treated to about 54 Gy without inguinal nodes, or with the short-course rectal schedule, or without mitomycin and 5-FU.
- Preoperative sarcoma left at 50 Gy after a positive margin, or 66 Gy given before the operation.
- A superficial skin cancer treated with exiting megavoltage photons, or with a high electron energy and no bolus.
- Lower-third vagina planned with pelvic nodes and no inguinal nodes.
- A spine stereotactic cord scored with a conventional maximum near 45 Gy, or with a cord dose substituted for the TG-101 cell.
- A prostate fossa given an intact high-70s prescription, or given 60 Gy in 20.
- Liver stereotactic radiotherapy scored by a cord maximum, or by the conventional whole-liver mean near 30 Gy.
- Wilms flank dose of 10.8 Gy used for high-risk neuroblastoma, or 21.6 Gy used as the favorable-histology flank dose.
- An infant Wilms dose produced by copying the older child's 10.8 Gy.

**Sources**

- Site protocols cited above (RTOG lung SBRT, ABS and EMBRACE for cervix). Confirm goals and constraints in the named document.
- QUANTEC for conventional organ tolerances and AAPM TG-101 for stereotactic organ tolerances. Confirm every number in the report.
- ICRU 50, 62, and 83 for the volumes those doses are prescribed to
- Regimens added in this overview: HD10 and HD11 for early Hodgkin dose, RTOG 0617 for 60 Gy lung, INT-0096 and CONVERT for limited-stage small cell, BC2001 for bladder preservation, Macdonald INT-0116 for postoperative stomach, NCIC SR2 for preoperative extremity sarcoma, RADICALS-RT for the prostate fossa, RTOG 0631 for single-fraction spine SBRT, TG-101 for the stereotactic cord cell, and the COG Wilms and high-risk neuroblastoma dose tables

## Staging and Spread Primer

**Objectives:** III.2

The exam writes stages in TNM shorthand and expects you to read it without a staging manual. T describes the primary tumor: TX means it cannot be assessed, T0 means no primary is found, Tis is carcinoma in situ that has not crossed the basement membrane, and T1 through T4 describe growing size or deeper invasion into neighboring structures. N describes regional lymph nodes: NX cannot be assessed, N0 is no regional nodal disease, and N1 through N3 describe increasing nodal burden. The nodal definitions are site-specific. For the breast N1 counts movable axillary nodes, for the head and neck N3 is a node larger than 6 cm or one with gross extranodal extension, and for the colon N1 and N2 count the number of positive nodes. A generic rule such as "N3 means contralateral" is not safe across sites. M describes distant metastasis: M0 is none and M1 is present. MX was dropped from the AJCC system, so a stem that offers MX as the correct designation for an unstaged patient is using an old convention.

Prefixes tell you when the stage was assigned. A lowercase c is the clinical stage from examination and imaging before treatment. A lowercase p is the pathological stage from the resected specimen. A lowercase y marks a stage assigned after neoadjuvant therapy, so ypT0 N0 means no tumor remained after chemotherapy or chemoradiation and surgery, a pathological complete response. A lowercase r marks a recurrence restaged after a disease-free interval. Neoadjuvant therapy is given before the definitive operation; adjuvant therapy is given after it.

The stage group combines T, N, and M, and sometimes other factors, into stages I through IV. Stage I is small, localized disease. Stages II and III grow in size and in nodal involvement. Stage IV in most sites means distant metastasis, although several sites also assign stage IV to very advanced local or nodal disease without metastasis (head and neck stage IVA and IVB, for example). Some sites fold prognostic factors into the group: grade for sarcoma, PSA and Gleason grade group for prostate, p16 status for oropharynx, and age for differentiated thyroid cancer. Gynecologic cancers are staged by the FIGO system, lymphomas by the Ann Arbor system (modified as Lugano), and primary brain tumors by WHO grade rather than TNM, because they almost never spread to nodes or distant organs.

Metastatic routes follow blood and lymph. Colorectal cancer drains through the portal vein, so the liver is the first distant site; a low rectal tumor can also reach the lung through systemic veins. Prostate cancer spreads to bone, classically as osteoblastic lesions in the pelvis and spine, by way of the vertebral venous plexus. Lung cancer spreads to brain, adrenal glands, bone, and liver, and the brain is a common first failure, which is why limited-stage small cell patients are offered prophylactic cranial irradiation. Sarcomas spread hematogenously to the lung and rarely to nodes. Breast cancer spreads to bone, lung, liver, and brain, and its first nodal station is the axilla. Melanoma spreads by blood to almost any organ, including brain, small bowel, and distant skin. Follicular thyroid cancer spreads by blood to lung and bone, while papillary thyroid cancer prefers cervical nodes. Testicular germ cell tumors spread by lymph along the gonadal vessels to the para-aortic nodes at the renal hilum, not to the groin, unless the scrotum has been violated. Ewing sarcoma goes to lung, bone, and marrow. Renal cell carcinoma goes to lung. The leading cancer in incidence for the whole population is skin cancer, with basal cell carcinoma the single most common type; among invasive cancers excluding skin, breast in women and prostate in men lead, lung is second, and colorectal is third, while lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death.

## Head and Neck Subsites and Prognosis

**Objectives:** III.2

Ohngren's line is an imaginary line drawn from the medial canthus of the eye to the angle of the mandible. It divides the maxillary sinus into an anteroinferior part, the infrastructure, and a posterosuperior part, the suprastructure. Tumors below the line present earlier through the palate and the gingiva and are more resectable. Tumors above the line grow toward the orbit, the pterygoid plates, and the skull base, and carry a worse prognosis. The line is a prognostic and surgical landmark, not a field border.

The retromolar trigone is the small triangle of mucosa behind the last mandibular molar, covering the anterior edge of the mandibular ramus, with its apex at the maxillary tuberosity. It is an oral cavity subsite. Because the mucosa lies directly on bone, tumors there invade the mandible early, and they spread to the tonsillar pillar and the buccal mucosa. Lymphatic drainage goes to levels I and II. The floor of mouth is the U-shaped mucosa between the tongue and the lower gingiva, over the mylohyoid muscle, containing the openings of the submandibular ducts. Floor of mouth and oral tongue tumors drain to levels I and II and then III, and a tumor near the midline drains to both sides. Both subsites sit against the mandible, so a dental evaluation precedes radiotherapy and the mandible is an organ at risk.

Human papillomavirus, detected in practice by p16 immunohistochemistry, defines a distinct oropharyngeal cancer. Patients are often younger, lighter smokers, with a small tonsil or tongue-base primary and large cystic nodes. The prognosis is substantially better than for HPV-negative disease at the same anatomic extent. The eighth edition of AJCC gives p16-positive oropharynx its own staging, so a p16-positive patient with multiple ipsilateral nodes is a lower stage than an HPV-negative patient with the same nodes. The standard definitive dose remains about 70 Gy with concurrent cisplatin; dose de-escalation for HPV-positive disease is a trial question, not a standard.

Parotid cancer is treated with surgery first. A parotidectomy aims to preserve the facial nerve, which runs through the gland and divides it into superficial and deep lobes. Postoperative radiotherapy is added for high-grade histology, close or positive margins, perineural invasion, facial nerve involvement, tumor extending outside the gland, nodal disease, or recurrent disease. The postoperative dose is about 60 Gy, higher where margins are positive. Radiotherapy alone is reserved for unresectable disease. The ipsilateral neck is treated for high-grade or node-positive tumors. The contralateral parotid is spared, and the cochlea and the temporal lobe sit at the deep edge of the field.

Trigeminal neuralgia is a benign pain syndrome treated with single-fraction radiosurgery when medication fails. The target is a few millimeters of the trigeminal nerve root near its entry into the pons, treated with a small collimator of about 4 mm. The prescribed maximum dose is high, about 70 to 90 Gy, far above any tumor radiosurgery dose, because the goal is to injure the nerve rather than to control a tumor. The brainstem surface a few millimeters away is kept to a small fraction of that maximum. The dose is not a 12 to 24 Gy metastasis dose and it is not fractionated.

## Lymphoma, Thoracic, and Upper GI Site Facts

**Objectives:** III.2

Hodgkin lymphoma is diagnosed by the Reed-Sternberg cell, a large binucleated cell with prominent nucleoli, in a background of reactive lymphocytes. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma has four subtypes: nodular sclerosis is the most common, typical of young adults with a mediastinal mass; mixed cellularity is next; lymphocyte-rich is uncommon; and lymphocyte-depleted is the rarest and carries the worst outlook. Nodular lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma is a separate entity with CD20-positive popcorn cells. The disease spreads contiguously from one nodal group to the next, usually starting in the neck or mediastinum. Staging is Ann Arbor: stage I is one nodal region, stage II is two or more regions on the same side of the diaphragm, stage III is both sides of the diaphragm, and stage IV is diffuse extranodal involvement. The suffix B marks the B symptoms: fever above 38 degrees Celsius, drenching night sweats, and unexplained loss of more than 10 percent of body weight in six months. Pruritus and fatigue are not B symptoms. The suffix E marks limited extranodal extension and S marks splenic involvement. A mediastinal mass wider than one third of the chest is bulky. Involved-site radiotherapy after chemotherapy has replaced the historical mantle and inverted-Y fields; the involved-site volume is the prechemotherapy nodal extent adjusted to the postchemotherapy anatomy, not a region.

Superior vena cava syndrome is obstruction of the superior vena cava by a mediastinal mass, most often right-sided lung cancer, particularly small cell carcinoma, and lymphoma. The patient has facial and arm swelling, distended neck and chest wall veins, plethora, headache, and dyspnea, worse when bending forward. It is an oncologic emergency when the airway or the brain is compromised. Radiotherapy is a classic urgent treatment and is still used when a diagnosis is known and the tumor is not chemosensitive; the first fractions are often larger, then the course settles into a standard schedule. In current practice a stable patient gets a tissue diagnosis first, because small cell carcinoma and lymphoma respond to chemotherapy, and an endovascular stent relieves the obstruction fastest. Spinal cord compression, malignant hypercalcemia, and tumor lysis are the other classic oncologic emergencies.

The esophagus is about 25 cm long. It begins at the cricopharyngeus at C6, about 15 cm from the incisors, and ends at the gastroesophageal junction at about T11, about 40 cm from the incisors. The cervical segment runs to the thoracic inlet, the upper thoracic segment to the azygos arch near the carina, the middle thoracic segment to the inferior pulmonary vein, and the lower thoracic segment to the gastroesophageal junction. Squamous cell carcinoma arises in the upper and middle thirds and is linked to tobacco and alcohol. Adenocarcinoma arises in the distal third and the gastroesophageal junction from Barrett's esophagus, the intestinal metaplasia caused by chronic reflux, and is now the dominant histology in the United States. The esophagus has a rich longitudinal submucosal lymphatic network, so disease skips along its length and the clinical target volume extends several centimeters above and below the gross tumor. A cervical esophageal tumor is treated with definitive chemoradiation to preserve the larynx. The heart, lungs, and spinal cord limit a thoracic esophageal plan.

The pancreas lies retroperitoneally at about L1 to L2. The head sits in the C-loop of the duodenum, and the uncinate process is the hook of the head that curls behind the superior mesenteric vein and artery. About three quarters of pancreatic cancers arise in the head and present with painless obstructive jaundice because they block the common bile duct. The operation for a resectable head tumor is the Whipple procedure, a pancreaticoduodenectomy that removes the pancreatic head, the duodenum, the gallbladder, the distal bile duct, and often the distal stomach. Body and tail tumors are treated with distal pancreatectomy, usually with splenectomy. Involvement of the superior mesenteric artery, through the uncinate process, is what turns a head tumor from resectable to borderline or unresectable. The duodenum is the dose-limiting organ when the head is irradiated.

Seminoma is the most radiosensitive solid tumor commonly seen in a clinic. The testis drains along the gonadal vessels to the para-aortic and paracaval nodes at the level of the renal hilum, not to the inguinal nodes, so the classic radiotherapy fields are abdominal. For stage I disease the modern options are surveillance, one cycle of carboplatin, or a para-aortic strip to 20 Gy in 10 fractions. For stage II disease with retroperitoneal nodes the field is the hockey-stick, also called the dog-leg: a para-aortic strip from the top of T11 down to the aortic bifurcation that then bends along the ipsilateral common and external iliac nodes to the top of the acetabulum. The para-aortic and iliac volume receives about 20 to 25 Gy and the gross nodes are boosted to a total of about 30 Gy for stage IIA or 36 Gy for stage IIB. The remaining testicle is shielded with a clamshell, and the kidneys are the organ to watch on the strip. The right testis drains preferentially to interaortocaval nodes and the left to left para-aortic nodes, and lymph crosses from right to left but rarely the other way.

## Sarcoma, Skin, and CNS Site Facts

**Objectives:** III.2

Ewing sarcoma is a small round blue cell tumor of children and adolescents that carries the t(11;22) translocation producing the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion. It favors the diaphysis of long bones such as the femur and the flat bones of the pelvis, and pelvic primaries have the worst outcome. It is radiosensitive and chemosensitive, and treatment is chemotherapy with local control by surgery, radiotherapy, or both; definitive radiotherapy is about 55.8 Gy and postoperative radiotherapy is given for positive margins or poor response. Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone malignancy in children and adolescents, peaking in the teenage growth spurt, with a second small peak in older adults with Paget's disease. It arises in the metaphysis around the knee, the distal femur and the proximal tibia, and produces osteoid, giving a sunburst periosteal reaction and a Codman triangle on imaging. It is relatively radioresistant, so treatment is chemotherapy and surgery, with radiotherapy reserved for unresectable tumors or positive margins. Multiple myeloma, a plasma cell malignancy, is the most common primary bone malignancy in adults; it causes punched-out lytic lesions, hypercalcemia, renal failure, anemia, and bone pain, and radiotherapy is palliative at 8 Gy once to 30 Gy in 10 fractions, while a solitary plasmacytoma is treated with curative intent at about 40 to 50 Gy. Chordoma of the clivus or sacrum and chondrosarcoma of the skull base are radioresistant and often treated with protons. Soft tissue sarcomas arise most often in the thigh, spread by blood to the lung, and rarely involve nodes; rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common soft tissue sarcoma of children.

Melanoma is recognized by the ABCDE features: asymmetry, irregular border, variegated color, diameter above 6 mm, and evolution over time. Breslow thickness is the most important prognostic factor in the primary. Surgery, wide local excision with sentinel node biopsy, is the primary treatment. Melanoma has a broad shoulder on its survival curve, so when radiotherapy is used it is often hypofractionated, for example 30 Gy in 5 fractions or 48 Gy in 20. Adjuvant radiotherapy is indicated for desmoplastic or neurotropic melanoma with close margins, for positive margins that cannot be re-excised, for mucosal melanoma, and after lymph node dissection when regional recurrence risk is high: extranodal extension, several involved nodes, a node larger than about 3 to 4 cm, or parotid node involvement. It reduces regional recurrence without improving survival. Melanoma also goes to the brain, where stereotactic radiosurgery is the usual approach because whole-brain response is poor. Merkel cell carcinoma is a different, neuroendocrine skin cancer that is very radiosensitive and often receives adjuvant radiotherapy to the primary bed and nodes.

Skin cancer is the most common cancer in the United States. Basal cell carcinoma is the most common type and almost never metastasizes, squamous cell carcinoma is second and can spread to nodes and along nerves, and melanoma is the least common of the three but causes most skin cancer deaths. Surgery is the standard first treatment for most skin cancers, including eyelid lesions; radiotherapy is chosen for poor surgical candidates, cosmetically sensitive sites where excision would be deforming, positive margins, or perineural invasion.

Ependymoma arises from the ependymal lining of the ventricles and the central canal. In children it most often arises in the fourth ventricle and in adults in the spinal cord, where the myxopapillary variant sits at the conus and the filum. Treatment is maximal safe resection followed by focal conformal radiotherapy to the tumor bed, about 54 to 59.4 Gy. Craniospinal irradiation is given only when dissemination has been shown on spinal MRI or by CSF cytology. That is the contrast with medulloblastoma, which always receives craniospinal irradiation because it seeds the whole axis. Among adult primary brain tumors glioblastoma is the most common malignant tumor and meningioma the most common tumor overall; brain metastases, most often from lung, breast, melanoma, renal, and colorectal primaries, outnumber all primary brain tumors. In children pilocytic astrocytoma is the most common brain tumor and medulloblastoma the most common malignant one.
